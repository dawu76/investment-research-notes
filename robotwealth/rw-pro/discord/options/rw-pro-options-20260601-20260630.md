## 4. The central hedging discussion: from community synthesis to Alex (S2) answering directly

This is the month's dominant thread, running June 9–13.

**The starting point:** MidKnight summarized what Alex from S2 had previously said about hedging — buy 6-month-plus options in something like SPY when vol is cheap, buying both calls and puts, in strips across the 5-20 delta range — but flagged uncertainty about how viable this is for small accounts specifically. veng1 pushed back that Alex's usual answer ("it's personal") isn't terribly useful. Rachit admitted a temptation to turn hedging into a full optimization problem, while worrying that's a rabbit hole with limited payoff.

**andyw's community-sourced synthesis** (his own words: "may be — quite probably is — complete and utter bollocks"), from pestering multiple S2 users including Alex directly:
- Spread deltas across the 5-25 range, but get some exposure on around 20 delta before going to 5 delta.
- Spread expiries across 3-12 months.
- The end result should "smile at you gently" in IB Risk Navigator — not look like an axe murderer's chart.
- SPY is usually sufficient unless you carry unusually large or specific exposure.
- Don't oversize individual short trades; spread across sectors (e.g., don't run 100 simultaneous semiconductor positions).

**71Ksander (Alex, S2's founder) then joined directly** and gave the most authoritative answers of the month:
- His core framing: hedging can't be prescriptive because account situations differ enormously — hedging a $250k book is nothing like hedging a $25k book, and hedging a professional trader's book is nothing like hedging a hobbyist's who can comfortably sit on unrealized losses. He explicitly separates himself from this: he's on the signal-generation side ("there is edge or there isn't"), not the risk-management-advice side.
- His concrete, repeatable suggestion for those wanting an automated approach: buy a strangle 6 months out every 2 weeks, with delta stepping from 5 up to 20 across those purchases (rather than a single fixed delta) — the logic being a small down-move pays off the 20-delta leg more often, while a serious down-move pays off the 5-delta leg disproportionately.
- On why hedging is secondary: it's not the revenue-generating side of the business — the job of a hedge is just to make sure the account doesn't blow up, and IBKR's Risk Navigator (free) is, in his view, the best tool available today for retail traders to manage that.
- On sizing for two example account types (prompted directly by veng1): a $25k account building up and a $250k income-focused account get essentially the same mechanical prescription — buy a 10-delta strangle 6 months out every two weeks — because, in his words, keeping it "simple and mechanical" matters more than precisely tailoring by account size at this level.
- On portfolio-level sizing (prompted by aditya): size to **vega exposure**, not fixed premium — open Risk Navigator, ask "if VIX jumps 10 points tomorrow, am I comfortable with that loss," and adjust the options chain until you are. He was candid about his own experience getting burned by a ~30% two-day silver move in January, framing risk tolerance as fundamentally a "variance game" that no risk framework fully protects against — comfort with variance and appropriately small position sizing, he argued, is more robust than any formal risk-management framework.
- On methodology generally: he's skeptical of retail traders reverse-engineering his approach from a simple GARCH-plus-VRP heuristic (Marco's guess: "recent vrp high + some garch for RV + macro vibes") — he confirmed that style of edge worked until roughly pre-COVID but requires meaningfully more sophistication now.
- On why he sometimes doesn't trade the single highest-probability signal: he spends most of his time in SPY specifically, because VRP there has been persistently elevated since "Liberation Day" (arguably even a "Trump risk premium"), so a high-enough (not necessarily highest) probability reading in SPY combined with that persistent structural richness is, in his words, worth "aping into" even by a systematic trader.

**jgold437 contributed an independently-developed hedging framework** worth treating on its own footing rather than folding into the Alex Q&A:
- His starting distinction: an edge has positive expectation and a defined reason; a hedge has negative expected value and exists purely to limit exposure — so *how much* to hedge and *how* to express it is an inherently personal decision, same as choosing whether to hedge at all.
- His actual risk-management philosophy is drawn from Dave Dredge's framing that avoiding deep drawdowns matters more than maximizing arithmetic return, and his primary risk-management lever is strategy sizing, not hedge overlays per se.
- Concrete rules he follows: never net short options in any single strategy (hedge options share the underlying but DTE/delta are chosen based on what's being sold — for VRP trades specifically, this usually means trading calendars); for strategies where the tail options are badly overpriced (e.g., UVXY), he dramatically overhedges via a correlated product like SPY — if the math says buy 8x puts as a hedge against short UVXY calls, he'll buy 16x, explicitly acknowledging that correlations (like prices) can break down; for long-stock momentum strategies (e.g., NDX 100-based), he holds 1-year puts at 1.5x strategy notional, rolled to a new 1-year tenor every 6 months.

**Sentiment:** A rare, high-value month where a genuine outside expert engaged directly and repeatedly with detailed, specific answers rather than generic advice — combined with a second independently-arrived-at framework (jgold437's) that largely converges on the same conclusion Alex reached from a different angle: hedging specifics are personal, but sizing discipline and staying within your own variance tolerance matter more than any formal framework.

**Pull quotes:**
- 71Ksander: *"hedging a 250k book is not the same as hedging a 25k book... I am on the signal generation side which is much less nuanced: there is edge or there isn't."*
- 71Ksander: *"how about buying a strangle 6 months out every 2 weeks and you go up from 5 delta to 20 delta? ... you will be very happy to see a little move down and have your 20 delta pay off more than week. And if you have a serious move down you will be very happy to see your 5 delta pay off."*
- 71Ksander: *"being comfortable with variance and small position to weather most of the storms, is much more robust in my opinion, than any 'risk management' framework."*
- jgold437: *"A hedge is completely different than an edge... If I am short some UVXY calls and the math tells me to be long 8x puts as a hedge, I'll buy 16x."*

## 8. Erik the Viking's covered-strangle approach: "just a wheel"

MidKnight asked Euan for his take on Erik the Viking's (Outlier Trading) covered-strangle strategy — selling puts on a name you want to hold, getting assigned or profiting if not, then running covered short strangles that lower cost basis on drawdowns via averaging down. MidKnight's own read going in: it looks structurally similar to trading delta-one and just averaging down with extra steps, so more a "deep pockets" approach than a real edge. **Euan's verdict, once MidKnight clarified the mechanics**, was equally blunt: it's just a wheel strategy (covered calls / secured puts), which makes money when options are richly priced — works in most underlyings, but isn't anything new or special.

**Sentiment:** A useful pattern-recognition moment — repackaged/rebranded strategies dressed up with new terminology get correctly identified as an existing, well-understood structure rather than taken at face value.

**Pull quotes:**
- MidKnight: *"It looks extremely similar to someone that is trading delta one, averaging down and recycling shares from the adverse adds. Which is not much of an edge imho, but instead a question of how deep ones pocket is."*
- Euan: *"it seems like it is just a wheel. which is just covered calls or secured puts. which makes money if the options are expensive. so it would work in most things, but it isn't anything 'new' or special"*

## Overall read

June's #options was dominated by one extended, high-quality thread: the lead-up to and aftermath of a small-account/hedging webinar that pulled in a genuine outside expert (71Ksander/Alex from SharpeTwo) willing to answer specific, detailed questions rather than deflect with "it's personal." The throughline across the hedging discussion, jgold437's independent framework, and Euan's brief but sharp interjections (tastytrade reassessment, VIX-carry clarification, "just a wheel" verdict on Erik the Viking) is a consistent message: hedging specifics are inherently personal and account-dependent, but the *discipline* generalizes — size to vega/variance exposure you can tolerate, keep mechanical rules simple enough to actually follow, and be honest about whether a structure has a real edge versus just being a repackaged wheel or averaging-down strategy with a new name. On the practical/logistics side, the month also produced two useful reference points: a four-way comparison of options-data vendors (ORATS, polygon.io, Databento, thetadata) for anyone building a backtesting pipeline, and a candid community assessment that RW's own documentation standard significantly outclasses a paid third-party service (S2) that many members are actively paying for and automating around.
