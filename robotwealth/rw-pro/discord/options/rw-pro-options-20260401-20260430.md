## 1. Getting started with options: a beginner's self-taught Greeks primer

Ben kicked off the month admitting he had "no fkn idea about options" despite wanting to understand earnings straddles, joking that Delta was a river mouth and Vega a Street Fighter boss to him. He then worked through his own understanding in two follow-up messages (credited partly to ChatGPT): implied volatility trading is called vega; theory says IV rises with uncertainty into earnings; you exit before earnings because IV certainty resolves; if price trends too much you're now trading direction as well as IV, so you hedge that directional exposure (delta) to keep pure vega exposure. He also sketched a rough semi-automated process: build an earnings calendar (e.g. via FMP), check average options volume at T-15, size the order via IB algo if volume is sufficient, exit at T-1, and manually monitor delta daily for hedging.

Book recommendations followed: Yan suggested Euan and Andrew's own *Retail Options Trading*. **Euan's response was notably self-deprecating but pointed to a different, more foundational resource**: while praising the RW book as "an astonishing work of unparalleled genius" (tongue-in-cheek), he actually recommended starting with *Option Trading* (Euan's other/earlier book, or a canonical text) — designed to take someone from basic definitions to professional-level trading, comparing it favorably to Natenberg's classic but "good" (implying more accessible).

**Sentiment:** A genuinely useful entry point for new members — Ben's self-taught summary is a good sanity-checkable mental model for anyone new to earnings vol trading, and Euan's book-ordering advice (foundational text before the RW-specific book) is a concrete recommendation worth following.

## 2. A lesson in systemic vs. idiosyncratic earnings moves (DAL)

**Sentiment:** A useful, low-frequency edge case flagged without overreacting — Euan explicitly pushed back against over-engineering a fix for a hindsight-obvious but hard-to-predict-in-advance risk.

## 7. The CAR volatility event: a real-time short-squeeze/bubble trade, traded and debated live

This was the month's dominant story, unfolding April 20–30 in real time across dozens of messages.

**The setup:** Yan flagged CAR up 382% in four weeks with 30-day IV at 191%, asking if it was "crazy enough" to sell puts. Euan called it "a decent bet." Ben noted one of his longer-term systematic strategies was already short the name (painfully, on the way up), while shorter-term systems had also been shorting into the rally.

**Members put on trades and compared notes in real time:**
- MidKnight sold a 20-delta, 11-DTE put for $31 (the 437.5 strike); Rachit tried a similar trade further out (380 strike) but didn't get filled.
- MidKnight reported the option was extremely spready (~$10 wide) with IBKR showing calculated IV around 240% at the time.
- Multiple trading halts occurred as the stock kept spiking — Rachit tracked them live ("2nd halt," "3rd halt... is there a betting market on number of halts XD") and noted market makers' quoting behavior was "kinda wild/interesting," with spreads especially wide in shorter tenors. He explicitly stepped to the sidelines rather than chase it further.
- IV kept climbing — Yan reported some contracts crossing 350% IV; Sam only half-jokingly asked whether it made sense to wait for IV 500-1000 before selling puts on "a crazed stock like $CAR."

**Euan's framing of why this is hard to systematize** is the most substantive analytical point in the thread: with events like this, there isn't enough historical precedent to derive real statistical numbers — it genuinely comes down to "this looks like a bubble so I'll sell puts," a judgment call rather than a backtested edge. Later, after the inevitable crash, **his post-mortem verdict was unambiguous**: CAR dropped faster than any bubble he'd seen in traditional finance, with some contracts' IV implying a 40% *daily* move — a move he judged clearly wasn't going to happen over the following two months, meaning anyone who bought those extreme-IV puts (rather than sold them) simply got lucky rather than being right.

**Other tactical approaches during the spike:**
- IrishJohnnie traded CAR like a runaway commodity (comparing it to crude oil, CL): selling front-month weekly puts while buying a further-dated back-month put (a specific expiry, late January) as protection.
- IrishJohnnie also flagged a Reddit skew study suggesting deep OTM round-number strikes behaved somewhat predictably in the chain.
- MidKnight's short-DTE put (sold for $31, 10 DTE) counterintuitively *gained* value to a $34 bid even as the stock rallied ~$100 that day — still with a ~$10-wide spread, illustrating how illiquid/dislocated the option chain was throughout the event.

**The crash, and the aftermath:** Euan didn't see the reversal coming ("well i didn't see that coming..."), and MidKnight noted that while a severe down day was expected, a >50% intraday change from the high wasn't. Post-crash, participants compared notes on how their specific structures fared: puts that lost the most value were the far-out, way-OTM ones; Sam relayed an acquaintance's account of paying $9 for 400-strike puts (stock around $750 at the time) and selling them for $185 — an extraordinary payoff that put Sam's own -6x premium loss in context. Rachit described a scanning technique — using an IV-normalization method Euan described in one of his books/webinars — to look for dislocations across the option chain and tenors as the event unfolded; he pulls the underlying data directly from IBKR. He was careful to frame this as identification, not automatic edge: *"a lot of times the spreads are crap or there is a legitimate reason for the dislocation"* — the scan just makes interesting structure easier to spot and compare across tenors, not a signal to blindly trade.

**Sentiment:** A rare, extended real-time case study of trading an extreme, genuinely unmodelable volatility event — valuable less for any specific trade recommendation and more for Euan's repeated framing (no historical precedent to lean on, judgment over backtested edge, and a clear-eyed post-mortem on why the extreme-IV puts were correctly priced rather than a buying opportunity) and Rachit's IV-dislocation scanning technique as a general-purpose tool.

## 8. Q1 earnings season: PEAD, straddle trades, and clarifying the earnings-calendar dataset

Running alongside the CAR event, the regular cadence of earnings-driven options trades continued:

- A rough week for short-vol earnings trades: Sam described being "killed" on a short strangle; MidKnight lost 6x premium on one trade and suggested such trades may suit traders who can actively manage them intraday; FullMetal37! (running these for about a year) reported a rough week too but noted (with an admittedly tiny 4-sample anecdote) that the first half of earnings season has seemed worse for him than the back half historically.
- **Euan's framing on why earnings-vol trading feels more volatile than it is**: because you have to actively engage with each trade, both good and bad outcomes feel amplified relative to their true statistical volatility — and it genuinely takes a few years of doing this to build real intuition for how the strategy performs over time.
- Specific structures discussed: MidKnight ran short strangles in unnamed names (61/74 DTE-0 for $2.02 credit; a PG strangle at 140/149 DTE-0 for $1.23 credit); Roel ran short straddles across INTC, PG, and SAP simultaneously, then pivoted INTC into a PEAD trade (a +40 delta / -10 delta call spread) after earnings; Yan got lucky on FCX with long straddles despite uncertainty over whether IB's earnings-calendar timing was accurate or the company moved the report time late.
- **Euan pushed back on a vix-level-based earnings-trade filter**: after Sam noted he'd have skipped the whole month's earnings trades if he'd filtered by "VIX above 20," Euan cautioned against being too absolute about that threshold — all vol regimes carry some earnings edge, high vol is simply better, and the interesting part (as usual, in his view) is that this runs against intuition.
- A dispersion-adjacent idea from FullMetal37! (shorting single-name vol into a big Mag7 earnings week, hedged with long S&P vol) got a direct correction from **Euan**: this isn't really a hedge, it's a separate trade dressed up as one, because those single-name options already carry very high VRP and correlation pricing — precisely because they're what professional dispersion traders are already choosing to sell. A useful reminder that "hedge" framing doesn't change the underlying risk/reward of a structurally rich options position.
- **robotkris gave a detailed, dataset-specific answer** to Geebach's question about why some tickers (e.g., WMT) show multiple earnings dates in the RW earnings dataset, and whether the last one is always correct: not always — multiple dates reflect the calendar being updated over time as estimated dates and estimates themselves change (and possibly some vendor-side duplication). The latest future date is usually the best current estimate for upcoming trades, but isn't guaranteed to be the true final date — when there's ambiguity, cross-check against another source (he recommended TradingView). He committed to writing clearer documentation on how to interpret and filter these rows, acknowledging the dataset (which blends future estimates, actuals, and past estimates) is "a bit confusing" as-is.

**Sentiment:** A realistic, unvarnished picture of a rough earnings season for short-vol traders, balanced by robotkris directly closing a data-interpretation gap that likely affects anyone building automated earnings-trade pipelines from the RW dataset.

**Pull quotes:**
- Euan: *"It is volatile. And because you have to actively engage it seems even more volatile than it is (in good and bad states). Probably takes a few years to really get an idea of how it's going."*
- Euan: *"I wouldn't be so absolute about the vix level. All vol levels have earnings edge but high vol is better. It is mainly interesting because (as always) it is against intuition"*
- Euan: *"That very much looks like a separate trade more than a hedge. Also, those options have a very high vrp and correlation price. Remember they are what the dispersion guys choose to sell"*
- robotkris: *"Multiple dates mean the earnings calendar for that ticker has been updated over time... For upcoming earnings trades, using the latest future date is usually the one you want - but when there's a duplicate future date for a ticker, it's worth checking against another source."*

## Overall read

April's #options was defined by two overlapping stories: a genuinely rare, extreme single-name volatility event (CAR) that the community traded and debated in real time with unusual honesty about its unmodelable nature, and the normal grind of Q1 earnings-season vol trading, which was a rough month for several members running short-premium strategies. Euan was the most consistently valuable voice across both — his repeated theme was resisting the urge to over-systematize: DAL's macro exposure was "shit happens" not a rule to encode, CAR's extreme IVs reflected genuine judgment-call territory rather than backtestable statistics, a VIX-level earnings filter was "too absolute," and a dispersion-style single-name-vs-index hedge was really just a separate rich-premium trade in disguise. robotkris's dataset clarification (multiple earnings dates per ticker, how to interpret them) is a concrete, actionable fix worth applying to any automated earnings pipeline built on the RW dataset. For anyone reviewing April's activity after the fact, the CAR thread (Section 7) is worth reading in full for the blow-by-blow of how an extreme vol event actually trades in practice — wide spreads, unstable Greeks, and multiple trading halts — rather than how it looks in a clean backtest.
