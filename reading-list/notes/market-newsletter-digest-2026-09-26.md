## 1. Rates: A Five-Percent Shelf, and Real Yields Still Doing the Work

* **The curve as one picture (Michael A. Gayed, The Lead-Lag Report, "The Bond Market Never Believed In Three And A Half Percent").** Treasury par yields on September 25: **3-month 4.24%, 1-year 4.50%, 2-year 4.81%, 10-year 5.17%, 30-year 5.49%**, with **2s10s at +36bp**. Everything from the 2-year to the 10-year sits in a **36bp band**. Gayed's reading: "the market treats roughly five percent as the long-run resting place for rates."
  * **The front end is pricing hikes, not cuts.** The 1-year at **4.50% sits 62bp above the fed funds midpoint of 3.875%** (range 3.75-4.00%), which Gayed reads as "roughly two additional hikes fully priced." The 1-year bottomed at **3.40% on February 10** and has risen more than a full point "without a single cut materializing." The 3-month bill did the same round trip: **3.62% trough on December 18, 2025, back to 4.24%**.
  * **Three years, three curves.** September 2023: 3-month **5.56%**, 10-year **4.44%** (deeply inverted). December 2025: 3-month **3.67%**, 10-year **4.18%**. Now: 3-month **4.24%**, 10-year **5.17%**. Gayed calls the current shape one "markets almost never show: flat at a high level, rising from a policy rate below it."
  * **The un-inversion happened the wrong way.** 2s10s has been positive since **September 2024**, but the steepening came from the long end rising (term premium, real yields, the 10-year from **4.18% at year-end to 5.17%**), not from cuts rescuing growth. "The curve normalized the wrong way, through the price of money going up rather than the economy coming down." Why it matters: this is why the 2023 inversion's recession playbook never fired, and why Gayed thinks "the steepening nobody celebrates is more threatening than the one everybody was braced for."
  * **His falsifiers:** a 1-year yield back below the policy midpoint (the market repricing cuts), or the 2-to-10-year band "breaking decisively below 4.5%." Absent those, "the cutting cycle is over in both directions."
* **Real yields: the running total.** The **10-year TIPS yield closed Friday at 2.83%, up from 1.93% at year-end 2025 and a 1.72% low on February 27** (Gayed, from Treasury's real yield curve). John Mauldin (Thoughts from the Frontline, "Reversing Financial Repression") has the TIPS curve at the September 24 close: **1-year 2.16% real / 2.38% breakeven; 5-year 2.53% / 2.45%; 10-year 2.81% / 2.27%; 30-year 3.17% / 2.23%**.
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-20]] had TSCS Research's **10-year TIPS at 2.68% with a 2.33% breakeven on September 16** (the highest real yield since November 2008). Since then the real yield has risen **13-15bp (to 2.81-2.83%)** while the 10-year breakeven *fell* **6bp (2.33% to 2.27%)**. This digest's arithmetic on the year-to-date move: the 10-year nominal is up **99bp (4.18% to 5.17%)** and the TIPS yield **90bp (1.93% to 2.83%)**, so roughly nine-tenths of the move is real yield. That matches TSCS's 96-of-104bp split in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-21]] and Simon Ree's TIP-vs-IEF test in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]]. Three different methods now give the same answer.
  * Why it matters: Gayed: "Every basis point of that rise is a higher guaranteed, inflation-adjusted hurdle placed under every asset in the portfolio: equities that must out-earn it plus a risk premium, credit that must clear it with a spread, gold which pays nothing and must simply be believed in harder." Mauldin draws the other conclusion from the same breakevens: with every tenor above 2.2%, "the market is telling us that they don't believe the Fed will have it down to below 2% even within five years."
* **The week's levels and speed (Lance Roberts, Real Investment Advice, via Adam Taggart's Thoughtful Money).** The 10-year went from **5.01% last Friday to 5.18% Thursday and touched 5.225% intraday Friday**, more than 20bp in a week; the **30-year hit 5.50%, the highest since 2004**; **MOVE rose from 80 on Tuesday to 104 on Thursday**. Mauldin describes Wednesday's move as "from below 5% to over 5.2%" before settling at 5.17%.
  * Continuity: matches [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] (10-year 5.18-5.23%, 30-year 5.47-5.5%, MOVE 104.6 vs. 78.6 on September 22). Roberts's 30-year at 5.50% confirms Bulltard's report of a cross above 5.5% on Friday; Gayed's par yield for the same day is 5.49%.
  * **Goldman's speed rule (via Roberts):** stocks tend to struggle once the 10-year rises **about 30bp in two weeks or 50bp in a month**. It is **up 28bp since September 9 and 37bp since August 21**, so the two-week test is 2bp from triggering.
* **Rates as an end state, not a cycle.** QTR's preview of a Gordon Johnson (GLJ Research) note: **forward rates are at a 16-year high**, signaling "5% Treasury yields may be the new normal," and "anyone waiting for Treasury yields to eventually collapse back toward the ultra-low levels of the 2010s could be waiting for something that isn't coming" (paywalled; only the premise is public). Gayed's version: "The era of free money ended, the era of cheap money ended, and the bond market is now telling you the era of merely normal money may end too."
  * **Mauldin on what savers can now get:** **4.9% on the 2-year**, and "only an extra 70 basis points for tying your money up for 20 years." Treasury-concentrated money funds pay **3.5-3.7%**, and **one- and two-year Treasuries pay roughly 1% more** through TreasuryDirect. His own firm, which parked deferred subscription revenue in short bills for 15 years, is now weighing moving "no more than 1-2 years" out the curve. His framing: financial repression "is ending," and "be careful what you wish for": savers wanted higher rates for 15 years; borrowers now pay them.
* **Supply and the fiscal backdrop.** Philoinvestor: **CBO projects a $2.1T deficit for 2026 after $1.8T in 2025**, total federal debt is **$40T**, and Treasury is buying back debt "to try and contain the situation." Adam Tooze (Chartbook) flags that the US "may be on track for twin deficits of 6 percent each," and Brad Setser notes US customs counted **about $349B of goods imports in August**. Bloomberg's Anna Wong (via Tooze): "The hunt for the culprit of the long term yields increase reminds me of a whodunnit storyline." Jesse Felder (The Felder Report) answers his own question of what is driving yields (war-driven inflation, a more hawkish Fed path, nominal GDP growth "rarely seen over the past thirty years," fiscal health) with "Yes."
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] had CBO's **$1.97T deficit for the first 11 months** and interest ($1.05T) above military spending. The $2.1T full-year figure is consistent with that run rate.
* **Mortgage transmission (Ryan McMaken, Mises Institute, via QTR):** the 10-year was **4.6% a month ago** and is now over 5.2%; the **30-year fixed mortgage rate surged above 7.4%** this week, with some observers suggesting **8% by year-end**. [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] had Bernstein's 7.49%, the highest since April 2024.
* Lekker Capital's Scouting the Tape #34 (paywalled) carries a podcast with DCP, a former Chicago-pit rates trader, on "the commotion and rising volatility across the bond market," plus a "Secular Inflation Series" piece on immigration and the labor force.

## 2. The Fed: A Committee With No Downside Growth Risk, and a Data-Heavy Week Ahead

* **What the September SEP says (Roberts).** The September 16 hike to **3.75-4.00% was unanimous**; Chair Kevin Warsh said it "will deliver a timelier return to our target." The **median dot is 4.1% for both 2026 and 2027**: one more hike this year and no cuts until 2028. The SEP has **core PCE at 3.4% this year**. On risks: **"Not one of the 18 participants sees growth weighted to the downside. Seventeen see inflation risks weighted to the upside."**
  * Roberts invokes Bob Farrell's Rule #9 ("When all the experts and forecasts agree, something else is going to happen") and says a coin flip has matched the committee's 12-month forecasting record since 2012. Why it matters: "A committee this confident about growth and this worried about inflation isn't positioned to deliver 'rate cuts' quickly if earnings disappoint." The 15-year reflex of the Fed cutting when earnings stumble "is gone."
  * Mauldin's structural point: "this particular Fed is actually behind the market, which is by design. Warsh is specifically not giving forward guidance, and wants to move the Federal Reserve policy to follow the markets, rather than trying to lead them." [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] had Noble and Dayan reading the same no-guidance stance as the 1999 Greenspan playbook.
* **The growth data behind the hawks.** **Flash composite PMI 58.4 in September from 56.0**, with output growing at the fastest pace in more than five years; **initial claims 197,000, near late-1960s levels** (Roberts). Mauldin cites the **Atlanta Fed GDPNow at 5% annualized for the third quarter**. Mauldin: "if you are in the housing business in one form or another, you could be forgiven for thinking that we are in a recession. But the general economy is doing quite well." Roberts: "the same strength that supports earnings is pushing the discount rate higher."
  * Data note: Mauldin writes "We are one week from the end of the fourth quarter" in the same sentence as the third-quarter GDPNow figure; given the September 26 date he means the third quarter.
* **The consumer side.** **University of Michigan final September sentiment 48.1, a four-month low; year-ahead inflation expectations 4.6%, up from 3.4% before the Iran conflict**, with gasoline near **$4.50** (Roberts). McMaken cites BLS data showing **real average hourly earnings negative year over year for five straight months**. Philoinvestor has **August CPI at 3.4%** year over year.
  * Continuity and a partial resolution: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] flagged a conflict between The Prime Report's preliminary 4.6% year-ahead expectation and Noble/Dayan's "above 7%." Roberts confirms **4.6% on the final read**, which supports the Prime figure; the 7% remains unverified.
  * Different measure: Philoinvestor's 3.4% is headline CPI; the prior digest's Tom Lee figure (core CPI 2.35%) is core. They are not in conflict.
* **Hike odds and positioning.** Roberts: markets price **roughly a 70% chance of a hike at the October 27-28 meeting, up from about 50% a week ago**. Barry Knapp (Ironsides Macroeconomics, "Special K," paywalled summary) goes further: "a meaningful risk of Fed hikes in both October and December, which would push policy materially above neutral and raise the odds of a volatility-driven equity correction." His argument is that the Fed is "betting the ranch on the K-shaped economy," and that "balance-sheet support that dulls rate transmission" leaves "small banks, businesses, housing, and other rate-sensitive areas carrying disproportionate pain."
  * Conflict: Roberts's "about 50% a week ago" does not match the series in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]] (54% on September 22, 71% on September 24). The weekly-change framing likely uses a different start date; both agree the level is now about 70%.
  * Knapp's portfolio moves: "outsized cash position," **cut bank exposure by 2.5% to add to technology**, and shift **2.5% of underweight fixed income from the long end into the belly of the curve**. That last move fits Gayed's shelf (§1): with 2-to-10-year yields compressed within 36bp, the belly pays nearly the long end's yield with less duration.
* **Next week is the test (Roberts's calendar).**
  * **Tuesday:** JOLTS. **Wednesday:** August PCE (consensus **core +0.2% m/m**), personal spending, final Q2 GDP (**2.1%**), ADP (consensus **38,000**), and quarter-end. **Thursday:** ISM Manufacturing (**54.6** expected), Accenture before the open (a read on AI consulting demand), Nike after the close. **Friday:** September payrolls, consensus **162,000, unemployment 4.1%, hourly earnings +0.3%**.
  * Fed speakers: **Richmond's Barkin Monday; Chicago's Goolsbee and St. Louis's Musalem Tuesday**. Roberts: listen for whether any of them push back on the October pricing.
  * Roberts's read: "A hot core PCE and a strong payroll print would all but lock in an October hike. With the 10-year already above 5%, that's the combination stocks are least prepared for." The asymmetric case is a soft payroll print: **"CTAs hold the largest duration shorts since April"**, so a weak number "could force them to cover, sparking a sharp bond rally."
  * **Quarter-end rebalancing:** the S&P 500 is **up 3.3% for the quarter** while long bonds sold off, "so pension rebalancing could lean toward selling stocks and buying bonds."
  * Continuity: Tom Lee's claim in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] that a **September 30 BLS methodology change would cut core PCE by 20-40bp** lands on the same Wednesday. If the print comes in at consensus with no visible methodology effect, Lee's dovish case weakens.

"Such is what a 5% 'risk-free' rate does to the rate-sensitive corners of the market."

His conclusion: "a lot of things that have to go right for another 15.9%, against a downside of 6% to 14% if only one or two of them go wrong." Roberts stays invested: "Markets rarely punish investors for missing the last 15% of a bull market. They punish investors who needed that 15% to be there."

* **Multifamily delinquencies (Ryan McMaken, Mises Institute, via QTR's Fringe Finance).** Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's August reports diverge but both are elevated:
  * **Freddie Mac: 0.64% seriously delinquent (60+ days) in August, up from 0.60% in July and 0.48% in August 2025**, rising consistently since February. It **exceeds August 2011's 0.35%** (the previous peak year) and is "the highest in well over 20 years," above the Great Recession peak.

  * Cross-reference: [[multifamily-credit-stress-and-rem-2026-09]], which found about half of the ~$2.2T of multifamily debt is Fannie/Freddie (mostly 7-10 year fixed) and placed the short-fuse risk in 2021-22 floating-rate bridge loans. ## 5. Oil and the War: The Blockade Is Working Well Enough That Escalation Is the Base Case

* **Prices.** **WTI fell to $91.96 Friday** as US and Iranian officials discussed reopening Hormuz, while **Brent "still hovered near $100"** (Roberts). Brent was **near $98 on Tuesday vs. $72** before the war started.
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] had November Brent at **$106.60** and December at **$100.22** on Thursday, and US crude near **$92** Friday morning. Friday's WTI close confirms the range held.
* **Why Sam Kovacs (Babylon Burns, "Why I Expect the Hormuz War to Escalate Again") thinks talks are "smoke and mirrors."** The US strategy since Trump's **July 13** Truth Social post has been "selective recovery": escort non-Iranian cargo while denying Tehran export revenue (Trump floated a **20% cargo charge** for protection, with no evidence anyone paid). On **August 24** Treasury extended pressure to Iran's overseas financial links in shipping, aviation, gold, technology and digital assets: "Getting a tanker past the blockade does little for Tehran if the buyer, insurer or bank cannot complete the transaction."
  * **The flows he cites (with his own caveat that conflict data is unreliable):** no fresh Iranian crude has reached China through Hormuz **since July 14**; Iranian crude and condensate loadings were **220,000-255,000 barrels a day in August vs. about 2 million in March**; about **6.5 million barrels a day** are estimated through Hormuz overall, mostly via ship-to-ship transfers. Kovacs doubts the high estimates but accepts that some oil is moving.
  * **The escalation loop.** "Every successful voyage improves Washington's leverage and weakens Iran's." Iran's leverage is making shipping unsafe ("a perfect closure is not necessary"); security chief Mohsen Rezaei has conveyed **seven conditions** (effectively the June MoU) and threatened ships on "unauthorized" routes; speaker Qalibaf argues for military pressure before negotiating. ## 6. Japan: ¥150 Trillion of Reserves Gone, and Where the Floor Might Be (TSCS Research)

TSCS Research's "What the F*ck Is Happening to Japan's Money?" rewrites original research by Alexander Kim (@AlexanderKim120, 110+ sources). It is the most detailed piece on BOJ balance-sheet plumbing this digest series has carried.

* **The drain so far.** BOJ current account balances peaked at **¥570 trillion at the end of April 2024** (from **¥58 trillion in March 2013**). They were about **¥460 trillion at the end of March 2026** and **¥424.7 trillion on August 20, 2026**: **¥35 trillion out in five months**, more than **¥150 trillion** from the peak. In fiscal 2025 balances fell **¥70.7 trillion**: **¥40.7 trillion from JGB redemptions exceeding purchases** and **¥26.0 trillion from banks repaying the BOJ's lending support program**. Yet the call rate still trades **about 2-2.5bp under IOR**. "At these reserve levels, the decline hasn't put a basis point of pressure on the call rate."
  * **The policy path:** six hikes since March 2024, the latest on **September 18, 2026, a 7-2 vote taking the rate to 1.25% from September 24** (basic loan rate **1.50%**), with the statement pointing to more. JGB purchases fall to **about ¥2 trillion a month from April 2027**; holdings drop from **about ¥480 trillion (March 2027) to ¥350-370 trillion by March 2030**, as bonds mature at **¥50-70 trillion a year**.
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-22]] recorded the BOJ's move to 1.25% (highest since 1995) and the yen weakening afterward; [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] had the yen at 157.95 after Japan's finance minister said Trump raised yen weakness. TSCS's piece explains what the balance sheet is doing underneath those headlines.
* **How a maturing JGB drains reserves.** When a BOJ-held JGB matures, the government repays it from its own BOJ deposits; bank reserves fall only when the government sells replacement bonds to private investors and their cash moves into the government's account. So reserves fall by the gap between maturities and BOJ purchases. "The BOJ calls it a 'reduction in JGB purchases', because the BOJ would rather eat glass than use a word with 'tightening' in it."
* **Nobody has said how much banks need.** **Required reserves are ¥13.5 trillion, 3.1% of balances** (July 2026); for every required yen, about **28** more sit on top. Reserve ratios have not changed since **October 1991** (top bracket **1.2-1.3%**). The BOJ has never published a target level; board member Naoki Tamura and a June 2026 board member have said it needs exploring, and Deputy Governor Uchida called the discussion "premature." TSCS: "Everyone finds their number out the hard way": the Fed in **September 2019** (repo above **5%** in a day with reserves at about **$1.34T**), and Japan in **2006**, when repo cracked about a month before the call rate.
  * **The Fed's second attempt is the model:** it stopped runoff from **December 1, 2025** with reserves at about **$2.8T** (about **9% of GDP vs. 6-7%** in 2019), before rates lurched. Japan's balances are **about 62% of nominal GDP**, the largest relative to bank credit in a 12-country BIS comparison.
  * **Five sources of demand:** required reserves; settlement (BOJ-NET moves **¥233 trillion a day**; peak intraday overdrafts rose from **¥14.4 trillion in 2025 to ¥17.7 trillion in May 2026**); regulatory (LCR/NSFR, though most regional banks are on the domestic standard); precautionary (the counterparty credit-line index is still about **89**, vs. 100 in July 2008); and deposit outflows. The BOJ's own stress test assumes **5-10% outflows on retail demand deposits and 100% on deposits from other financial institutions**: "The market is the thing can do the leaving."
* **Where it bites first: regional banks.** In July 2026 regional banks held **¥49.1 trillion**, an excess of about **15x** required reserves vs. **25x for city banks and 28x system-wide**. Balances by type: **city banks ¥152 trillion, foreign banks ¥65 trillion, regional ¥49 trillion, trust ¥33 trillion, second-tier regional ¥8 trillion**. Regional balances fell **8.1% from May to July** vs. **3.5%** for the total, though TSCS cautions that two months is "a shit sample" and May-July is seasonally weak (since July 2025 regionals fell **19.2%** vs. **19.6%** for the system).
  * **The lending support program is the mechanism.** Regional institutions held **74%** of the outstanding balance in December 2025. The majors repaid fast (**¥28.4 trillion to ¥13.8 trillion** in six months); regional institutions barely moved (**¥42.6 trillion to ¥39.7 trillion**). Most of what regionals owe is **four-year money at 0%**, now earning **1.25%** at the BOJ: TSCS estimates **¥16-21 trillion** still owed after June 2026, worth **¥0.20-0.24 trillion a year** of carry, **9-12% of first-tier regional banks' ¥2,241.2 billion of FY2025 core net business profit**. "No treasurer ever got fired for sitting on free money." That carry goes to zero by **March 2028**.
  * **Repayment schedule (upper limits):** **¥8.4 trillion in September and December 2026, ¥7.8 trillion in March 2027, ¥14.6 trillion through June-December 2027, ¥9.9 trillion in March 2028**.
  * **The BOJ's own runoff estimate (Box 5):** holding everything else fixed, **city and trust banks keep about 94% of their balances, other reserve-requirement institutions 91-92%, regional banks about 60%**. The tail: **a quarter of regional banks have lending support loans worth 50%+ of their current account, and about 4% owe more than their entire account** (15% among other reserve-requirement institutions excluding shinkin).
  * **Funding is getting harder at the margin.** Deposits fell year over year at **about 30% of regional banks and half of shinkin banks** (April 2026 FSR); at 61 regional banks deposits rose **2.1%** but loans **4.7%**, taking loan-to-deposit from **about 77% to 79%**. Deposit beta is about **40%**. Posted ordinary deposit rates went from **0.001% (January 2024) to 0.306% (August 2026)**; one-year time deposits pay **0.4% at regional banks vs. 0.755% at internet banks**. Household investment-trust holdings rose **25.7%** vs. **0.6%** for cash and deposits; retail JGB sales hit **about ¥1 trillion in August**, the most since 2008. **51 of 97 regional banks had unrealized securities losses** in March 2026, so they will pledge JGBs in repo rather than sell them.
  * **First bonds:** Yokohama, Shizuoka and Shiga Bank issued their first senior yen straight bonds (June-July 2026), **¥92 billion** combined against about **¥40 trillion** of lending support still outstanding. Shiga's five-year came at **2.398%**; its **¥20 billion** covers about **2.6%** of its roughly **¥780 billion** of BOJ borrowing. TSCS: "Next to that bathtub the bonds are a teaspoon."
* **The threshold estimate.** TSCS's projected path for total current account balances: **¥424 trillion (August 2026) → about ¥305 trillion (March 2028) → ¥262 trillion (March 2029) → ¥224 trillion (March 2030)**, with a range of **¥188-260 trillion** for March 2030. As a multiple of required reserves: **28.5x on September 2, 2026 → about 20x in March 2028 → about 14x by March 2030**.
  * **The benchmarks:** 2006, when rates rose with balances near **¥12 trillion** (about **2x** required); scaled to today that is about **¥60 trillion** total, which TSCS rejects as too low for today's plumbing. Shigenori Shiratsuka's econometric satiation point of **¥168.7 trillion** (2009-16 data) and his assumed **¥250 trillion** long-run ceiling (19x required). TSCS picks **¥250 trillion of total balances** as the level to watch hardest, reached **during FY2029** on its central path; on Shiratsuka's reserve-balance ruler the same 250 equals about **¥282 trillion** of totals, reached **around October 2028**. "If there's a 2029 date for this anywhere in your book, find out which ruler it came off."
  * **The climate facility could pull it forward.** About **¥11 trillion** of climate loans mature **February 1, 2027** and **¥14 trillion** on **July 21, 2027**; new loans now float (capped at **¥50 trillion** total, **¥10 trillion** per bank). If none roll, **up to about ¥25 trillion** more drains, pulling the 2028-29 dates forward by **six or seven months**.
* **What is not happening yet, and how to tell.** In August 2026 the **overnight GC repo rate was 0.93% vs. IOR of 1.00%**; after the 2024 and early-2025 hikes GC settled **11-14bp** under IOR, and since the December 2025 and June 2026 hikes about **7bp** under. That narrowing is mostly more JGB collateral coming loose (repayments free pledged bonds; collateral utilization fell to about **70%**), not cash scarcity, but TSCS cannot rule out the latter. **Three-month T-bills have yielded about 10bp over IOR since December 2025**, partly from priced hikes. On **September 1** TONA was **0.977%** but the highest single trade printed **1.03%**, above the then **1.0%** IOR: "The average tells you nothing about that bank."
  * **The two falsifiers:** if by the **March 2028** quarter-end overnight collateralized call and JSDA repo rates haven't gone more than a couple of basis points over IOR, "the regional squeeze never reached prices." If GC repo trades persistently above IOR **while total balances are still above ¥300 trillion**, reserve demand is higher than every estimate. Near-term checks: whether the call rate moves one-for-one with the September 24 IOR change, and the **Tokyo Money Market Survey due in October**.
  * TSCS's bet: "the regionals feel this first as a cost": thinner margins and a busier BOJ window through March 2028, with a real scramble for cash as the tail case, not the base case.
* **The global channel: foreign banks and swapped yen.** Foreign banks held **¥65.5 trillion** in July 2026 (**99.8% excess**), up about **¥18 trillion** from **¥47.5 trillion** in January 2024; their share of total balances rose **from 8.8% to 14.5%**. Most is yen obtained by swapping dollars and parked at the BOJ to earn IOR above the swap-implied yen cost, which the BIS calls a cross-currency basis trade, not a carry trade (it has no exchange-rate risk). Stripping them out, domestic banks reach **19x required reserves around September 2027** while totals are still about **¥346 trillion**.
  * **Why US rates matter to Japanese plumbing:** Japanese investors supply about **¥254 trillion** of yen through FX derivatives (BIS, assuming 80% hedging); life insurers' hedge ratios fell from **about 60% (2022) to 40% (2024)**. The MOF's worked example: a dollar investor swapping into yen could earn **4.00% hedged on Japanese T-bills vs. 3.70%** on 52-week US bills. TSCS's inference: the Fed's **September 16 hike** could widen the dollar premium again, raising foreign balances and making "the total look healthier while the domestic part thinned out underneath it."
  * **Overseas holders are already large:** **14%** of all JGBs but **55.6%** of short-term government securities, **47.2%** of cash-bond turnover and **72.7%** of futures turnover; nonresidents bought a record **¥13.3 trillion** of 10-year-plus JGBs in 2025. The BOJ still owns roughly **half** of JGBs ex-bills (**49%**, December 2025), and the IMF puts Japan's gross financing needs at **roughly half of GDP**.
  * **Carry, separately:** the BIS put the pre-August 2024 yen carry trade at a median **¥40 trillion (USD 250 billion)**. CFTC speculative net yen shorts hit about **180,000 contracts in July 2024** (most since 2007), flipped net long in five weeks; in 2026 they reached about **160,000** by late July and **about 120,000 were unwound in the first week of August**. BIS research (May 2026): when shorts are large, a surprise **25bp** BOJ tightening lifts the yen about **10%**; when they are not, the effect is insignificant. MOF intervention: **¥11.7 trillion** in April-May 2026 and about **¥15.4 trillion** from late July to late August, which lines up with a **-¥15.3 trillion** FX component in August's fiscal factors, so part of August's reserve drop was intervention, not runoff.
  * Continuity: [[yen-carry-trade-unwinding-2025]] framed the carry trade as a **$5-20 trillion** funding structure. TSCS's sources put the measurable speculative piece far smaller (about USD 250 billion at the 2024 peak) and separate it from hedged basis positions; the larger exposure, per the IMF, is Japanese institutions' unhedged foreign assets.
* Why it matters: Japan is the one large economy where reserves are being drained on a fixed schedule while the central bank is still hiking. The first stress, if it comes, shows up at regional-bank quarter-ends and in repo, not in the headline call rate. And the yen funding that foreign banks recycle into JGBs and repo depends partly on the US-Japan rate gap, which the Fed just widened.

## 9. Chips and China: Intel 18A Is Real but Not Ahead; Alibaba and Huawei Keep Closing

* **SemiAnalysis's Panther Lake teardown (STEEL lab, free).** Panther Lake is the first commercial chip with backside power delivery (**PowerVia**) and Intel's first gate-all-around transistors (**RibbonFET**, four stacked nanosheets vs. Samsung SF2's three), on Foveros-S packaging. "Intel's manufacturing arc has shifted from nebulous roadmaps to shipped silicon."
  * **The competitive verdict:** "Panther Lake's 18A compute logic and TSMC N3E GPU logic" have **similar logic density**; the 18A example is **18.6% denser than Intel 3**. But **"18A does not lead TSMC N3P, N2 or Samsung SF2 in peak density,"** the CPU cores are incremental, and **the high-end GPU tile still uses TSMC N3E** (12 Xe3 cores); the small GPU tile is on Intel 3 and both I/O tiles on TSMC N6.
  * **Where PowerVia helps:** moving power to the backside lets 18A use a **five-track** logic library (vs. **seven-track** for N3E and Intel 3) while giving M0 wiring **2.63x the cross-section per line (1.84x per routing pitch)** of N3E. The PDK lists a **32nm** M0 pitch; Panther Lake's high-performance libraries shipped at **36nm**. The cost: added capacitance, thermal resistance and process complexity.
  * **Area changes vs. Lunar Lake (TSMC N3B):** P-core area almost unchanged while **L2 grew from 2.5 MiB to 3 MiB (20% more L2 in the same area)**; shared P-core **L3 shrank 14.8%**; the LP E-core cluster is **5.0% smaller** (its **1 MiB** L2 region **-8.4%**, **1.5 MiB** region **-14.9%**); the **NPU is 36.9% smaller**, consolidating the same INT8 MAC count into half as many engines (scratchpads and SHAVE DSPs **12 to 6**) and adding FP8, while still meeting Microsoft's **40 TOPS** Copilot+ floor.
  * **TSMC still wins on the GPU tile:** an Xe core on the Intel 3 tile is **~69% larger** than on Lunar Lake and **~55% larger** than on the N3E tile. N3E SRAM macros reach **~23.7 Mbit/mm² vs. 18.3** on Intel 3 (**30%** denser), **~16.9 vs. ~10.4** including bank circuitry. Xe3's shared L1/SLM grew **33% (192 to 256 KiB) for 5% more area**.
  * **Packaging:** measured microbump spacing of about **25.24µm** at the compute-tile edge is tighter than Intel's nominal **36µm** Foveros-S pitch. Wildcat Lake (launched **April 16, 2026**) uses the same 18A without the passive base, a cheaper path for value products.
  * SemiAnalysis's summary of Intel's recovery: "The process roadmap is back, but Intel does not hold the same process-technology leadership position it held prior to 10 nm." Why it matters: the stock ran more than 20% in two weeks (§3), and [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] listed INTC among the few holdings that beat the Nasdaq this year; this is the first independent evidence that 18A is a working, roughly N3-class node, not a leading one.

## 12. Process Notes: Variance, Skin in the Game, and Who Holds the Risk

* **Poker is training, not a trading strategy (Memos From Larp Capital, Worst Contrarian).** The lesson that transfers: "you can't judge a decision by its outcome" (aces all-in preflop against jack-ten suited wins about **78%** of the time; losing doesn't make it a bad call; SIG has its interns play poker for this reason). What doesn't transfer: poker has an agreed correct way to play and a well-traveled path from beginner to competent; trading has no agreed method and "a million ways to skin the cat," so a good poker player trading alone "will probably blow up before he even realises what is happening."
* **Skin in the game (Niels Kaastrup-Larsen, Top Traders Unplugged, on Taleb).** Check whether advisors bear the consequences of their advice; prefer strategies with "scars" (drawdowns survived) over backtested perfection; "the frequency of being right is less important than the magnitude of success when one is right."
* **Who bears zero-net-supply risk (The Diff, via Read.Haus).** For insurance and derivatives, rich people hold the residual risk by default and earn the premium: "for the long tail of other risks, the balance sheet of some rich person is the backstop." Also from The Diff's Capital Gains: adjustments to standard accounting (adjusted EBITDA) tend to be optimistic and leave room for inaccurate reporting.
* **Compounding through people (Westaway on Home Depot).** **$1,000 at the 1981 IPO with dividends reinvested is about $17M today vs. about $170,000** in the S&P 500. The moat was not the warehouse format but tradespeople teaching customers, and associates owning stock: "A rival can copy a warehouse in a year. Copying that takes a generation."
* **Waiting as a skill (Gyulai).** "A trade placed to relieve that discomfort swings at nothing the market offered."
* Sources: Memos From Larp Capital, Niels Kaastrup-Larsen (Top Traders Unplugged), Byrne Hobart (The Diff), Kyle Westaway (Weekend Briefing), Mike Gyulai (Macro Ops).
