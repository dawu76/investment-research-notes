## 2. Idiosyncratic return autocorrelation traced to an unstable 3-day rolling beta

**Question raised:** Charles noticed idiosyncratic returns from his risk model showed **~10x higher autocorrelation than raw returns** and asked whether anyone else had seen this.

- `-k` initially reported the opposite (lower/more stable autocorrelation on his own model) and probed Charles's estimation approach — weighting scheme, ridge vs. OLS, two-stage cap-weighted procedure or not.
- Charles shared his setup (unweighted cross-sectional ridge per date, 9 factors, no cap-weighting) and a candidate explanation: signals built from close prices mechanically share information with contemporaneous idio returns (**~0.51 same-day correlation**), which then gets projected forward through the autocorrelation channel — a real problem for any IC-based signal evaluation, since non-return-based signals get unfairly penalized.
- The actual root cause emerged a few days later: Charles isolated it to **a single risk factor — the 3-day rolling beta**. With only 3 observations per estimate, the beta jumps around massively day to day (each new day replaces 1/3 of the data), which creates strong serial correlation in the residuals. The numbers were stark: idio autocorrelation went from **-0.03 (raw returns) to +0.17** just from neutralizing with the unstable beta_3, and **the effect disappeared entirely by lookback 7**.
- `-k` confirmed the mechanism and offered his own estimation approach for comparison: OLS, exponentially weighted with a **252-day center of mass** (noting 60 or even 20 could probably work), estimating 25 macro factor loadings via time-series regression rather than a 3-day window.
- On the open question of whether this residual persistence is tradeable, `-k`'s view was measured: *"my hunch is that i think it's important for risk, but not as important for signal construction"* — autocorrelation in the alpha factor you actually want to trade could be a real problem, but for risk-model purposes it mostly reflects instability in the beta estimate itself. Charles's backtest attempts to trade the persistence directly weren't profitable.

**Sentiment:** Genuine root-cause debugging, cleanly resolved — the diagnosis (lookback too short) was tested and confirmed rather than left as a hypothesis, and the fix (longer lookback / EWMA) was concrete and actionable.

**Pull quotes:**
- Charles: *"the idio autocorrelation goes from -0.03 (raw returns, no neutralisation) to +0.17 just from neutralising by beta_3 alone. by lookback 7 the effect is already gone."*
- `-k`: *"i use OLS, exponentially weighted with a 252-day center of mass. you could probably get away with 60 or even 20."*
- `-k`: *"is this persistence in the idio residuals not tradeable though? if it is, you might as well trade on it."* — Charles: *"i don't think I've been able to conclude this yet, but the backtest certainly aren't profitable."*

## 3. Basket sizing and the trade buffer: cash redistribution and a signal-filter-ordering bug

**Question raised:** MidKnight noticed his basket size changing day to day even without an obvious signal change, and wondered whether he should be adjusting existing position sizes rather than opening new ones — a thread that ran most of the month and culminated in a real implementation bug found in the last week.

- llIHeroic and ilikepizza314 both suggested letting zero-weight positions hold an "empty spot" (base size n/40 rather than shrinking to n/38, n/39) so the trade buffer — not basket resizing — absorbs small signal changes.
- **robotkris** gave the mechanical explanation directly: when a ticker's zscore drops (e.g., from 1 to 0.25), its weight goes to zero and the freed-up cash gets redistributed across the remaining active positions. He was explicit that this is *"definitely a feature, not a bug"* but flagged two real costs worth knowing: it causes rebalances even in tickers whose signal didn't change, and it can increase concentration risk. His own practice is to do nothing about it and let the trade buffer prevent over-trading; he noted the more durable fix — adding factors that behave better through the middle of their range — was already planned as upcoming lab work.
- The thread resurfaced sharply on **April 28** when George and MidKnight found their portfolios disagreeing (George: n=82 after the min-depth filter, but still landing on 45 positions post-cutoff, wondering what was different). MidKnight traced it to **the order of operations**: he'd been applying the `abs(mean_zscore) >= 0.5 && consistency >= 0.5` filter *before* the depth calculation (guaranteeing a fixed basket size, e.g. always 60 tickers), then over the weekend switched to filtering *after* depth calculation — which meant the realized basket could now be smaller than the target basket size, and he hadn't fully appreciated how many tickers that change would chop off until George's numbers didn't match.
- **robotkris** closed the loop by shipping an actual fix: a new `signal_threshold` parameter that zero-weights (rather than filters out) any ticker below the threshold — deliberately preserving the ticker in the output because *"knowing that a ticker has zero weight despite being in the universe and having a non-zero zscore is useful info if you need to reconcile things."* He acknowledged the tradeoff plainly: it increases both turnover and concentration risk, and if adopted, users should expect the same universe size but a variable count of non-zero weights each day.

**Sentiment:** Constructive engineering discussion that surfaced a genuine, previously-invisible implementation divergence between two members' code — resolved with a documented parameter rather than a silent guess.

**Pull quotes:**
- robotkris: *"that'll be happening because because a ticker's zscore drops from say 1 to 0.25, which causes its weight to go to zero, and resulting in unallocated cash. So all else being equal, that cash will get distributed into the rest of the active positions."*
- robotkris: *"I want to preserve it in the output - knowing that a ticker has zero weight despite being in the universe and having a non-zero zscore is useful info if you need to reconcile things."*
- MidKnight: *"no, I know why they are different. But I'm surprised it was chopping off so many tickers!"*

## 6. Operational playbook: hourly vs. daily data, and when to actually execute

**Question raised:** Ahead of a webinar on running the strategy operationally, MidKnight shared his full manual workflow and asked what others were doing — which then unpacked into a broader thread on the hourly-vs-daily data distinction and execution timing.

- MidKnight's process, shared in detail: pull positions from TWS via a custom Activity Flex statement (tedious to set up — needed the `BasketTag` field, ultimately solved via daily trade confirmations rather than a custom Flex), then run a separate reconciliation script that keeps a simple ledger (ticker + position, position can be negative, with a staleness check on the date). His own framing: *"it is a bit manual in bits but in my early days of running this system, I don't mind being in the loop."*
- akhan asked the more basic question underneath all this — what's the actual difference between the hourly and daily API endpoints? **ilikepizza314** gave the clean answer: daily data computes the z-score off the **last 20 days of daily closes**; hourly data uses the **last 19 days of closes plus the most recent hourly "close" price** as a stand-in for today's still-forming close — i.e., "you are using the most recent intraday price as an estimate of what the actual daily close price will be that day." **robotkris** added the decision rule: *"if you're not set up to trade intraday, you would use the daily endpoint and submit orders around the open the next day."*
- On timing, `-k` gave the strongest and most specific advice in the thread: *"try not to execute at the next open. you're giving up a good bit of overnight alpha. if you have to, put your trades on the open auction. that way you get to avoid the terrible spreads"* — and noted this is automatable even outside your own timezone. mm's own testing supported the same conclusion from the other direction: performance holds up reasonably executing at the next open, but is meaningfully worse than entering at the prior close, with spreads "varying pretty drastically around the open."

**Sentiment:** Practical, high-signal knowledge-sharing — MidKnight's reconciliation writeup and `-k`'s overnight-alpha warning are both directly reusable by anyone standing up their own execution pipeline.

## 7. Execution style: MOC vs. TWAP/VWAP, and the agreement-strength weighting formula

**Question raised:** Andre asked whether anyone had considered VWAP/TWAP execution between 3:30–4:00pm instead of Market-on-Close, after a few instances where MOC orders on larger trades didn't fully execute.

- **Euan** gave a direct, confident answer: he personally uses a version of TWAP, and *"Moc isn't in any way optimal. It minimizes tracking error but will generally be sub optimal in price terms."* mm confirmed he TWAPs over roughly the last 10 minutes into the close as well.
- mm added a nuance worth flagging for anyone considering the switch: he's effectively relying on his own brokerage filling orders **at mid or better most of the time near EOD** (tested across ~1000 assets), which is a broker/venue-specific advantage — *"if it weren't for some of the more illiquid pairs (e.g. some REITs) i'd probably just market into everything over a couple orders"* — i.e., the "just market in" simplification only works because of that specific fill behavior, not universally.
- NP raised the harder practical question this creates: how do you actually choose a limit price to balance fill probability against price optimality, especially without a deep understanding of how a given broker's TWAP algo works internally? That question was still open at month-end.
- A related but separate sub-thread: mm questioned the **agreement-strength weighting formula** — `tanh(sqrt(depth * consistency)) * exp(-0.5 * depth * max(0, 0.6 - consistency))` — pointing out it seemed to punish high-depth signals below a 0.6 consistency threshold, which felt backwards versus "higher depth = more trustworthy." **robotkris** gave the design rationale directly: the modifier is meant to punish disagreement *asymmetrically* — conserve weight when agreement is high, but drop weight steeply when it isn't, and *"since disagreement carries more weight when depth is higher, we want to punish that harder... below 0.6, pairs disagree, and when depth is high, that disagreement is even more telling so punish those tickers harder."* He also flagged that the function looks smoother than it behaves in practice, since consistency can only take a handful of discrete values for a given depth (e.g., only 1, 0.5, or 0 at depth 4) — so the exponential is really acting like "a small lookup table," not a continuous penalty.

**Sentiment:** Genuinely open execution-methodology debate, with real expert conviction (Euan) on one side balanced against a practical broker-specific counterexample (mm) — a good example of "it depends on your setup" being backed by actual data rather than used as a cop-out.

**Pull quotes:**

## 8. Event risk: M&A takeovers and a Claude-based manual scanning workflow

**Question raised:** NP asked how the group thinks about idiosyncratic event risk after one of his shorted tickers (SILA) jumped on takeover news, moving the day's P&L despite being a small position — was there a "smarter" way to act on this in advance?

MidKnight's answer became the de facto community practice: manually scan the day's basket tickers for M&A-related news before submitting orders, calling it "a manual task injection for me." Specifically, his workflow is to **paste the basket's ticker list into Claude** and ask directly: *"Are there any corporate actions that would affect these tickers today?"* — reviewing the summary and, most days, leaving the basket unchanged and submitting as-is. He was candid about the limits of this approach: *"I think it would be cool to have it more automated, but it would be a lot of work for me and I prefer putting that effort elsewhere at the moment."* Later in the month, Matt G reframed this as a reasonable structural answer rather than a stopgap: using an LLM with web search nudged toward useful news sources is a sensible way to catch unusual corporate actions *"in the absence of a good data source"* for pre-announced M&A specifically. NP separately noted the harder edge case both he and MidKnight flagged: there's not an obvious mitigation for a takeover announcement that had **no rumors priced in beforehand**.

**Sentiment:** Pragmatic acceptance rather than a solved problem — the group converged on "manual LLM screen as a stopgap" without claiming it fully addresses genuinely surprise announcements.

**Pull quote:** MidKnight: *"I ask Claude: Are there any corporate actions that would affect these tickers today? Then I paste the tickers in from my basket trader csv file. It presents me back a summary and I most of the time leave the basket file alone and submit them all."*
