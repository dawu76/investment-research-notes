## 2. Making sense of the data model (the recurring pain point)

This wasn't one thread — it was the dominant *type* of question all month, from JK's docs-mismatch question (Feb 9) through George's z-score-aggregation confusion (Feb 9) to gilch needing a worked sign-convention example (Feb 10) to Koen J's combo-rank confusion (Feb 17).

**Findings, mostly from robotkris directly answering these one at a time:**
- Confirmed a documentation/code mismatch existed (JK, Feb 9) — robotkris suspected "a claude booboo" in the docs, not the SQL.
- Z-score sign convention: the z-score attaches to the *first* ticker in the spread. Aggregating a ticker's mean z-score across multiple spreads requires flipping sign depending on which side of each spread that ticker sits on — a subtlety several people (George, gilch) got wrong on first pass.
- `num_appearances` mismatches people were computing themselves came from robotkris calculating it on the top 10% of spreads internally rather than the ~750 actually pushed to the API — he flagged this as something to fix/simplify.
- Combo-rank 1 is essentially never seen in API data (MidKnight, confirmed by robotkris) because liquidity and other filters are applied *after* the raw combo-ranking.
- robotkris's overall framing, repeated more than once: **there's no "correct" implementation** — the value is in understanding what the data tells you, not copying his exact pipeline.

**Sentiment:** Persistent low-grade confusion, patiently resolved — a sign the onboarding docs/API needed catching up to the pace of the rollout (robotkris explicitly acknowledged this: "Sorry it's not more front and centre yet").

**Pull quote:**
> robotkris: "You're landing on the key problem with the simple approach of just trading the top N mispriced tickers... namely, that every decision becomes arbitrary: What should N be? How long should you hold for? How do you break ties in zscore?"

## 3. The PAA/PAGP case study — a single pair that's almost too good, and what it teaches

**Question raised:** -k pointed out early (Feb 12) that if you just wanted to trade the single best pair, PAA/PAGP (Plains All American Pipeline vs. its GP holding company) prints reliably — ~5.5% annualized on 1x long/short, so worth some leverage.

**Findings:**
- PAA and PAGP are literally two share classes of the same underlying company (LP vs. GP), making it a *structural* pair rather than a "network agrees this is mispriced" pair — qualitatively different from the triangulation-based signal the rest of the strategy is built on.
- Tax/regulatory snags surfaced fast: PAA is a Master Limited Partnership (MLP), which means non-US taxpayers face a 10% withholding tax on gross sale proceeds (LucD), and K-1 tax forms are a genuine pain for retirement accounts (gilch). Separately, ppaolo flagged that PAA/PAGP fall under the EU's PTP (publicly traded partnership) rules, making the spread **infeasible for EU traders** to trade at all.
- robotkris's most interesting observation (Feb 12): PAA/PAGP consistently ranks top-10 by combined LSR+ED score, but when you flatten pair-level scores into per-ticker scores (as the triangulated approach does), this pair *disappears* — the tickers barely co-occur in other spreads, so they don't survive the "network agreement" cut. He didn't treat this as a flaw: some trades belong in a portfolio of structurally-linked pairs (like PAA/PAGP), separate from the portfolio of consensus-mispriced tickers the main API serves.
- This spawned a follow-on data-quality thread: gilch tried to build a K-1/MLP-partnership screen by scraping SEC filings and asking Copilot for an exhaustive list, but couldn't get confidence in completeness. MidKnight suggested Bloomberg/FactSet/CapIQ would have it; gilch floated FINRA's API as another option. No resolution by month end — robotkris said he'd consider it as a separate API endpoint if a data source turns up.

**Sentiment:** Enthusiastic about the pair itself, quickly tempered by the realization that "the best pair" isn't representative of the strategy, and comes with tax/regulatory landmines depending on jurisdiction.

**Pull quote:**
> robotkris: "It occurs to me that this pair is consistently in the top 10, but as soon as you flatten your spreads, it gets lost. The tickers hardly appear in other spreads, and therefore don't make the cut when we construct a portfolio. Does that make it a bad trade? No, of course not."

## 4. robotkris's Feb 13 research dump: why naive top-N trading is structurally arbitrary

This was the single biggest, most substantive thread of the month — a long solo research post from robotkris, triggered by Branko's simpler "just trade top/bottom N mispriced tickers" idea, working through why that approach — while probably profitable after costs — forces a series of unprincipled decisions.

**The build-up:** 4D and gilch briefly pushed a "PAA/PAGP has a Kelly criterion of 85" framing, which robotkris shut down cleanly — that's a single low-volatility structural pair, not representative of stat arb generally, and he warned against "blind data mining... trying random combinations of things without understanding the underlying dynamics."

**Then the actual research, delivered as a sequence of charts and short explanatory posts:**
- **Forward returns by signal decile:** the raw mean-z-score signal is roughly monotonic against forward returns across multiple holding horizons (1d, 2d, 3d...) — but not cleanly so. The middle deciles (3–6) are basically indistinguishable from each other, meaning naive top/bottom-N or proportional weighting spends a lot of capital in a "dead zone" that mostly just incurs costs.
- **Decay is real and fast:** signal predictiveness is "pretty much gone by day 5" and can even snap back (reverse) on days 2–3.
- **Quality stratification helps:** pairs with higher combined LSR+ED "quality" scores show a bigger spread between top and bottom signal deciles — i.e., quality genuinely improves signal-to-noise, not just universe selection.
- **"Triangulation depth"** (how many spreads a ticker appears in) has a log-normal distribution and needs careful binning, but more appearances does help (steeper slope = more appearances = stronger signal).
- **Turnover is brutal**: even with a 7-day hold on a top/bottom-5 rule, you cycle through a 50-ticker portfolio and "that's going to cost a fortune to implement systematically."
- **The core thesis, stated explicitly:** every fix to the discrete-trade framework (reduce N, extend hold period, accept overlapping positions) just introduces another arbitrary parameter. This is the argument for moving to continuous/proportional-weighting portfolio construction instead of discrete entry/exit trades.

-k contributed a genuinely nerdy tangent mid-thread on Kelly criterion generalizing to the multivariate/portfolio case (mean-over-variance), which robotkris and others picked up as a lens for why proportional weighting is theoretically well-motivated, not just a implementation convenience.

**Sentiment:** This was received as the most valuable single post of the month — MidKnight ("mind is blown"), hac ("I read them multiple times"), llIHeroic ("that was really useful for me at least") all reacted strongly. robotkris himself was self-deprecating about the process ("kind of stream of consciousness stuff... messy, iterative").

**Pull quotes:**
> robotkris: "All these problems point in the same direction: the discrete trade framework is forcing us to make arbitrary decisions... Let's actually trade it with some reasonable-sounding rules and see what we get."

> robotkris: "The main issue is that there's no principled way to pick parameters. It's not a 'bad' approach. It probably makes money after costs. But it's rather arbitrary."

> robotkris (closing summary): "I think it's a good idea to start simple and small while we figure this all out together... But acknowledge that the simple approaches force us into arbitrary decisions that are almost certainly sub-optimal — just keep this in mind."

## 5. Going live: position sizing and concentration risk (mid-month cluster, Feb 18)

**Question raised:** Michael TW 88 asked for a P&L pulse-check as he prepared to go live that night, sparking a broader conversation about position count and sizing across several members already trading it.

**Findings, broken out by contributor:**
- **-k** shared a live PnL chart (5 open pairs at a time, normalized to 1x leverage) and recommended ~$2,000 per open position given IBKR's minimum commission structure. He noted 10/20/50-position portfolios have similar *average* return but meaningfully higher Sharpe and much less autocorrelated (smoother) PnL than concentrated 5-pair trading — "so drawdowns are less painful." He personally prefers proportional weighting over trading extremes, but said up to 50 positions (per mm) is reasonable too.
- **mm** independently converged on capping around 80–100 positions total — beyond that "it's just messy and not much extra juice." Reported ~2 months of simulated results tracking closely to live PnL, and separately flagged that going beyond 20/20 mainly matters if any of your assets are thin at the top of book.
- Both -k and mm attributed a rough patch in late January PnL to identifiable, concentrated single-name events: the Donald/Greenland news cycle, CADE/ASB/HBAN merger noise, and SPGI/MCO earnings — -k drew the lesson plainly: **don't trade too few pairs or take too concentrated positions**, even though expected return is similar regardless of concentration because top-ranked pairs tend to carry more "juice."
- robotkris (Feb 18, later) confirmed this matched his own experience and previewed that he'd cover the proportional-vs-extremes tradeoff (lower total return/higher risk-adjusted return vs. the reverse) more formally in an upcoming webinar.

**Sentiment:** Practical, collaborative — multiple people comparing live numbers openly, converging on similar conclusions independently (mm and -k arrived at overlapping position-count recommendations without having compared notes first).

**Pull quotes:**

> -k: "the return for 10/20/50 on aggregate is similar on average with a slightly higher sharpe, but the pnl there is far less autocorrelated... so drawdowns are less painful."

## 6. Factor exposure, cost structure, and risk neutrality

**Question raised:** rusty_shackle4d asked robotkris directly whether factor exposures get neutralized during rebalancing (Feb 22).

**Findings:**
- -k ran the actual regression (on his own non-degenerate implementation, not the toy 5-pair version) against the statistical factor model from "gappy's book," estimated on the S&P 500: essentially no correlation to any standard factor, portfolio beta ≈ 0.01, and only the alpha t-stat was significant. Rolling factor exposures were small but not literally zero.
- His explanation for why this falls out naturally: the pair-selection process is already roughly industry-neutral by construction (spreads are filtered to same-industry pairs), so explicit factor-neutralization on top isn't really needed.
- Cost reality check: -k reported live commissions running ~2bps/day trading $1,000–2,000 per ticker — "not been that bad live."
- Separately, bdkoepke and Ben ran commission-only backtests in RealTest and found a "pretty heavy drag" on a full long/short-5 portfolio using standard per-share commission assumptions (no bid/ask spread modeling, no short fees) — a reminder that -k/mm's live cost numbers assume decent execution, and naive backtest cost assumptions can look worse.

**Sentiment:** Reassuring — the strategy behaves close to market/factor-neutral as designed, and live costs are manageable at reasonable position sizes, though backtested cost assumptions vary a lot depending on what you model.

**Pull quote:**
> -k: "you're already roughly industry neutral because of the way the pairs are selected, the portfolio beta is like 0.01, and none of these coefficients are... pretty high."

## 7. New signal research: IBS and volume imbalance

**Question raised (implicit):** Can secondary signals (beyond LSR/ED/z-score) meaningfully improve forward-return prediction?

**Findings:**
- **Ben** tested Internal Bar Strength (IBS — long a close near the low, short a close near the high) as an overlay signal, and found it stood out among 10+ signals he tried, with an "agreement/gated" variant (only trade when IBS and the primary signal align) trading less often but more heavily on agreement, and looking promising early. He shared a public Colab notebook with the extracted logic.
- **robotkris** responded enthusiastically: RW had used IBS to good effect on an old FX strategy before, and agreed it likely won't survive costs as a standalone signal but can meaningfully help *execution/timing*. He committed to incorporating it into that week's simulations.
- **mm** independently worked on a **volume-imbalance** signal: z-score the volume ratio between the two assets in a pair, take the absolute value and negate it (so it's always ≤0), then average across all pairs a ticker participates in. A value near 0 signals the pair's volume balance is "normal"; more negative implies more imbalance than usual. He framed this as a *confidence/sizing* signal rather than a directional one — it lets pairs with abnormal flow get down-weighted. robotkris noted a puzzling result: the volume-imbalance effect seemed to help *more* with larger portfolios, the opposite of what he expected.
- mm was appropriately self-skeptical about his own implementation the next day, flagging a possible bug, though he still stood behind the underlying spread-autocorrelation logic.

**Sentiment:** Genuinely exploratory and additive — both signals were received as promising complements, not replacements, for the core LSR/ED scoring.

**Pull quote:**
> robotkris: "IBS is a really neat idea @Ben! It's one of those things that won't survive costs on its own but can really help with execution. We used it on one of our old FX strategies to good effect."

## 8. Universe construction and data gaps

**Findings:**
- robotkris explained the stock universe is constrained by market cap, trailing volume, and average unadjusted price to ~2,000 stocks — throwing the *whole* market at the ranking would be "counterproductive," since illiquid/wide-spread names would dominate the top scores. He acknowledged there's "probably some alpha" in the excluded low-liquidity tail, but it needs to be modeled with more care (capital constraints, execution).
- Regional banks structurally dominate the pairs list (mm estimated ~35% of the top 2,000 pairs) — a pattern that's persisted "since at least July," per -k, who framed it positively: a large, densely interlinked cluster is exactly the kind of structure the triangulation approach is built to exploit, not an artifact to fix.
- min_depth filter sensitivity: George found min_depth ≥ 5 cut the tradeable universe from 386 to just 83 tickers — a surprisingly steep cliff. robotkris's own chart showed the range varies from ~70 to ~110 tickers depending on the depth threshold chosen.
- M&A/corporate-actions data remains a gap: robotkris is currently using "Yahoo Finance + some elbow grease," actively looking for a dedicated data source. Ben suggested FMP's M&A endpoint; Michael TW 88 flagged Alpaca's Corporate Actions API (historical back to April 2020, covers mergers/dividends/splits/spin-offs) as promising and said he'd test it. Michael TW 88 also shared he'd already shipped a practical stopgap: a file-driven ticker blacklist that strips a to-be-acquired ticker (and all its pairs) from the signal pipeline before computation.

## 9. robotkris's "version 1" simulation reveal (Feb 26–27)

The month's other major research drop: robotkris shared concrete backtested portfolio simulations for the first time, explicitly framed as "the simplest thing supported by the data," not a claim of correctness.

**Key assumptions and results:**
- Top 100 tickers by quality (depth ≥ 4), weighted by mean z-score plus consistency/depth metrics (some tickers get zero weight), $100k notional, IB's most expensive Pro-tier commission structure.
- Costs looked "probably a bit conservative," and trading same-day (calculate at 3pm, trade the close) is flattering versus trading the next close — decay dynamics of that gap are slated for the next webinar.
- Concentration risk was explicitly **not** addressed in this cut: max single position regularly exceeds 5% of the portfolio.
- Repeating the exercise on a top-50 universe showed materially higher concentration risk but similar overall quality; comparing top-100 vs. the next batch (tickers 51–100) showed the two return streams looked "a bit uncorrelated," which is why blending them (trading the full 100) produces a higher Sharpe than either half alone.
- **mm** and robotkris cross-checked implementations independently and converged: daily net exposure drifts within roughly ±$10–25k of dollar-neutral on a $100k book; both were using depth- and consistency-based construction; mm's version using a "top N (50–100) by quality, depth ≥ 4/5/6, drop below ~90th combo percentile, drop consistency < 0.5, weight by −zscore × √(appearances × consistency)" approach was landing at **~2.2 Sharpe after costs** in the 50–100 position range.
- robotkris introduced a consistency-based step function that week: zero out (rather than just discount) tickers with agreement/consistency below 0.5. This cut the effective portfolio from ~100 to ~65 tickers while preserving return and Sharpe — a meaningful cost/turnover win, since fewer, higher-conviction positions trade less.
- MidKnight's read: this aggressive culling gets you to the same return/Sharpe *more cheaply* — robotkris confirmed that's exactly the trade-off, phrased as "trade less" for the same outcome.

**Sentiment:** Very positive — andyw ("Epic. Just epic."), William ("that was an awesome seminar... this research project has been amazing to follow along with"), llIHeroic checking robustness to the depth cutoff and finding it stable from 5→4.

**Pull quotes:**
> robotkris: "Haven't dealt with concentration risk — see how our maximum position is regularly above 5%. You may or may not be OK with that... So lots of things to think about. But it's looking OK so far."

> mm: "50-100 positions seems to be the sweet spot, about ~2.2 sharpe after fees and everything."

> robotkris: "cutting the tickers from 100 to about 65 using the consistency approach is better from a cost perspective."

## Overall read

February was the "getting off the ground" month for #stat-arb: the API and first Lab data landed Feb 9–13, and the channel spent the first two weeks working through mechanical questions (broker choice, sign conventions, data model quirks) that are typical of any new quant data product's first users. The real substance came from two robotkris research pushes — the mid-month (Feb 13) teardown of why discrete top-N trading is fundamentally under-specified, and the end-of-month (Feb 26–27) delivery of an actual "version 1" simulated portfolio with concrete Sharpe numbers (~2.2 after costs, 50–100 positions). Community members going live in parallel (mm, -k, Michael TW 88) independently converged on similar position-count and sizing conclusions, which is a healthy sign the underlying signal is robust to implementation choices — though everyone agrees concentration risk (regularly >5% single-position weights) and turnover/cost management remain open, unresolved problems heading into March. The PAA/PAGP saga is a good cautionary tale for the group: the single best-looking pair on paper turned out to be structurally different from the strategy's core thesis and comes with real tax/regulatory friction depending on jurisdiction — a useful reminder not to over-fit to standout individual results.
