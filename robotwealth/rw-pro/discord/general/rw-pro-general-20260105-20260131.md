## 1. Dividend arbitrage seasonality study, and the BOIL short rebalancing discipline

Yan opened the month sharing a rudimentary R study following up on something Euan raised in a webinar: `avg_delta_pct`, the average gap between a stock's dividend amount and its overnight price move from prior close to ex-dividend open, expressed as a percentage of prior close. His finding — stocks drop by *less* than the dividend amount on average on the ex-div date, an effect he found more pronounced in European names. Rachit and Ben pushed on the methodology (full dividend history vs. last event only, special dividends, the three relevant dates — announcement, ex-dividend, payment), and Yan candidly flagged this isn't tradable as-is (can't reliably trade at the open price, and you need to own the stock before the prior day's close to settle in time to actually receive the dividend).

Running in parallel: a live discipline discussion around the BOIL short position, whose market value had dropped sharply that week (average price $33.27, last $18.80, per veng1). Sam explained his approach — trimming/adding to keep the *market value* of the short constant, rebalancing at 10% moves rather than letting the position drift. **robotkris weighed in decisively on the "why"**: without rebalancing, a position that moves against you grows disproportionately large (bad), and one that's gone your way shrinks well below the exposure you originally wanted — so rebalancing back to target isn't optional risk-taking, it's what keeps you at the exposure you actually decided on.

**Sentiment:** Constructive and instructive — genuine methodological rigor on the dividend study, and a clean risk-management principle reinforced by robotkris on the BOIL side.

**Pull quotes:**
- robotkris: *"I would definitely rebalance this thing... if it had gone against us, the position size would grow way bigger than you want. And now that it's gone down a lot, our position size is almost half what it was when we put it on. So rather than accept thos[e exposures, it makes sense to rebalance back to the exposure you wanted originally]."*
- Sam: *"The idea is to keep the market value of the BOIL short constant. Because it's gone down today, I've shorted more this afternoon... I do it at 10% changes"*

## 3. hazler's "very first professional trade" story: iceberg orders vs. a market-moving spoofer

The month's best narrative thread. hazler, killing time waiting on Claude Code, told the story of his first professional trade at a Chicago HFT shop in 2010: market-making CME Treasury futures with 1-lot size, he noticed a counterparty repeatedly adding quantity in a characteristic size (likely a click-trader with a fixed UI size) that was pushing the market — spoofing. His mentor's reaction on being shown it: "We're going to make a lot of money on this." The firm didn't spoof itself, but built a strategy to exploit the other party's spoofing: iceberg orders on the offer when the spoofer pushed the market up, iceberg on the bid when pushed down — keeping the book imbalanced in the firm's favor without adding the "questionable" size themselves.

- The trade **virtually never lost money**, making $30-50k on good days (mostly going to the firm). It ran for months until the spoofer disappeared — hazler suspects partly because his own icebergs had grown large enough to blunt the spoofer's ability to move the market.
- The coda: years later, hazler consulted for the CME on identifying spoofers, and as part of that work discovered who the original firm had actually been trading against — that firm was later the target of a CFTC enforcement action for similar behavior in S&P futures.
- MidKnight matched it with his own 2004 Eurex fixed-income flipping story, and zbanga (ex-3RedPartners, by hazler's guess) confirmed similar lead-lag exploitation of spoofing was common practice elsewhere, though effective despoofing algos mostly require manual work and a trader watching the book.

**Sentiment:** Genuine appreciation — a rare, detailed war story from someone with real market-structure/HFT background, well-received by the group.

**Pull quotes:**

## 11. Sizing down for volatility spikes: building a systematic trigger from "Trading Through Armageddon"

Rachit's answer was only half-joking ("probably everything will be going nuts... I just look at VIX + VVIX"), but the more substantive contributions came from Dan and **-k**:

- MidKnight suggested the VIX term structure itself (which he watches via vixcentral.com) likely contains extractable systematic signal about where risk sits across time horizons, even without a fully worked-out rule.
- **-k contributed the most concrete, implementable answer**: a simple systematic trigger formula, `(vix - vix.ewm(20).mean()) > 10` — flagging a vol spike when VIX is more than 10 points above its 20-day exponentially-weighted average. Matt G adopted this directly, clarifying he wouldn't apply it to risk-premium-harvesting strategies he's comfortable riding through drawdowns on, but would use it to proactively de-risk the rest of his book.

**Sentiment:** Genuinely productive — moved from a vague course-inspired question to a concrete, adopted systematic rule within the thread, with real strategy-specific nuance from Dan.

## 12. Prediction markets: where's the edge left, and a new tool (EventEdge)

stian asked where to even start looking for edge in prediction markets, assuming the easy stuff (arbing outcomes that don't sum to 1, cross-market arb) is long gone. The group converged on a fairly bearish-on-easy-edge, bullish-on-hard-edge view:

- FullMetal37! and Dasokay both pointed to the **favorite-longshot bias**: contracts priced at 80%+ are systematically underpriced (those outcomes occur more often than the price implies), though Dasokay's linked research used Kalshi data and both cautioned to stay away from financial/economic-outcome markets specifically, since those are already priced efficiently by sophisticated participants — summarized bluntly as "essentially just selling dreams to 100x punters."
- **macrosteaks gave by far the most detailed research roadmap**, flagging real (if latency-sensitive) pure arb still exists but requires skilled execution automation and careful handling of "landmine" contract-pairs that look identical but subtly differ. His own next steps: estimating short-term volatility to price 15-minute crypto up/down markets, and separately investigating whether order-book state on the most liquid exchanges is predictive — while explicitly worrying the sharpest bots in that space are already "printing" and may be too far ahead to compete with.
- **nzrod (POLY) shared a concrete tool**: EventEdge (lp.eventedge.io) — independent probability estimates for Polymarket with daily rationale, built and refined over the past couple months, offered with the standard no-guarantees caveat.

**Sentiment:** Realistic and research-minded — no illusions about easy edge remaining, but genuine appetite (macrosteaks) to go build something in the harder, still-open corners of the space.

**Pull quotes:**
