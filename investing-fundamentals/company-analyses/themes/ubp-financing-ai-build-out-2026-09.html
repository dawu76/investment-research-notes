The argument in four lines

01 · THE GAP
$820B vs $750B
The five hyperscalers (MSFT, AMZN, GOOGL, META, ORCL) now spend more than they generate: ~$820B capex vs ~$750B operating cash flow in 2026, and $1.0-1.3T vs ~$900B in 2027.
02 · NOT THE BALANCE SHEET
~$400B
The four AA-or-better names can add ~$400B of debt in 18 months without a downgrade, ~$600B before falling to single-A. The constraint is the price the bond market charges.
03 · THE REAL RISK
~40%
Of $2.3T in cloud backlog is owed by OpenAI and Anthropic, two private companies. The risk sits in the order book, not the borrowers.
04 · THE BEAR CASE HAS A DATE
2027-28
Signed leases and take-or-pay contracts start billing then, whatever lab revenue is by then, while capex growth slows: +111% (2026), +45% (2027), +11% (2028).

Spending has passed cash generation

For the first time, the five largest cloud companies spend more on data centers than their businesses bring in. The difference has to come from borrowing, partners or equity.

Most obligations are off the balance sheet

Reported debt understates what these companies have committed to. Signed leases that haven't started, chip purchase deals and guarantees don't show up as debt yet.

Who loses first in an overbuild

An overbuild "destroys the marginal builder and the operator that borrowed against a single contract," not an AA company with a second business.

This matches the wiki's own ai-compute-commencement-wall-and-refinancing-trap view: solvency risk for neoclouds and private credit, valuation-multiple risk for tier-1 hyperscalers.

Takeaways for investors

1. Losses land on the leveraged edge. See the ladder above. The fragile layer is single-contract structures, not AA balance sheets.
2. For shareholders the risk is margin, not default. Lab shortfalls will likely show up as "renegotiation dressed as partnership": deferrals called rephasing, and sub-leased capacity hitting a market sold as scarce.
3. Equity and credit are one trade. ~35% of 2026 funding came from equity, and the group holds $600B+ of stakes in OpenAI, Anthropic and SpaceX it could sell. If the equity de-rates, bonds must fund a bigger gap just as appetite is weakest. Six of these names are ~17% of MSCI ACWI, so most portfolios already own the equity that would need to be sold.
4. Oracle is the pivot credit. The one name where a lab renegotiation reaches the rating. A downgrade to high yield would trigger forced selling by holders with index caps.
5. OpenAI exposure is mostly indirect. It arrives through ORCL, CRWV and project paper for sites built for it. Anthropic's structures put an IG balance sheet between lender and lab (Alphabet guarantees Fluidstack leases; Broadcom backstop). OpenAI's do not.
6. Watch the ex-OpenAI number. Microsoft backlog +84% y/y, but +25% excluding OpenAI, near its historical rate. The cleanest read on organic demand.

7. What to watch (UBP's list)

  • The new-issue concession on the next jumbo deal (UBP weights this most)
  • Microsoft's first bond in nine years
  • The Anthropic listing documents
  • Oracle's $20B at-the-market programme
  • Issuance should pause in H2 2026 and resume in H1 2027

Checking the claims

The notes recomputed UBP's totals and checked them against other sources in the wiki. Most hold. The problems all point the same way: UBP is slightly too relaxed about Oracle and the guarantee layer.

Methodological points

Backlog-to-lease coverage (1.5x-4.5x) looks safer than it is. It sets backlog against building lease payments only. Chips and servers, ~60% of site cost by UBP's own figure, aren't counted, nor are power and operating costs. Oracle's 2.0x coverage sits next to FCF of -$24B.
"20bp on a 5.5% coupon changes very little" holds for the issuer, not the holder. On a 2063 bond with ~15 years of duration, 20bp of widening is a ~3% price loss. With the 10-year at its highest close since 2007, long AI paper carries both spread and rate risk.
Purchase commitments overlap with capex forecasts. $1.57T, 55% of the $2.9T. Adding them to capex projections double counts.
Treating take-or-pay as a loan book is the report's strongest idea. Rating agencies have done this for pipelines, shipping and power for 30 years, but not yet for compute. If they start, lab leverage (~$1.25T against ~$105B ARR, about 12x) looks very different.

A pattern worth tracking

Brookfield studied the three sector default cycles above 10% since 1997 (telecom 1998-2004, homebuilders 2006-11, energy E&P 2014-17). Same three warning signs each time, with defaults spiking about three years later:

  • [x]Capex ramp. Here.
  • [x]Negative FCF. Arrived in 2026 at Meta, Oracle, Amazon (partly) and Alphabet (one quarter), and deeply at the labs and neoclouds.
  • [~]Growth of B- and lower issuance. Growing (AI ~4.5% of HY, CoreWeave B-rated), but much of it sits in private credit outside the index.

Three years from 2026 is 2029, which lines up with UBP's 2027-28 commencement wall and Burry's 2028-29 write-off window.

How this cycle differs

  • The central borrowers are AA/AAA with large non-AI cash flows. That wasn't true of WorldCom or Global Crossing.
  • Capacity is in use today (rental rates on old GPUs are rising). Fiber was mostly dark.
  • The risk has moved into structures (SPVs, guarantees, take-or-pay) that indices and rating agencies don't yet fully count.

Where they diverge

1. Only Brookings computes what has to be true.

Capital base of $9.61T for 182.7GW built by 2032. At a 10% return, with IT (68% of cost) on a 6-year life and buildings/power (32%) on 20 years, the annual capital charge is 19.37%: $1.86T of cash flow needed. At a 50% margin that's $3.725T of annual revenue by 2032 (9.2% of GDP), vs ~$100B for OpenAI + Anthropic today. Range: $2.29T (5% return, 67% margin) to $6.72T (15%, 33%); $6.0T if GPUs last 3 years. As a price: $5.5/GPU-hour at full use, $6.9 at 80%, $7.9 at 70%, vs $6-10 market rates today, so the open question is utilization and pricing power across ~180GW of new supply.

UBP has no equivalent. Its closest is the ~12x commitments-to-ARR ratio and spread widening as a market signal. This is the biggest gap in UBP.

Note on the source note: the UBP note says this implies "~80%/year revenue growth sustained for ~8 years." Going from ~$100B to $3.725T is ~37x: about 83% a year over 6 years (2026-32), or ~57% a year over 8. The Brookings note's "~80% a year through 2032" is the consistent reading. Wiki calc
2. Detail runs the other way.

UBP gives per-company leverage, backlog, capex/revenue and lab commitments. Brookings treats the five hyperscalers in aggregate, except for the Hyperion case, but supplies the national capacity and investment model UBP doesn't attempt.

3. Only in Brookings.
  • Data center REIT beta to the S&P 500 rose from ~0.5 to ~1 since 2018. They no longer diversify.
  • A $500B NVIDIA-backstopped facility (Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, KKR; Aug 2026) with a 25% residual-value guarantee (up to $125B): the chip supplier backstopping its customers' purchases of its own chips. A parallel Google/Broadcom TPU arrangement too.
  • The $10.3T headline excludes future GPU refreshes around 2030.
4. GDP shares aren't comparable.

Brookings' 3.63% is a 2025-32 average of the full buildout. The 2.6% shown on this page is a wiki estimate from one year of hyperscaler capex. Don't quote them against each other.

5. Stance.

Brookings: "premature to conclude AI infrastructure already poses systemic risk," and asks for better disclosure. UBP ranks names by fragility and lists specific watch items.

Net read: the two papers back each other up on the facts they share (Beignet economics, the 2026 capex/cash-flow crossover, the 3-4x off-balance-sheet multiple) while answering different questions. Applying Brookings' 10%-return, 50%-margin test to UBP's numbers turns UBP's ~12x commitments-to-ARR ratio from an informal red flag into a measurable distance from the $3.7T revenue bar.

Verdict

The core claims hold up. The balance-sheet, off-balance-sheet and backlog arithmetic reconciles, and events since publication (Oracle's Jupiter notice, rising OpenAI burn estimates) support UBP's risk map.

The weak spots: a cumulative capex figure that doesn't reconcile, a probably overstated Oracle index-cap buffer, undercounted guarantees, and a coverage metric that leaves out chip costs. All point the same way: if anything, the report is slightly too comfortable about Oracle and the guarantee layer.

The main conclusion stands: hyperscaler IG is safe at wider spreads, and the fragile layer is leveraged single-contract structures. That matches the wiki's own commencement-wall assessment.
