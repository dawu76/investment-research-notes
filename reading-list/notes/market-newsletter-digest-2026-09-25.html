Theme 1

Rates: the long end takes over, and real yields are doing the work

The 10-year kept climbing after breaking its 2007 high, the 30-year took the lead, and inflation-protected bonds fell almost as much as nominals. This is a real-yield move, which hits bonds and long-duration stocks through the same discount rate.

  • 10-year closed at 5.18%, touched 5.23%Highest close since July 2007. Up 17-22bp in seven sessions, and the pace hasn't slowed

    Levels and the delta from the prior digest. The 10-year closed Thursday at 5.18%, its highest close since July 2007, up 7bp on the day (Simon Ree, Tao of Trading). Friday morning it touched 5.23% before falling back as US-Iran Hormuz talks reportedly progressed and US crude dropped to about $92 (Bernstein).

    MacroVisor puts the Thursday close within 10-15bp of its highest level since 2002.

    • Running total across the digest series: 5.01% on September 16 (digest 09-20), the first close above the July 2007 high on September 23 (09-23), 5.11% close / 5.14% intraday on September 23-24 (09-24), and now 5.18-5.23%. That is about 17-22bp in seven sessions, after Pollinate Trading had already called the prior 41bp/21-day move a 94th-percentile one-month move. The pace has not slowed after the break. Bernstein notes the 10-year is "up a full point this year"; George Noble frames it as 4% to 5.2%.
    • MacroVisor: a 20bp-plus surge in the 10Y over two days (Wednesday and Thursday), with "nearly the whole Treasury curve" above 5% this week and the 5-year breaking 5% Wednesday for the first time since 2007.
  • The 30-year is now leading5.47%, highest since 2004. The 5s30s gap widened from 41bp to 44bp on a hawkish-Fed day

    The 30-year is now leading. It rose 7bp to 5.47%, its highest since June 2004 (Ree), and James Bulltard reports it crossed 5.5% for the first time since 2004 on Friday. The prior digest had it at 5.40-5.44%.

    Ree's test from the day before was whether the 30-year would outrun the 5-year, since that would mean the fiscal argument is winning over the Fed argument. It did: 30Y +7bp vs. 5Y +4bp, widening the 5s30s gap from 41bp to 44bp.

    Ree is careful that a 3bp one-day widening is not a trend, but it matters because it came on the most hawkish Fed-speak day of the year (Theme 2). A hawkish Fed plus a steepening curve is the market saying hikes alone may not fix the long end.

    • This reverses the pattern the 09-24 digest recorded from Macro Is Dead (the curve had flattened about 25bp since early September as front-end hike pricing outran the long end). Macro Is Dead predicted the flattening would turn into a steepening once hike pricing was "substantial." One day of data now points that way.
  • Third weak auction in nine days7-year at 5.085%, highest since 1993. Indirect bidders took 57.2% vs. a 64.6% average; only 39% of the buyback was taken up

    The 7-year auction was the third weak auction in the sequence. Treasury sold $44B at 5.085%, the highest 7-year auction yield since April 1993, with a 0.7bp tail and indirect bidders (the foreign and institutional proxy) taking 57.2% vs. a 64.6% average (Ree). QTR calls it the "biggest tail since March."

A 39% take-up means holders of old long bonds are not eager to sell them back even at Treasury's price, so the buyback program Fabian Wintersberger credits with dampening the long end (Theme 6) has limited reach.
  • This is a real-yield selloffTIP -0.5% vs. IEF -0.55%. It hits bonds and long-duration stocks through the same discount rate

    The key decomposition: real yields, not inflation expectations. Ree's most useful detail: TIP fell 0.5% while IEF fell 0.55%, so inflation-protected bonds sold off almost as hard as nominals.

    "Investors aren't demanding extra compensation for inflation. They're demanding a higher real return, which is the discount rate on every stock in the S&P moving higher."

    This matches TSCS Research's finding in the 09-21 digest that 96bp of the 104bp rise in the 10-year since February 27 was real yield and only 8bp was breakeven. Why it matters: a breakeven-driven selloff would argue for TIPS and commodities as hedges; a real-yield selloff hurts both bonds and long-duration equities through the same discount-rate channel, which is why Vincent Jansen (Theme 6) now finds real yields competitive with equity shareholder yield.

    • George Noble and Danny Dayan (Macro Musings) add a structural reason to distrust breakevens. Breakevens have sat near 2.5% the entire cycle regardless of prints, and they argue the Fed bought far more 10-year nominals than 10-year TIPS during QE, mechanically compressing breakevens, then read that same compressed market as proof expectations are anchored. Their advice: "Stop plugging break-evens into your models."
  • Bond volatility caught up, equity volatility didn'tMOVE went from 78.6 to 104.6 in three sessions; VIX is 15.7-17.7

    Bond volatility caught up. The MOVE index jumped about 21% on Wednesday, then another 9.6% Thursday to 104.6, its highest since March (QTR, Ree). Continuity matters here: the 09-21 digest had MOVE at 81 for the week ending September 18, and the 09-23 digest had it at 78.6 on September 22, both flagged as unusually calm for the yield level (135 in October 2023 at similar 10-year yields).

    The "fear not priced" gap that TSCS Research flagged has closed by about a third in two sessions. MOVE is still below the 115 recorded in March 2026 and well below October 2023's 135.

    • Equity vol has not followed. VIX was 15.67 Thursday (Ree) and 17.65 Friday morning (MacroVisor). MacroVisor: the VIX "says equity traders aren't pricing much stress into a bond market that just repriced by 20bp in two days." Bernstein's pre-market version: stock swings "near their calmest of the year" while bond swings "just hit their highest since March."
  • Global confirmation and a firmer dollarGerman 10-year at 3.59% (high since 2009), DXY above 100, 30-year mortgage at 7.49%

    Global confirmation. The German 10-year hit 3.59%, its highest since 2009 (Ree), extending the European repricing the 09-24 digest covered via the 110bp French-German spread. The dollar index is above 100 and the Bloomberg Dollar Spot Index is up about 2% in two weeks, its best run in six months, after the Fed's first hike in three years caught speculators short per CFTC data (MacroVisor).

    Dollar risk reversals have jumped at all three tenors since the hike, which MacroVisor reads as "a second market confirming this rally." Morgan Stanley publicly said "we were wrong" on its weak-dollar call and now expects a stronger dollar into 2027 (Bernstein).

    The yen rose as much as 0.6% to 157.95 after Japan's finance minister said Trump raised concerns about yen weakness; MacroVisor treats it as a bounce until MOF or BoJ acts, with a MOF primary-dealer meeting Monday.

    • Mortgage rates: the average 30-year mortgage is 7.49%, highest since April 2024 (Bernstein). That feeds the housing-weakness point Kyler Johnson makes in Theme 2.
  • Three explanations for why yields are hereOil (+65% correlation with the 10Y), 6%+ nominal growth, and fiscal dominance (interest $1.05T vs. defense $833B)

Theme 2

The Fed: hawkish talk, contested data, and a 1999 analogy

Three Fed officials talked up more hikes, but October hike pricing held near two-thirds rather than rising. The data argument (core PCE, the payroll breakeven, energy pass-through) is unresolved, and September 30 is the next test.

  • Three Fed speakers lean toward another hikeWilliams puts a year-end hike on record; he is a permanent voter

    Three officials talked up more hikes on Thursday (Ree): NY Fed's Williams, "It's likely that another rate hike may be appropriate by the end of the year"; Cleveland's Hammack, inflation risks "tilted to the upside"; Philadelphia's Paulson, "some modest further tightening may be warranted." Paulson's shift from the hold camp was first noted in the 09-24 digest; Williams putting a year-end hike on record is new and carries more weight because the NY Fed president is a permanent voter and usually signals the leadership's view.

  • Hike pricing held but didn't extendOctober odds 66-70% vs. 71% prior. Swaps price three hikes over the next year

    Market pricing held, it did not extend. October hike odds are "about two-in-three" (Bernstein, Friday), "roughly 70%" (The Prime Report, Thursday night) and 66% (Pelosky, after the midweek selloff). The 09-24 digest had 71%, up from 54% on September 22 and under 10% a month earlier.

    So the jump is intact but stalled. MacroVisor says swaps fully price three more hikes over the next year.

    A paywalled Credit From Macro to Micro note reads SOFR September 2027 as implying a terminal rate around 4.85-4.90%. Those two framings need reconciling against the current policy rate, which neither piece states, so the digest does not compute an implied path.

  • Noble and Dayan: back to June 1999They say the Fed sees policy as still loose. In 1999 the 10-year reached 6.75% before stocks broke

    The 1999 analogy (Noble and Dayan, Substack live, "Back to June 1999"). Their claims: Warsh called the neutral rate "an academic exercise" and described the hike as removing accommodation, which means the Fed thinks policy is still loose; there is no forward guidance, so the market cannot price the endpoint; and Greenspan ran the same playbook in 1999, when "stocks and bonds played ping-pong until the 10-year hit 6.75% and things broke in March 2000." The relevance: if the analogy holds, the current 5.2% 10-year is not the stopping point, and the equity damage comes late.

    Their supporting data:

    • Household wealth is up nearly $70T this cycle, and the savings rate is 3% vs. 8% last cycle even with much higher rates. Boomers are drawing down savings instead of adding to them, which reverses one of the forces that held rates down after 2008.
    • The 2-year has gone from 4.3% to 4.93%, and import prices are running about 7%. That is why they think Bessent's last lever (a weaker dollar) would feed inflation and hurt bonds anyway. Noble says he read Bessent's doubling of the buybacks as "a call to short more bonds." ConflictRee has the 2-year at 4.87% Thursday; The Prime Report's September 18 snapshot had 3.98%, a figure the 09-23 digest already flagged as likely wrong.
    • Credit spreads have not moved while the 10-year went from 4% to 5.2%, and stocks are up over 80% since the Fed stopped hiking in 2023. Their conclusion: "The market is screaming that policy is too loose." Positioning advice: own gold, gold miners, energy and copper; avoid consumer, tech, long duration and 60/40. Dayan thinks gold is dead money until the Fed cuts; Noble disagrees.
    • They cite University of Michigan inflation expectations above 7%. ConflictThat conflicts with The Prime Report, which has the September preliminary one-year expectation at 4.6%. The 7% may refer to a different horizon or subgroup; treat it as unverified.
  • The dovish case and a dated testCore CPI 2.35% vs. core PCE 3.4%. A Sept 30 BLS change could cut core PCE by 20-40bp

    The dovish side. Jim Paulson (via Kyler Johnson, DeepValue Capital) argues the hike rested on "recent rear-view mirror" assessments, pointing to slow job growth, August core CPI at its lowest year-over-year rate since 2021, and soft retail sales. Johnson adds that year-over-year job growth bottomed at only +0.073% in December 2025, a level historically associated with recession, though it never turned negative, and that rising mortgage rates are pushing down housing, about 40% of core CPI.

    Tom Lee (Fundstrat, via Podcast Alpha) calls the Warsh hike a mistake: core CPI 2.35% (down from 2.5%) vs. core PCE 3.4%, a full-point gap he blames on mechanical fee components and an AI-driven memory-chip price spike. He expects a September 30 BLS methodology change to cut core PCE by 20-40bp.

    That is a dated, falsifiable test: if core PCE does not drop and the Fed stays hawkish, Lee's macro case (and his ETH targets in Theme 11) weakens.

    • Apollo, quoted by Johnson, takes the opposite side on energy pass-through: diesel "is an intermediate input embedded in the delivered cost of nearly every physical good," so it "migrates with a lag into core goods and services, which is exactly the kind of pass-through the Fed cannot dismiss as transitory." This is the mechanism behind the hawks' patience: core CPI can look benign today while the diesel shock (record $6.53/gallon on Tuesday per Bernstein) is still working through.
  • Low-hire, low-fire labor market+162K payrolls vs. a breakeven now under 10K. Labor share hit a record-low 52.8%

    The labor half of the mandate (Ed D'Agostino, Mauldin Economics, "The Optics of Low-Hire, Low-Fire"). August payrolls were +162K, unemployment 4.1%, 7.0M unemployed vs. 7.3M openings. The structural point: an April Fed note projected labor-force growth could fall below 10K/month in 2026, so the payroll gain needed to hold unemployment steady drops from about 155K (2023-24) to 85K (2025) to under 10K (2026).

    • Why it matters weak payroll prints no longer imply rising unemployment, which weakens the Paulson/Johnson recession read above. One tension the piece does not address: if breakeven is under 10K and August added 162K, unemployment should be falling, yet it held at 4.1%.
    • Productivity is carrying growth: since Q4 2019, nonfarm business output is up 17.4% on hours up only 3.3%, so output per hour is up 13.7%.   • Consumers are gloomy but still spendingUMich 48.1 (four-month low), Costco comps +6.7%, new home sales 684K

    Consumer data. University of Michigan sentiment fell to 48.1 in September (final), a four-month low, with gas above $4 for more than two months; year-ahead inflation expectations hit their highest since June (Bernstein). The preliminary read was 47.8 (Prime).

    Costco comps rose 6.7%, which MacroVisor reads as "spending hasn't cracked and gives the Fed little reason to ease off." New home sales ran at 684K vs. about 615K expected, the fastest pace this year (Ree).

Theme 3

Credit: stress is in the basement, not the whole building

High-yield and investment-grade spreads sit near their tights. The damage is in CCC borrowers who must refinance 2021-22 debt at today's rates, and most of HYG's drop is interest-rate exposure, not default risk.

  • HYG's 52-week low is mostly rate mathHYG -4.1% vs. rate-hedged HYGH -0.4% over a year. HY spread 2.73%, near its tights

    The scary chart and why most of it is bond math (Simon Ree). HYG closed at 77.89, its lowest since April 2025, while SPY sat about 1.4% off its record. In Ree's 11 years of daily data, HYG had never closed at a 52-week low with SPY within 5% of its high; it has now done it 6 times since September 10.

    His decomposition: over the past year HYG is down 4.1% in price, while HYGH (the rate-hedged version) is down only 0.4%, so about 90% of HYG's decline is Treasury duration, not default risk. Adding back $4.36 of distributions since the October high, a holder is still up about 1%.

    • Spreads confirm it. The ICE BofA high-yield spread was 2.73% Wednesday, 13bp off its 3-year tight of 2.60% (August 28), and investment-grade spreads are 0.77%, a few basis points from their tights. Pollinate Trading cited the same 2.73% in the 09-24 digest as "near the tightest of this century," so the reading has held for at least two sessions through the auction stress. Ree also notes that at the last two big SPY tops (January 2022, February 2025), HYG was within 1% of its high and gave no warning, so HYG's track record as a top signal is weak.
  • The real stress is in CCCCCC spread 10.93%, up 147bp since June. BB sits at 1.59%

    Where the suspicion is justified: CCC. The CCC spread hit 10.93% on Wednesday, its widest since April 7, 2025 (Liberation Day), up almost in a straight line from 9.46% at the start of June (about +147bp), while BB spreads sit at 1.59%, near 3-year tights. Kyler Johnson makes the same observation (CCC widening while AAA and BBB stay flat) and ties it to small caps: IWM falling while SPY is flat.

    • Torsten Slok (Apollo, "Higher for Longer Hits the Lowest Rated") gives the mechanism. With CCC yields around 15%, every month rates stay high pushes more CCC borrowers from the 2021-22 vintages into the refinancing wall with less cash to service debt. Pain is sharpest in heavily levered, PE-backed technology, healthcare and consumer discretionary, where floating-rate debt, thin margins and AI disruption risk combine. "Monetary policy is working with a lag and working unevenly": strong balance sheets locked in cheap fixed-rate debt, so transmission is "running mainly through the bottom of the credit stack." His recommendation is to move up in quality, since high-grade credit still offers attractive all-in yields.
    • JPMorgan equity strategists (via Ree) make the matching equity argument: margins are high, net debt is 20-40% below historical norms and corporate debt has 5-6 years to run, so higher rates take a long time to bite. Thursday tested it (30-year at a 22-year high, three hawkish Fed speakers, Houthi missiles on Saudi Arabia) and SPY closed basically flat.
  • Ree's tripwiresJunk spread above 3.00% or HYGH below 84.78 would mean stress is spreading

    Ree's tripwires. Either the overall junk spread through 3.00% or HYGH breaking its March low of 84.78 would mean the "rot is starting to spread" upstairs. "Credit tops tend to start in the basement and work their way upstairs."

    His overall rating: "a dim yellow warning light."

  • Cross-reference: ai-compute-commencement-wall-and-refinancing-trap for the refinancing-cliff framework, which the CCC data now shows operating in the general leveraged-loan market and not only in AI.

SourcesSimon Ree (Tao of Trading), Torsten Slok (Apollo, The Daily Spark), Kyler Johnson (DeepValue Capital).

Theme 4

AI financing: Project Jupiter's details, and demand that keeps signing

Oracle's New Mexico campus is strained by power and permits, not demand. New compute contracts keep getting signed, but over half the AI backlog depends on two private labs.

  • Project Jupiter: bigger and later than reported$165B campus, $18B loan from about 20 banks, pipeline delayed to Feb 2027

    What is new on Oracle since the 09-24 digest. That digest covered the force majeure notice, Oracle's -3.28% day, debt below 90 cents, and Craig Shapiro's CDS ladder (Oracle about 224bp). The new details:

    • Size and lenders. Project Jupiter is a 2.45GW, $165B campus (Ree) backed by an $18B bank loan from roughly 20 banks (MacroVisor). Blue Owl has about $3B of equity in the project (Ree). The notice went to Blue Owl's Stack Infrastructure (The Prime Report). Oracle's credit gauge hit a record Thursday (MacroVisor, QTR, Ree), and the shares are down 29% this year (MacroVisor).
    • Timing. The gas pipeline feeding the site is delayed nearly six months, to February 1, 2027, after repeated permit denials (MacroVisor); Reuters says the project is now about a year late (via Ree).
    • What the clause does. ConflictMacroVisor: if both sides agree force majeure applies, Oracle could win up to a three-year delay on rent, though it would still owe the full lease term once payments start. WSJ (via Bernstein): Oracle can't get out of the lease and owes rent whether or not the site has power. Theme 9

China: the datacenter buildout nobody measured, Alibaba's cloud, and the summit

SemiAnalysis counts over 24GW of Chinese datacenter capacity, its hyperscalers just turned FCF-negative on doubled capex, Alibaba's AI cloud accelerated for a ninth quarter, and the Trump-Xi summit delivered only a short truce extension.

  • China has over 24GW of datacentersvs. 56GW in the US. Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu doubled capex and turned FCF-negative

    SemiAnalysis China Datacenter Model (preview). Building-level tracking of 1,000+ facilities across 60+ operators puts China's fleet at over 24GW, vs. 56GW in the US (2026 year-end), about 15GW APAC ex-China, 14GW EMEA and 2GW Latin America. That excludes ~20GW of dated pipeline and ~30GW announced.

    Published estimates of China's capacity had differed by 15x, so this is the first hard number.

    • Capex inflection: in 2Q26, Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu spent a combined $20B, more than double year over year, and all three posted negative free cash flow for the first time. Including ByteDance, combined capex went from about $35B (2024) to $50B+ (2025) and is tracking toward $100B in 2026. US hyperscalers turned FCF-negative earlier in the cycle (the 09-22 digest tracked construction-in-progress above $374B); China is now following the same pattern a few quarters behind.
    • ByteDance occupies about one-fifth of China's delivered capacity and rents nearly all of it, making it the most important customer for every wholesale colocation provider. GDS and VNET signed about 1.3GW of wholesale orders in 1H26 (vs. under 10MW retail) but captured only about a third of ByteDance and Alibaba orders in 2024-2026 YTD.
    • Why vacancy and shortage coexist: legacy retail racks (about 90% of early facilities ran under 2kW) sit at 30-50% utilization and can't host AI densities, while wholesale AI buildings in the west fill fast. At VNET, wholesale utilization is back above 70% vs. about 60% for retail. Wholesale prices halved from about $80/kW/month, and GDS expects another 18 months to digest the reset.
    • Speed and cost: China delivers a 100MW facility in about 12 months (down from ~18 months in the cloud era) with 3-6 months of permitting, vs. 12-18 months of construction plus 12-13 months of permitting in the best US modular case. The US buildout is power-gated; China's is chip-gated. Grid-company capex for the 15th Five-Year Plan (2026-2030) is over $746B (¥5T), 40% above the prior plan. Inner Mongolia, with power at about half Tier-1 city prices, has become "China's Johor." Overseas leasing by Chinese hyperscalers is set to double to about 4GW by 2029, before counting GPUs rented from Western clouds.
    • Why it matters this is the counterpoint to the US permitting story in Theme 4. Project Jupiter is late on a gas-pipeline permit; China's binding constraint is chip supply under export controls.
  • Alibaba: AI cloud accelerates, profit missesCloud +45%; AI run rate $7.3B guided to $10B; net income -38%

    Alibaba FY1Q27 (Outperforming the Market). Revenue +8.6% to Rmb 269B, in line. AI cloud and compute revenue +45% to Rmb 48B, the ninth straight quarter of acceleration, with adjusted EBITA margin up 4.4 points to 12% (from 10% last quarter).

    AI-related products are Rmb 12.4B per quarter, a $7.3B run rate that management guides to $10B next quarter (+37% sequentially). MaaS ARR passed Rmb 16B in August with a Rmb 30B target by year-end.

    Management sees a capex payback under three years and targets $100B of external cloud revenue by 2031 at a 20% EBITA margin. The cost side: net income fell 38% to Rmb 21B vs. Rmb 25B expected, EPS Rmb 8.52 vs. 10.60, and the AI labs and applications segment lost Rmb 13.9B.

    Core commerce (CMR) fell 7% while quick commerce grew 45%.

    The Trump-Xi summit. Mostly ceremony. The one deliverable, a two-month truce extension (to January 10 per Bernstein; Bessent said from November), has not been confirmed by Beijing (MacroVisor, Inside China).

    USTR Greer said AI chips were not discussed and export rules stay as they are; he promises "a lot more details" Monday, and the first thing to check is farm purchases, where China is behind on about $17B of commitments (Bernstein). China Beige Book reports US companies ordered more from Chinese suppliers in September; Eurasia Group puts the odds of stable ties at their highest since Trump returned.
