## 1. Rates: The 10-Year Clears Its Last Cap Since 2007

* **The core move.** The 10-year Treasury yield jumped roughly 14-16bp on Wednesday (September 23) to close at **5.11%, touching 5.14% intraday** (QTR's Fringe Finance, Sharpe Two puts the intraday high at 5.08%), **its highest level since July 2007**, and MacroVisor had it at **5.13%** by Thursday morning. The 30-year climbed to **5.40-5.44%, its highest since 2004** (MacroVisor, QTR), and the **5-year crossed 5% for the first time since 2007** (Sharpe Two). The Breakdown (Blockworks) independently corroborates via crypto-market plumbing: the move was **the largest single-day increase in yields in over a year and the second-biggest single-day MOVE index increase this year**.
  * This directly extends [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]]'s finding that the 10Y closed at its highest since July 2007 on the September 23 flash PMI, that digest captured the initial break; today's newsletters confirm the level held and grade the follow-through into the 30-year and 5-year, which had not yet broken their own multi-decade caps the day before. **10Y up roughly 35bp in September alone** (QTR), consistent with the digest series' running total: 5.01% on September 16 ([[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-20]]), then the July-2007 close on September 23.
  * **The trigger, per Pinebrook Capital's sigma decomposition:** real yields rose 13bp (2.63% to 2.76%) and nominal yields 15bp (4.96% to 5.11%) on September 23. Scored against four different volatility lookback windows, the move registers as **2.3σ to 2.9σ depending on the window** (pre-2020 low-vol window scores it most extreme; post-2020 high-vol window scores it mildest). Pinebrook's historical-shock study (2009-present, 43-50 observations per bucket) finds only one statistically robust pattern: **downward real-yield shocks mean-revert** (average +15.4bp recovery over 60 days, t=2.25, p=0.03). Wednesday was an **up-shock**, which falls into the "directionally-suggestive-but-unproven" bucket (p=0.26), the data leans toward yields extending rather than reverting, but doesn't prove it.
  * **MacroVisor's account adds the mechanism**: the flash composite PMI hit **58.4 against 55.3 expected, a five-year high**, manufacturing printed **57.0 against 53.7 expected**, and **input cost inflation rose to its highest since October 2022**. This is the same PMI print [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]] flagged as triggering the initial break; today's newsletters give the manufacturing sub-index and input-cost detail that digest lacked. MacroVisor: "Good news is bad news again... a booming economy with a supply shock on top is exactly what paves the way for the Fed to hike comfortably."
  * **The 5-year auction was the second leg.** Treasury sold $70bn of 5-year notes at **5.033%, about 3bp above the pre-sale level**: a "tail," meaning buyers demanded a discount. MacroEdge Research calls it a "broken" auction "which really deserved a D-." **Dealers absorbed their biggest share since 2024** (MacroVisor, citing the FT), meaning end investors stepped back. J.L. Bernstein's Pre-Market Brief flags the **7-year auction at 1pm ET** as the next test: a repeat of weak demand would confirm buyers are still backing away.
  * **Fed repricing accelerated further.** Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson, previously in the hold-steady camp, now says underlying inflation isn't budging and the Fed may need to hike again (Bernstein). MacroVisor has **October hike odds at 71%** with **37bp priced by year-end**; QTR's Fringe Finance and MacroVisor both cite roughly the same range (67-71%). This extends [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]]'s "October hike odds moved from about 54% to about 70%", today's newsletters confirm that repricing held and ticked slightly higher, a month after odds were under 10%. Richmond Fed's Barkin separately named three hawkish drivers: sustained high gas prices, new Canada tariffs, and AI-buildout supply-chain pressure on consumer prices (Bernstein).
  * **The selloff is global and reaching duration records elsewhere too.** Japan's 10Y hit **3.07%, its highest since 1996** (MacroVisor); the French-German spread widened to **110bp**, a level "last seen weeks before Draghi's 'whatever it takes' in 2012" (MacroVisor), a new comparison this digest hasn't carried before, distinct from [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]]'s eurozone-composite framing. German Ifo beat (89.9 vs. 89 expected) adds to the case for an October ECB hike. Bond volatility itself is stressed: **the ICE BofA MOVE index hit its highest since March** (MacroVisor).

## 2. Oil: Brent Rallies on Iran's Hard Line While WTI and the Crack Spread Tell a Different Story

* **Brent diverged sharply from crude fundamentals on renewed Hormuz rhetoric.** Iran's president told the UN his country **won't allow free passage through the Strait of Hormuz under a US blockade** (Bernstein). Brent **closed at $103.08, snapping a five-day losing streak, then pushed to $105.59 in futures trading** by Thursday morning (MacroVisor); Bernstein separately reports Brent **"pushed past $105"** with next-August contracts at their **highest since May**. This is a continuation of [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]]'s Brent-snapping-a-losing-streak story (that digest had Brent at $99.25 settling, then above $100 on US-Iran talks); today's spike to $103-105 shows the round trip extending further as diplomatic hopes faded again.
  * MacroVisor's positioning read: **Iran is "reviewing Washington's latest response"** while President Pezeshkian insists Hormuz stays closed with the blockade in place, a harder line than the "could reopen within 7 days" signal from a "senior Iranian official" cited in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]]. The gap between official rhetoric (harder) and unnamed-official signaling (softer) is itself informative: it suggests real internal disagreement inside Iran's negotiating posture and no settled line.
* **WTI is telling the opposite story, and the crack spread is the tell.** Lance Roberts (Real Investment Advice) reports **crude peaked at $106 on September 15 and has since fallen nearly 15% to $90**, even as **gasoline futures rose 1.9% over the same period**. The crack spread (what a refiner earns turning crude into fuel) **widened from roughly $40 to $58/barrel, more than three times the twenty-year median of $17 and in the 99.8th percentile of every trading day since 2006**. Roberts's framing: **"no relief at the pump despite crude falling,"** because refining capacity and freight costs are offsetting the crude decline. This is a sharper, more precisely quantified version of the refined-product-tightness thread MacroEdge (Permian) and MacroVisor carried in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]] around the diesel export-ban debate, today's data shows the crack-spread blowout as measurable, not just directional, and gives a specific percentile (99.8th) that lets future digests track whether it's still widening or has peaked.
  * The Brent/WTI divergence itself (Brent up on geopolitical risk premium, WTI/crack spread reflecting a real US refined-product supply problem) means the "oil is rallying" headline from Bernstein and MacroVisor and the "oil is falling but gas isn't" headline from Roberts are both true simultaneously, describing different parts of the barrel.
* **A second, unrelated lubricant-supply shock is compounding the refined-product story.** Adam Tooze (Chartbook), citing the FT: a **worldwide motor-oil shortage** has pushed Group III base oil (used in full-synthetic motor oil) to **$12.45/gallon, almost quadrupling since February**, with **Costco capping DIY motor-oil purchases at one per customer** after raising its 10-quart full-synthetic price from **~$30 a year ago to $57.99**. This is a distinct supply chain from crude/diesel (base-oil refining capacity, not Hormuz transit), but it compounds the same "goods inflation from refining bottlenecks, not from crude itself" thesis Roberts's crack-spread data supports.

## 3. AI Credit Stress: Oracle's Force Majeure Notice and the CDS Repricing

* **The event.** Oracle sent a **force majeure notice** to the Blue Owl Capital unit developing **Project Jupiter**, a **2.4-2.45GW AI data-center campus in New Mexico** tied to the Stargate buildout and Oracle's OpenAI relationship, relying heavily on **Bloom Energy fuel cells** (QTR's Fringe Finance, Matt Levine, Bernstein). Per Bloomberg (via Matt Levine): the natural-gas pipeline needed to supply the site has been **pushed back roughly six months** on permitting problems, and a **separate air-quality permit for the fuel-cell system remains pending**: the campus has already hit "the denial of a permit key to its plans for energy resources." Rather than trying to exit as tenant, **Oracle is positioning to defer payments** if the facility doesn't come online in 2028 as planned. **Debt tied to the development is already trading below 90 cents on the dollar** (Levine, citing Bloomberg's Natarajan/Ford/Seligson).
  * **Oracle and Bloom Energy's damage-control response** ("remains on our planned schedule," "commonplace in developments of this scale") drew skepticism from QTR: "companies don't issue force majeure notices because everything is going fucking fantastic." Oracle fell **-3.28%** on the day (Bernstein); Blue Owl ($OWL) fell too.
  * **Matt Levine's framing distinguishes two AI-credit risks.** The large-scale, long-run risk is *too many* data centers getting built (empty capacity if AI demand disappoints). The **smaller, more immediate risk Project Jupiter exemplifies is too few data centers getting built on schedule**: permits and construction lag behind bond issuance, tenants have somewhere else to go, and the project-specific bonds don't get paid back even though aggregate AI demand is intact. He cites a Quinn Emanuel client alert warning that "force majeure clauses will be central to the disputes that follow" as the AI buildout enters "a period of heightened construction, supply chain, and regulatory risk," and flags (via an October 2025 S&P Global Ratings note on the comparable Meta/Blue Owl "Beignet" deal) that most such leases cap tenant rent-abatement risk at a fixed dollar amount ($750M in that case) through a defined date, meaning the downside for lenders in a well-structured deal is bounded, which is the counter-argument to reading this as systemic.
  * **Craig Shapiro (The Alethea Narrative) supplies the CDS quantification that turns this into a market-wide signal, not a single-name story.** AI-linked CDS is tiered by funding model and **has been widening since June**: **Microsoft ~50bp, Meta ~94bp, Oracle ~224bp, CoreWeave above 850bp**. The **Meta Hyperion data-center bond hit an all-time low** the same day. Shapiro's transmission-chain argument: credit stress starts at the financing layer (tenants contest/defer payments → developer cash flows uncertain → lenders and structured-paper holders mark down positions → new AI debt clears wider or not at all → debt-funded builders like Oracle and neoclouds slow) and only *then* reaches the "insulated" megacap core, because chip/equipment suppliers sell to debt-funded buyers and cloud revenue depends on capacity arriving on schedule. **Equities have not yet followed**: the Nasdaq held up on a day real yields rose 14bp, and Shapiro's explicit watch item is whether CDS keeps widening while the Nasdaq holds, "the equity market is underpricing the financing risk behind the buildout."
    * This sharpens the bifurcation framework [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]] introduced (AI capex/megacaps insulated from rates vs. housing/small caps exposed): Shapiro's contribution is a *specific transmission channel* (credit, not rates) through which the "insulated" side stops being insulated, and today's Oracle event is presented as the first concrete instance of that channel activating.
  * **QTR's separate dispatch adds the private-credit angle**: "another private credit fund limiting withdrawals," which QTR continues to call a possible "fuse" for an AI unwind within 6-10 months (a call first made in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]]; today's newsletters don't name the fund, so this is unconfirmed continuity rather than new evidence).
* Sources: QTR's Fringe Finance (2 dispatches), Matt Levine (Money Stuff), Craig Shapiro (The Alethea Narrative), J.L. Bernstein (Pivot & Flow, 2 dispatches).

## 4. AI Buildout Economics: A $10.3 Trillion Bet, the Capital Cycle, and the Model Price War

* **Michael Parekh (ARD #170) sizes the buildout against prior US infrastructure booms.** Citing a new Brookings paper (Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh) and the Wall Street Journal: **total US AI/data-center investment from 2025 through 2032 is projected at ~$10.3 trillion, or about 3.6% of US GDP per year for eight years**: "data-center spending is greater than that for the canals, railroads and grid combined." For comparison: the **1800s railroad boom ran ~2%/year**, **1950s interstate highways ~1%/year**, **electrification ~0.5%/year** (all measured against their era's smaller GDP base). **Goldman Sachs separately estimates AI investment at close to 2% of US GDP this year alone.** Parekh's own qualitative addition: unlike railroads (engine technology barely changed year to year), AI infrastructure specs are being rethought roughly **every six months**, forcing continuous re-architecture mid-buildout, a risk dimension the historical comparisons don't capture.
  * **Michael Burry's independent, harder-edged version of the same "size of the bet" question**: aggregate **net capital investment/GDP for the S&P 500 sits at 2.07% as of June 30, the highest level in any capital cycle over the last four decades except the dot-com peak** (which didn't fully recover for a decade and a half). Burry flags that in the 2000 cycle, **capex continued rising well past the NASDAQ's March 2000 peak**, and in the 2007 cycle, **investment continued ramping through 2009** even as banks failed, i.e., capex peaking is not a reliable top signal; it can keep growing through a bust. He also flags 12 straight quarters (2003-2006) where depreciation *exceeded* capex, following the TMT bust, as the historical analog for what he expects AI-era depreciation write-downs to eventually look like. His companion Part V teases a forensic balance-sheet check on "epic levels of spending" backed by **$3 trillion of aggregate purchase commitments, future leases, guarantees, and construction-in-progress across the Big 5 hyperscalers** (Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Oracle), consistent with, but a separate estimate from, the FT's $300B off-balance-sheet residual-value-guarantee figure cited in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]].
* **DeepSeek's monetization is accelerating faster than its narrative implies.** Per The Information (via Parekh): DeepSeek's **annualized revenue run rate hit $1 billion, more than double from under $500 million a few months ago**, while it finalizes a **$7.5 billion raise at a ~$75 billion valuation** ahead of a planned Shanghai listing. It **raised prices 2.3-4.5x in August with no customer attrition**, runs an **~83% developer-business gross margin** (above Anthropic's and OpenAI's over similar periods, per the same reporting), and CEO Liang Wenfeng says **more than 70% of compute goes to training new models, less than 30% to serving existing ones**: prioritizing capability over near-term revenue capture. This extends [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]]'s §4 finding that **DeepSeek V4.1 Flash usage was up ~170% on OpenRouter in a week**; today's figures show that usage growth converting into real, high-margin revenue rather than just market share. Risk flags unchanged from prior coverage: export-control-limited Nvidia access, pending Huawei training-chip availability (possibly Q4), and China's internet regulator investigating DeepSeek and Moonshot over Anthropic's data-routing allegations.
* **Google confirmed it remains in the frontier race.** New DeepMind chief Koray Kavukcuoglu ("KK") said **Gemini 4 is in early post-training** and Google hopes to ship it before year-end, after Gemini 3.5 Pro's promised June release never materialized (The Information, via Parekh). Google's structural argument (Parekh's own read, not sourced to KK): full-stack ownership from TPU silicon through cloud to billions of search users lets Google ship "good enough" models at scale/latency advantages the pure-play labs (focused on the enterprise segment) don't share.
* Sources: Michael Parekh (AI Ramblings Daily, ARD #170), Michael Burry (Cassandra Unchained).

## 7. China: A Cross-Border Framework for Predicting Regulatory Blowups

* **Baiguan (guest: Pandawatch/Enrique Becerra)** presents a framework built from **25 historical regulatory-action case studies split evenly between the US and China**, arguing that "national flag" is a poor proxy for regulatory risk: US for-profit college stocks fell **~90%** in 2010 (same policy concern as China's 2021 after-school-tutoring crackdown), **Coinbase and Futu both fell 30-40%** on unlicensed-operator findings (SEC vs. CSRC), and **FICO is down 60%** since the FHFA moved to open credit-scoring competition, comparable to Chinese online-lender share collapses under new rate caps. The key operational difference: **US regulatory drawdowns typically play out over 2-3 years (company-by-company); Chinese ones can complete in a week** (system-wide, simultaneous).
  * **The single most predictive flag, present in 24 of 25 cases**: "the narrative is already in print," meaning regulators and state media had publicly flagged the policy concern repeatedly in speeches and op-eds before acting (China's tutoring crackdown had 3 years of prior warnings). The second most common flag (21/25): the target company was visibly doing very well while drawing public complaints about it.
  * **The recovery framework**: companies whose *core value* (the product itself, or an untouched part of the system) survives regulatory action tend to fully recover: Moutai **10x'd** in the six years after 2012's anti-extravagance crackdown; Visa/Mastercard **10x'd** after the 2010 Durbin Amendment cut debit fees but left the card-network oligopoly untouched. Companies whose core value *is* the regulated thing (bank financing for Chinese property developers, government-backed loan guarantees for US for-profit colleges) tend not to recover.
  * **Applied to current cases**: Futu's valuation is already down ~1/3, which the piece reads as roughly proportional to the ~1/3 of its business (unlicensed mainland-China brokerage) that got hit, with two-thirds of the franchise (its actual product) untouched. Trip.com is framed as the real swing case: SAMR probed Trip.com's dominant market position this week (system-level, not product-level), and whether it follows Meituan's path (probe barely dented earnings; SAMR summoned Trip.com's competitors over unfair-competition concerns days later, read as a relief signal for management) or Alibaba's path (multi-year share loss to PDD/Bytedance during a scrutiny period) depends on whether competitors show up while management is distracted.
* Sources: Baiguan (Pandawatch/Enrique Becerra).

## 8. Positioning, Yield-Chasing Structures, and Crypto Plumbing

* **The crypto/Treasury yield spread hit a fresh record of persistent negativity (The Breakdown, Blockworks).** In 2026, **96.9% of days have shown onchain stablecoin lending rates paying less than T-bill yields**, exceeding 2023's 89.0% and marking the **third-longest such discount stretch on record**; the current gap is **-115bp, in the bottom quintile of its entire history**. Historically, when the spread has sat this deep in the bottom quintile, the **90-day forward change has averaged +85bp**, with the modal outcome closer to **+100bp**: meaning onchain lending rates (or, mechanically, stablecoin supply) would need to reprice up to close the gap, a dynamic reinforced by the rising Treasury curve documented in §1.
