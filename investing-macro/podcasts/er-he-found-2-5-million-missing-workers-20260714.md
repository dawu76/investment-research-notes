# He Found 2.5 Million Missing Workers | Eric Pachman on the Labor Crisis That is Already Here

Detailed overview and analysis of the *Excess Returns* podcast featuring **Eric Pachman**, founder and lead strategist at Data 4 The People, hosted by Matt Ziegler. The episode was released on July 14, 2026.

---

### I. Executive Summary
Eric Pachman provides a granular, data-driven analysis of the U.S. labor market and inflation dynamics. He highlights that headline employment metrics mask a severe labor crisis: the BLS model revisions in January 2026 caused **2.5 million prime-age workers** to vanish from labor force projections, while male labor force participation has collapsed to a record-low **54.7%**. The engine of job growth is concentrated in the low-wage **Medicaid Care Economy** (home health and personal care aids making $35,800/year). State-level QCEW data shows that **32 to 33 states** would have experienced net job losses without this sector, making the proposed **$1 trillion Medicaid cut starting in 2027** a major tail risk for job growth and consumer spending. On inflation, Pachman criticizes Core CPI/PCE methodologies and explains the U.S. refinery bottleneck using the **Fruit Salad Analogy**: record U.S. shale production is light-sweet crude, while Gulf Coast refineries are designed for heavy-sour crude. Mismatching crude grades spikes crack spreads, and closing the Strait of Hormuz is the "COVID of oil markets," threatening critical diesel inventories and driving systemic logistics inflation.

### III. Detailed Key Takeaways

#### 1. The 2.5 Million Missing Workers and Collapsing Male Participation
Pachman highlights that in January 2026, the BLS/Census reshuffled their population models. This statistical adjustment shifted **2.5 million prime working-age people** out of the labor force projections into older demographic brackets (e.g. 75+). Furthermore, the labor force participation rate has hit a critical cliff. While women have supported labor growth, **men are leaving the workforce**. For men aged 45 and over, the participation rate has collapsed to **54.7%**—a historic low, down from 62.9% historically and the prior record minimum of 57.5% in the 1990s.

> "A change in the way that the models were measuring the age... we lost about two and a half million prime age workers... labor force participation rate fell... [For men 45+] it's down to 54.7%... the lowest level by far on record."
> — *Eric Pachman*

#### 2. The Medicaid Care Economy and the $1 Trillion Budget Cut
Headline job creation is heavily dependent on a single low-wage sector: the **Medicaid Care Economy** (comprising *Social Assistance*, *Individual and Family Services*, and *Home Health Aids*). 
*   **Largest Employment Class:** Home health and personal care aids represent the largest single occupational class in the U.S., with **4,358,810 workers** earning a median wage of **$17.21/hour ($35,800/year)**. This workforce is 80% female and 30-40% immigrant, with wages sitting near the poverty line.
*   **State-Level Vulnerability:** Utilizing QCEW census reports, Pachman proves that through late 2025, **32 to 33 states would have had net job losses** if not for the Medicaid care economy.
*   **The Fiscal Tail Risk:** The federal budget targets a **$1 trillion cut to Medicaid over 10 years**, beginning in **2027**. This represents a massive tail risk for employment, as states will be forced to defund the primary source of job creation.

> "Overwhelmingly this growth is coming from... social assistance... and home healthcare aids... 4,358,810 [workers] making an hourly median of $17.21... 32 to 33 states would have had negative job growth if it wasn't for Medicaid care economy... and in 2027 is when most of those cuts start."
> — *Eric Pachman*

#### 3. Core CPI Volatility Fallacy and OER Surrogacy
Pachman argues that "Core" CPI (excluding food and energy) is an arbitrary and outdated metric from the 1973 oil crisis. If the Fed stripped categories based on actual statistical volatility, they would exclude healthcare (highly volatile) and include groceries (stable). Furthermore, the single largest component of CPI, **Owners' Equivalent Rent (OER)** (26-27% weight), is a subjective survey number based on phone calls asking homeowners what they *think* they could rent their house for. Under the surface, **42% to 45%** of all 180 CPI sub-components have broken one standard deviation above their historical mean, showing that inflation is highly systemic rather than core-constrained.

> "Core was defined... coming out of the 1973 oil shock... If you were just doing it based on volatility, you would strip out all of healthcare and you would include all the groceries... [OER] is 30% [of CPI]... a fabricated surveyed number... [Meanwhile] 42 to 45% of all items... have breached one standard deviation over their historical mean."
> — *Eric Pachman*

#### 4. The Fruit Salad Analogy and Strait of Hormuz Chokepoints
Pachman explains the structural mismatch in U.S. energy production:
*   **The Fruit Salad Analogy:** Crude oil is not a single commodity; it is a blend of different hydrocarbons (fruit salad).
*   **The Mismatch:** U.S. shale produces ultra-light sweet crude (high in gasoline molecules). However, U.S. Gulf Coast refineries are complex heavy-crude systems designed for heavy-sour crude (high in diesel and jet fuel). Sending light shale crude to these heavy refineries breaks refinery yields, spiking **crack spreads** (margins).
*   **Chokepoint Crisis:** Geopolitical conflicts (Iran/Strait of Hormuz) disrupt global shipping lanes, forcing the wrong crude to the wrong refineries. Closing the Strait is the **"COVID of oil markets,"** creating supply blockages that take years to clear.
*   **Diesel Inventory Threat:** Distillate/diesel inventories are at multi-decade lows. Since diesel runs rail, trucking, and agricultural logistics, a supply shock instantly drives fuel surcharges that pass through to consumer retail prices. Retailers like Walmart, Target, and Costco are currently cutting prices to preserve volume, forcing them to absorb these rising fuel surcharges at the expense of corporate margins.

> "Think of oil as a fruit salad... U.S. shale... is very light oil... but Gulf Coast refineries... were designed to produce... heavier stuff [diesel]... When you close the Strait [of Hormuz], you disrupt all the flows... the wrong oil goes to the wrong place... We just created COVID for the oil markets... and diesel is more important because it feeds into almost all the other ones."
> — *Eric Pachman*

#### 5. The Single Income Stress Test
Historically, a middle-class family could own a home and raise children on a single income. Pachman's **Single Income Stress Test** shows how precarious the U.S. consumer is. If a family of four relies on a single median income, introducing just **$15,000 of surprise pre-tax expenses** (health bills, vehicle repair, etc.) turns the entire U.S. map red—plunging the family below the poverty line. This explains the structural necessity of dual-earner households and the record number of individuals holding multiple jobs.

> "If you wanted to do that now... earn a single median income... and you have $15,000 of surprise expense... the whole country turns red [under the poverty line]... and that's pre-tax too... That's why they have to work two jobs."
> — *Eric Pachman*

### IV. Portfolio Positioning & Action Items

1.  **Hedge Retail Margins:** Reduce exposure to low-cost retailers (Walmart, Target, Costco) that are vulnerable to diesel fuel surcharge cost pass-throughs as they subsidize pricing to maintain foot traffic.
2.  **Tactical Underweight on Consumer Discretionary:** Prepare for consumer demand contraction, as the Single Income Stress Test demonstrates that U.S. households have less than $15,000 in pre-tax financial breathing room.
3.  **Prepare for Medicaid Cut Tail Risk:** Underweight healthcare services, nursing home operators, and staffing agencies that rely on Medicaid funding, anticipating severe margin pressure starting in 2027.
4.  **Monitor Crack Spreads and Oil Logistics:** Monitor the 3-2-1 crack spread and diesel inventories relative to 40-year historical norms (not just 5-year averages) as leading indicators for logistics costs.
5.  **Utilize QCEW Over CES for True Employment Health:** Track the Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW) database rather than monthly Non-Farm Payroll surveys to get unmodeled, W2-verified state-level job data.
