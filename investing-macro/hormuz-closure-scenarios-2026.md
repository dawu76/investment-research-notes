## 1. Why the Strait of Hormuz Is Uniquely Critical

The Strait of Hormuz is a 33km-wide navigational chokepoint between Iran and Oman at the mouth of the Persian Gulf. It is functionally irreplaceable:

| Metric | Quantity | Global Share |
|---|---|---|
| Crude oil throughput | ~17–18 million barrels/day | ~20% of global supply |
| LNG throughput | ~100–110 million tonnes/year | ~20% of global LNG trade |
| Refined products | ~3–4 million barrels/day | ~10% of global refined products |
| Total energy value at $80/bbl | ~$500bn+/year in crude alone | — |

**Countries whose exports are entirely Hormuz-dependent**:
- Qatar: 100% of LNG exports (77 MTPA — world's largest LNG exporter)
- Kuwait: ~100% of oil exports
- Iraq (southern fields): ~80% of oil exports (~2.5 mb/d)
- UAE: ~90% of oil exports (~2.5 mb/d)
- Saudi Arabia: ~75% of oil exports (~6 mb/d via Hormuz; some via Yanbu Red Sea pipeline at max 5 mb/d)
- Bahrain: 100%
- Iran: 100% (already under sanctions but relevant to spare capacity math)

**There is no full substitute**. The two partial alternatives — Saudi Arabia's East-West Abqaiq-Yanbu pipeline (5 mb/d capacity, currently underutilized but not near capacity) and the Abu Dhabi Habshan-Fujairah pipeline (1.5 mb/d capacity) — together can reroute at most 6.5 mb/d vs 17–18 mb/d that normally flows. The shortfall is ~11 mb/d. For reference, the 1973 Arab oil embargo cut global supply by ~5 mb/d and caused a 400% price spike.

## 2. Global Baseline: What Is Being Disrupted

### Oil Market Structure

- Global oil consumption: ~102 mb/d
- Approximate Hormuz-dependent share at risk: ~11 mb/d (net of pipeline rerouting)
- Global Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) capacity: ~1.5 billion barrels across IEA members
  - US SPR: ~350 million barrels (reduced from 700mb pre-2022 drawdowns)
  - IEA total: ~1.2 billion barrels of public reserves; ~500–600 million more in industry stocks
  - **Draw rate**: IEA coordinated release at 60 mb/day = ~20 days of full offset; 30 mb/day = ~40 days of partial offset

### LNG Market Structure

- Global LNG demand: ~410 MTPA
- Qatar alone: 77 MTPA (19% of global trade)
- Qatar + UAE + Kuwait combined: ~95–100 MTPA
- LNG is **harder to stockpile than crude**: It requires cryogenic storage (-162°C) at specialized import terminals with fixed capacity. Most terminals hold 2–4 weeks of inventory

### Price Transmission

Oil prices are global and near-instantaneously transmitted. Gas/LNG prices are regionalized but interconnected via spot cargo diversion. The primary transmission channels:

1. **Oil price spike** → inflation in all oil-importing nations → central bank dilemma (hike to fight inflation vs. cut to fight recession)
2. **LNG price spike** → electricity price spike in gas-dependent power markets (Europe, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan)
3. **Supply chain disruption** → petrochemical feedstocks (plastics, fertilizers, chemicals) become scarce → food prices rise (fertilizer) → manufacturing costs rise (plastics, resins)
4. **Shipping insurance premiums** → war risk premiums make non-blocked routes more expensive even if physically available

## 3. Scenario A: +1 Week (Total ~2 Weeks Closure)

### Global Oil Market

**Price**: Brent crude likely at $100–130/bbl from pre-crisis ~$75–80. The initial spike could exceed $150 intraday on rumor/fear before settling.

**SPR response**: IEA would almost certainly coordinate an emergency release within days of closure announcement — historically triggered at $100+ sustained levels. The 2022 coordinated release of 60 mb/day was the largest in IEA history. Expect a similar or larger release announcement which psychologically caps the price spike but doesn't change physical reality.

**Supply shortfall**: At 2 weeks, physical inventories at most destinations remain adequate:
- OECD oil industry stocks: ~2.6–2.8 billion barrels (equivalent to ~60–65 days of consumption)
- Tankers in transit (floating storage): additional 1–2 weeks of buffer
- Net physical shortage: **minimal for 2 weeks** — this is primarily a price/fear event, not a physical event

**GDP impact (global)**: Every sustained $10/bbl increase in oil prices reduces global GDP by approximately 0.1–0.2%. A $40–50 spike at 2 weeks → -0.4–0.5% annualized GDP impact, mostly via inflation and consumer spending compression. **Transitory if resolved.**

**Equity markets**: Immediate sharp sell-off (S&P -3–5%), energy stocks spike (+15–25%), airlines/shipping/industrials sell off. Reversal likely fast upon credible closure resolution.

**Key risks at this stage**: Insurance and shipping companies start repricing war risk premiums on all Persian Gulf routes. Tanker operators may begin voluntary avoidance even for non-Hormuz routes (Suez, Red Sea), compounding the disruption.

## 4. Scenario B: +1 Month (Total ~5–6 Weeks Closure)

### The Physical Shortage Begins

At 5–6 weeks, the crisis transitions from psychological/price disruption to **genuine physical shortage** in several categories:

**Oil**: OECD physical stocks have drawn down ~300–400 million barrels via SPR release + demand destruction from high prices. This represents ~10–15% of buffer stock. Still technically adequate globally, but distribution becomes uneven — countries without SPR access (many EM nations) face real shortages.

**LNG — the more acute problem**: LNG storage globally holds 2–4 weeks of inventory. By 5–6 weeks:
- Asian LNG importing nations (Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, China) are drawing tanks to minimum operational levels
- European winter gas storage (if in winter months) faces accelerating drawdown
- Qatar LNG — 77 MTPA, ~210 million cubic meters/day — simply cannot be replaced. Australia, US, and other LNG exporters operate near full capacity and cannot meaningfully surge
- Spot LNG prices could reach $50–80/MMBtu (vs. normal $8–15 in Asia) — 5–10x normal levels

**Estimated global GDP impact**: -1.5–2.5% on annualized basis. This is approaching early-recession territory for most developed economies. Manufacturing PMIs fall below 45 in major economies; consumer confidence collapses.

### Inflation Surge

- CPI in oil-importing developed nations: +3–5% additional inflation within 60 days
- Food inflation: Fertilizer (natural gas feedstock) becomes scarce → nitrogen fertilizer prices spike 2–3x → food costs rise with 3–6 month lag
- Transport inflation: Freight costs (fuel surcharges) spike → every manufactured good becomes more expensive
- Central bank dilemma: **Stagflation** — inflation demands rate hikes, but recession risk demands cuts. The policy mistake risk is highest in this scenario.

### Financial Market Stress

- Sovereign credit spreads widen for oil-importing EM nations (India, Philippines, Thailand, Pakistan, Egypt)
- Airlines begin seeking government support or filing restructuring; many global airlines operate on 60–90 day fuel hedges — those hedges expire during this window
- Shipping rates on alternative routes (Cape of Good Hope rerouting adds 2–3 weeks to Middle East-Asia runs) spike 300–500%
- JPY strengthens sharply (Japan repatriates overseas assets to fund energy purchases — historical pattern)
- USD strengthens as risk-off + petrodollar recycling flows disrupted

### Geopolitical Acceleration

By week 5–6, every major power faces domestic political pressure to resolve the closure:
- US: Congressional pressure, gasoline prices at $5–6/gallon nationally, political crisis for any administration
- China: Manufacturing contraction, social stability concerns from energy cost spikes
- India: Severe economic distress (India imports ~85% of oil, large Middle East dependency)
- EU: Potential winter energy emergency (if October–March); possible emergency session invoking Article 122 TFEU (energy emergency powers)

## 5. Scenario C: +2 Months (Total ~3 Months Closure)

### The Structural Damage Threshold

Three months represents the point of **permanent economic damage** — not just disruption that reverses upon reopening.

### Oil and Gas

**Physical oil**: Global spare capacity has been exhausted. SPR releases have drawn down US reserves to ~200 million barrels (from 350mb) — politically very difficult to continue. OPEC+ non-Gulf producers (Nigeria, Libya, Venezuela, Russia) cannot meaningfully surge. US shale can add 200–300 kb/d within 90 days but this is marginal against an 11 mb/d shortfall.

**Brent price range**: $150–200/bbl in the base case. Under panic/financial stress: $200+ spikes are possible for short periods. For reference, the 2008 spike reached $147 without any physical shortage — purely on demand growth/speculation.

**LNG**: A 3-month LNG shortage without Qatar gas is categorically different from a temporary price spike:
- Power plants built around gas begin emergency fuel switching to coal, diesel, oil — creating cascading demand for alternative fuels
- Industrial gas users (petrochemicals, steel, glass, cement) face operational shutdowns
- Entire LNG-based fertilizer industries in importing nations halt production
- Some LNG import terminals in East Asia begin operating at structurally low storage levels, creating safety and operational risk

**Global GDP impact**: -3–5% annualized. This is a **global recession**, not a slowdown. Historical parallel is the 1973–1974 oil shock, which produced GDP contractions of 2–3% across developed economies and 15–25% in some EM oil importers.

### Supply Chain Collapse

The petrochemical supply chain unravels:
- **Plastics feedstocks** (ethylene, propylene — largely from naphtha or natural gas) become scarce → automotive, electronics, medical devices, packaging face shortages
- **Fertilizer**: Natural gas is the primary feedstock for ammonia/urea. With Qatari gas offline and European gas prices astronomical, global fertilizer production falls 15–25% → food crisis in 6–12 months (agricultural lag)
- **Shipping**: Cape of Good Hope rerouting adds 12–15 days to East-West trade routes. At 3 months, this becomes normalized — carriers restructure schedules, but port congestion at non-Hormuz ports creates new bottlenecks

### Geopolitical and Military Escalation Risk

At 3 months, the probability of military intervention to reopen the Strait rises substantially:
- The US Fifth Fleet (headquartered in Bahrain) would have been active throughout
- Coalition military pressure (US/UK/France/other NATO + Gulf states) to physically clear or neutralize the closure threat
- China faces an existential energy threat (~40% of oil imports via Hormuz) — their calculus on intervention or negotiation changes materially
- Saudi Arabia faces existential revenue pressure (~$400bn/year oil revenues at risk)

**The historical record**: No closure of the Strait has ever lasted more than a few days (even during the Iran-Iraq War tanker war period of 1984–1988). The 3-month scenario is unprecedented in the modern energy system.

### Financial System Stress

- Sovereign debt crises in oil-importing EM nations (Sri Lanka 2022 was a dress rehearsal — multiply by 20)
- Bank stress from energy company loan losses, airline bankruptcies, shipping company failures
- Potential for a credit event comparable to 2008 in its systemic nature — but inflation-driven rather than deflation-driven
- Fed/ECB/BOJ face impossible policy environment — emergency swap lines, potential G7 coordinated response

## 6. Taiwan: Deep Dive

Taiwan occupies a uniquely exposed position in a Hormuz closure scenario:

### Taiwan's Energy Dependency Profile

| Energy Source | Share of Power Generation | Import Dependency |
|---|---|---|
| LNG / Natural gas | ~35–40% | ~100% imported |
| Coal | ~35–40% | ~97% imported (Australia, Indonesia, US) |
| Nuclear | ~6–8% (being phased down) | Fuel partially imported |
| Renewables (solar/wind) | ~10–15% | Domestic generation |
| Oil (power + transport) | ~7–10% | ~98% imported |

**Critical vulnerability**: Taiwan imports approximately 10–12 million tonnes of LNG per year (2024 figures), making it one of the top 5 LNG importers globally. The Middle East (Qatar, Oman, UAE) supplies approximately **25–35% of Taiwan's LNG imports**. The remainder comes from Australia (~50%), US (~15%), and other sources.

Taiwan holds approximately **7–14 days** of LNG in tank storage at receiving terminals — critically thin vs. the disruption timelines modeled above.

**Taiwan's Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR)**: Taiwan maintains approximately 60–90 days of oil (crude + products) in combined government and private strategic reserves. This is compliant with IEA guidance for OECD-equivalent nations, though Taiwan is not an IEA member.

### Scenario A (+1 Week): Taiwan

**LNG**: Spot LNG prices spike to $30–50/MMBtu. Taiwan's state utility (Taipower) begins activating coal and oil backup power plants to conserve LNG in storage. No blackouts, but electricity prices rise 30–50% within weeks as emergency procurement occurs on the spot market.

**Oil**: Adequate from SPR + existing inventory; no supply interruption.

**Semiconductor manufacturing**: TSMC and other fabs run normally. However, corporate risk management begins assessing fuel reserves and backup power capacity. Generator fuel (diesel) becomes a procurement priority.

**Economic impact**: GDP headwind of -0.3–0.5% annualized; primarily through energy cost inflation and manufacturing cost increases.

---

### Scenario B (+1 Month): Taiwan

**LNG Crisis Begins**:
- Taiwan's LNG storage hits operational minimums (3–5 days of supply) within 3–4 weeks of no Qatari cargoes
- Emergency spot procurement at $40–70/MMBtu depletes foreign exchange reserves at an accelerating rate
- Some gas-fired power plants face fuel shortages even as coal capacity is maximized
- Taipower begins implementing off-peak industrial demand response protocols — asking large industrial users to voluntarily reduce consumption during peak hours

**Power Grid Stress**:
- Taiwan's peak summer electricity demand is ~37–38 GW; gas-fired capacity is ~15–18 GW
- If 30–40% of gas capacity goes offline due to fuel shortage, available generation falls to ~28–32 GW — tight but manageable *if* demand side responds
- Rolling brownouts become probable in weeks 4–6 if no emergency LNG cargo can be secured

**Semiconductor fabs — the critical node**:
TSMC operates over 10 major fabs in Taiwan. Key facts:
- A semiconductor fab cannot tolerate even momentary power interruptions — microprocessors are destroyed mid-fabrication
- Each fab has multiple layers of backup power: UPS (seconds), diesel generators (hours to days), connection to dedicated power supply infrastructure
- TSMC's self-generation capacity (natural gas + some fuel oil) provides short-term buffer
- However, if gas supply is interrupted for weeks, even TSMC's onsite reserves become inadequate
- **The value at risk**: TSMC produces ~$75–80bn in revenue annually from Taiwan fabs. A single month of reduced output would cost the global semiconductor supply chain $6–8bn in direct value + cascading downstream losses (Apple, Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm all at risk of wafer supply shortage)

**Geopolitical dimension**: Any power instability at TSMC creates an immediate global supply chain crisis. The US, Japan, and South Korea have enormous incentive to help Taiwan secure emergency LNG supply — potentially via naval escort of LNG tankers, emergency diplomatic channels with Qatar, or diversion of Australian/US cargoes.

**Economic impact on Taiwan**: GDP contraction of -2–4% annualized; NT dollar under pressure (energy import costs spike foreign exchange outflows); inflation surge of 5–8%; Taipower faces potential insolvency without government backstop (was already running losses pre-crisis).

---

### Scenario C (+2 Months): Taiwan

**Energy emergency declared**: The Executive Yuan (cabinet) would invoke emergency powers under the Energy Administration Act and the Electricity Act to mandate rationing.

**LNG**: Taiwan is now buying any available LNG globally at essentially any price. Spot prices at $60–100/MMBtu mean Taiwan's annual LNG import bill triples or quadruples — from ~$15bn/year to $45–60bn+ annualized. Taiwan's foreign exchange reserves (~$570bn, one of the world's largest relative to GDP) provide the financial capacity to outbid most competitors, but the physical supply simply may not exist.

**Power grid**: Multiple gas-fired plants are offline. Taiwan is operating at 70–80% of normal generation capacity. Structured rationing is in effect.

**Industrial output**:
- Electronics/semiconductor: Operating at reduced capacity but prioritized; Taipower provides dedicated supply circuits
- Steel, cement, glass, chemicals: Major curtailment (30–50% reduction)
- SME manufacturing: Severe disruption; many factories running limited shifts or shutting temporarily
- Services sector: Reduced hours, some commercial closures

**GDP impact on Taiwan**: -6–10% annualized GDP contraction. This is a severe recession by any measure. Taiwan's export-dependent economy (exports = ~70% of GDP) faces dual headwind: reduced output capacity + global demand destruction (key customers like Apple, Nvidia reducing orders as their own costs rise and consumers pull back).

## 7. Energy Rationing: Probability, Priority Structure, and Industrial Hierarchy

### Probability of Formal Rationing by Duration

| Duration | Taiwan Rationing Probability | US Rationing Probability | EU Rationing Probability | Japan/Korea Rationing Probability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| +1 week | 5% (voluntary demand response only) | <1% | <1% | 5% |
| +1 month | 50–65% (partial/sector-specific) | 10–15% | 25–35% | 45–55% |
| +2 months | 90–95% (formal economy-wide) | 35–50% | 70–80% | 80–90% |

*Note: US rationing probability is lower due to domestic production (~13 mb/d) and natural gas self-sufficiency.*

### The Priority Hierarchy for Energy Rationing

All major energy-importing economies follow a broadly similar priority structure, established through decades of emergency planning (Japan's post-Fukushima protocols, EU Gas Regulation 2017/1938, Taiwan's own energy emergency plans). The hierarchy from highest to lowest protected:

**Tier 1 — Non-Negotiable (Never Curtailed)**
- Hospitals and healthcare facilities
- Emergency services: police, fire, ambulance
- Military and national security infrastructure
- Water treatment and sewage (public health imperative)
- Telecommunications and internet backbone
- Financial system infrastructure (exchanges, payment systems, central banks)
- Air traffic control and aviation safety

**Tier 2 — Critical National Infrastructure**
- Nuclear power plant cooling and safety systems (non-negotiable regardless of fuel source)
- Fuel distribution infrastructure (refinery and pipeline operations — needed to produce the fuel that runs everything else)
- Food cold chain (refrigerated warehouses, distribution centers)
- Mass transit (trains, metro systems — enables workforce to function)
- Government operations at essential level

**Tier 3 — Economically Prioritized Industry**

This is where it gets *politically and economically contentious*. In Taiwan's case, the prioritized industries would be:

- **Semiconductors / TSMC / advanced electronics** — Formally designated as strategically critical. Taiwan's entire national economic identity is bound up with TSMC. The government would guarantee power supply to semiconductor fabs even if it means rolling blackouts elsewhere. This is not controversial in Taiwan — it is national policy.
- **Export manufacturing (broadly)** — Taiwan's foreign exchange earnings depend on exports. Maintaining some export manufacturing protects NT dollar and funding capacity
- **Pharmaceutical manufacturing** — Drug supply chain
- **Food processing and agriculture** — Pumping stations for irrigation, food production facilities
- **Petrochemicals (essential feedstocks only)** — Enough to maintain basic plastics and chemical production for critical downstream users

**Tier 4 — Commercial and Services (Rationed)**
- Retail (reduced hours, temperature restrictions on HVAC)
- Hospitality (hotels, restaurants — significant energy users)
- Office buildings (reduced lighting, HVAC setpoints mandated)
- Entertainment venues
- Non-essential government buildings

**Tier 5 — Residential (Last to be Formally Rationed, but Informally Pressured)**
- Residential electricity is politically the most sensitive to ration
- Governments historically prefer *price signals* (extremely high electricity bills) over *hard rationing* for residential users
- However, at 2+ months, formal rolling blackout schedules (e.g., 4 hours off per district per day) become likely
- Hot water rationing (reduced hours for electric hot water heating) is typically among the first household-level measures
- Air conditioning mandates (thermostat setpoints legally mandated) in summer months

### Specific Industrial Curtailment Detail

**Steel**: Energy-intensive (electric arc furnaces use ~500 kWh/tonne). Would face 30–50% production curtailment in Scenario B, 50–70% in Scenario C. Taiwan's steel industry (China Steel Corporation) would likely negotiate a formal allocation agreement with Taipower.

**Cement and Glass**: Among the most energy-intensive industries (cement kilns operate at 1,400–1,500°C). Extremely difficult to restart after cold shutdown (refractory lining damage). These industries would likely negotiate continuous operation at reduced rates rather than stop-start, which would be more costly. Probably 20–30% curtailment.

**Petrochemicals (Formosa Plastics, etc.)**: Naphtha cracking requires continuous high-temperature operation. Partial curtailment (20–40%) based on downstream priority (medical plastics vs. packaging).

**Semiconductor fabs**: Protected but not immune. TSMC would likely implement:
- Reduce less-critical R&D and pilot line operations
- Prioritize most advanced nodes (3nm, 5nm) that have highest economic value per kWh
- Run mature nodes (28nm, 40nm+) at reduced capacity if power allocation requires it

**Data centers**: Taiwan has significant data center capacity. Non-critical data workloads (streaming, consumer apps) would be requested to shift to off-peak or reduce capacity. Mission-critical cloud/financial data centers protected under Tier 1/2.

## 8. Impact on Everyday People

### The Price Channel — Universal and Immediate

Regardless of where someone lives, the first impact is price:

**Gasoline/transport**: In the US, gasoline price increases of $1–2/gallon in Scenario A, $2–3/gallon in Scenario B, $3–5/gallon in Scenario C. For a household driving 15,000 miles/year at 25mpg (600 gallons/year), this represents:
- Scenario A: +$600–1,200/year in fuel costs
- Scenario B: +$1,200–1,800/year
- Scenario C: +$1,800–3,000/year

**Home heating/cooling**: Natural gas bills double in Scenario B, triple+ in Scenario C. For a typical household spending $1,500/year on gas heat, this is +$1,500–3,000 in additional cost.

**Electricity**: Even nations with less gas-dependent grids see electricity price increases as gas power sets the marginal price. Expect 30–60% electricity bill increases in Scenario B, 50–100%+ in Scenario C.

**Food**: The fertilizer → food price transmission has a 3–6 month lag (crop cycle dependent). Scenario A sees minimal food impact. Scenario B sees early commodity price rises (futures markets). Scenario C sees real food cost increases of 15–30% at the household level within 6 months.

**Everything else**: Products made with plastics (packaging, appliances, electronics, automotive parts), chemicals, or transported by road or air become more expensive. This is a broad-based cost-of-living shock.

### Taiwan Everyday People — Specific Impacts

**Scenario A (+1 week)**:
- Electricity bills up 20–30% (Taipower passes through spot procurement costs)
- Gasoline up ~NT$5–8/liter
- Normal daily life continues; people notice prices but behavior unchanged
- Some voluntary energy conservation messaging from government

**Scenario B (+1 month)**:
- Electricity bills up 50–80%; some businesses pass costs to consumers
- Possible rolling demand-response periods (not formal blackouts but requests to avoid peak usage 14:00–20:00)
- Businesses begin adjusting hours; some factories go to shortened shifts
- Public transit becomes more attractive (price and crowd increases)
- Government issues conservation guidance: mandated AC setpoints (26°C minimum), no decorative lighting, businesses close earlier
- Psychological anxiety spike — people begin stockpiling candles, portable power banks, small generators
- Grocery prices begin rising (5–10% on packaged goods)

**Scenario C (+2 months)**:
- **Formal rolling blackouts** (likely 3–6 hours per district, rotating schedule announced weekly)
- Families with medical equipment (home oxygen, dialysis) receive exemptions and priority — requires registration with Taipower
- Work-from-home becomes impossible for hours each day; businesses adapt with shifted hours
- Air conditioning becomes a luxury — health risk for elderly during summer months
- School schedules adjusted around power availability (Taiwan's climate in summer makes classrooms without AC dangerous for sustained learning)
- Mental health impact: Sustained uncertainty, financial stress, loss of normal routines
- Small businesses (restaurants, retail) face existential crisis — refrigeration intermittency, inability to operate normal hours
- ATMs and point-of-sale systems may have intermittent outages; cash demand spikes
- Social stress increases: residents blame government, energy companies; political ramifications for ruling party
- **Youth and worker anxiety**: Concern about TSMC job security, export industry layoffs → economic anxiety compounds energy anxiety

### Historical Analog: Japan Post-Fukushima (2011)

The closest modern parallel to a major industrial nation implementing energy rationing is Japan after the March 2011 Fukushima disaster, which took offline ~30% of Japan's electricity generation capacity almost overnight:

- Large industrial users (steel, auto, electronics) negotiated 15–25% reduction agreements with TEPCO
- Households asked to reduce consumption by 15%; compliance was remarkable (Japanese cultural norm)
- Office buildings mandated: no escalators, minimal lighting, 28°C AC setpoints
- Summer 2011: No formal blackouts in Tokyo, but only because of extraordinary voluntary conservation
- GDP impact: -0.5% in 2011, followed by partial recovery
- Lesson: **Voluntary conservation in high-social-cohesion societies can avoid formal rationing** — relevant for Taiwan and Japan more than for the US or Europe

## 9. Alternative Routing and Mitigation

### What Can Partially Compensate

**Saudi East-West Pipeline (Petro Rabigh/Yanbu)**:
- Capacity: ~5 mb/d (operating at ~2–3 mb/d normally)
- Can theoretically add 2–3 mb/d of incremental non-Hormuz oil flow
- Requires Saudi Aramco operational decisions and sufficient tanker availability at Red Sea terminals
- Covers ~20–25% of the shortfall

**ADNOC Habshan-Fujairah Pipeline (UAE)**:
- Capacity: 1.5 mb/d
- Already used for some Abu Dhabi exports
- Adds marginal relief

**Cape of Good Hope routing for LNG**:
- LNG tankers can reroute around Africa — adds 10–15 days to Middle East-Asia runs
- Reduces effective tanker fleet capacity (ships spend more time in transit)
- Does not add supply — just reroutes existing production, but with delay
- The additional transit time of 10–15 days represents effectively 5–10% reduction in global LNG fleet capacity

**US LNG surge**:
- US LNG nameplate capacity: ~13–14 bcf/day (after recent expansions)
- Currently operating near full rates
- No meaningful surge available in 30–60 day timeframe
- New LNG export capacity: 2–3 year construction lead time

**Demand destruction (the painful self-regulating mechanism)**:
- High prices force behavioral change
- Industry curtails voluntarily under economic pressure before formal rationing
- Substitution: Where oil/gas is flexible (power generation), utilities switch to coal, nuclear, or renewables
- Net effect: 1–2 mb/d of demand reduction naturally at $120+/bbl; 3–4 mb/d at $150+/bbl

### What Cannot Compensate

- The Qatar LNG volume (77 MTPA = ~210 mcm/day). Australia and the US are operating at or near capacity. There is no 77 MTPA of spare LNG supply anywhere in the world.
- The Iraq southern oil fields (~2.5 mb/d) are entirely Hormuz-dependent with no alternative exit route
- Kuwait is entirely landlocked to the Persian Gulf — no alternative pipeline routes

## 10. Investment Implications

### Immediate (Scenario A)

| Asset Class | Direction | Magnitude |
|---|---|---|
| Crude oil (Brent) | ↑ | +50–70% from pre-crisis |
| Natural gas / LNG spot | ↑ | +200–400% in Asia |
| US energy equities (XLE) | ↑ | +20–35% |
| Airlines (UAL, DAL, etc.) | ↓ | -15–25% |
| Chemical / plastics manufacturers | ↓ | -10–15% |
| Shipping (non-tanker) | ↓ | -8–12% (higher costs) |
| Tankers (VLCC operators) | ↑ | +20–40% (war risk premiums, rerouting demand) |
| Defense contractors | ↑ | +10–20% |
| Gold | ↑ | +5–10% (geopolitical premium) |
| USD | ↑ | +2–5% vs. EM currencies |
| JPY | ↑ | +3–5% (Japan repatriation) |
| S&P 500 | ↓ | -5–8% |
| Taiwan equities (TWSE) | ↓ | -8–15% |
| TSMC ADR | ↓ | -10–20% |

### Extended (Scenarios B and C Compounding)

- **VLCC tanker operators**: One of the few asset classes that benefits throughout — war risk premiums, rerouting demand, and elevated rates persist
- **US domestic energy (shale, LNG)**: Sustained earnings windfall; accelerated capex to maximize production
- **Defense sector**: Elevated geopolitical risk premium for the duration
- **Gold**: Transition from modest geopolitical premium to full safe-haven bid as recession risk rises
- **Bonds (Treasuries)**: Stagflation dilemma — initially sell off (inflation), then potentially rally as recession risk rises (demand destruction wins)
- **TSMC / semiconductor supply chain**: The most critical monitoring position. A sustained Hormuz closure that materially impacts TSMC output would be the largest supply chain shock to the global tech sector since COVID-19

### The Asymmetric Trade

The most interesting asymmetric trade in a Hormuz closure scenario:

**Long VLCC tankers + Short airlines** as a spread trade captures the core supply chain disruption: tanker rates spike as ships reroute and war premiums inflate, while airlines face fuel cost destruction with limited ability to pass costs through (demand elasticity + competition). This spread has historically been among the most reliable expressions of oil supply shock scenarios.

**Long energy infrastructure (pipelines, LNG terminals) outside the Persian Gulf** benefits from the long-term structural signal: every major energy importer accelerates diversification investment after a Hormuz event, regardless of outcome. Australian LNG terminal operators, US Gulf Coast LNG exporters, and non-Hormuz pipeline operators see sustained investment and demand.
