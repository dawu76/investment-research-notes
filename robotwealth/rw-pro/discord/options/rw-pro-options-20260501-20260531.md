## 1. Merger arbitrage options: a specialist's game

FullMetal37! asked a very specific, well-reasoned question: with eBay rumored to be bought out at $125, is selling the 125 or 130 calls a good trade, on the logic that if the deal fails the stock drops (call sellers win) and if it succeeds the stock won't trade above $125 anyway (call sellers also win)?

Euan's answer was a clear, credibility-first caution rather than a direct yes/no: **merger trading is genuinely a specialist discipline, and he explicitly said he isn't one of those specialists.** Two concrete risks he flagged that the "heads I win, tails I win" framing misses:
- There could be a bidding war, which breaks the assumption that the stock is capped at the deal price.
- If the acquisition is paid fully or partly in stock (rather than cash), the options don't simply expire worthless or get closed out — they convert into options on the new composite company, which changes the entire risk picture.

His closing line is the real takeaway: *"the details are everything."*

**Sentiment:** Appropriately humble — Euan declining to overstate his expertise in a specialist niche is itself useful guidance (don't treat general options intuition as sufficient for merger-arb-specific risk).

**Pull quotes:**
- Euan: *"The most important takeaway from what I'm about to write is that merger trading is very much a specialist game and I am not one of those specialists."*
- Euan: *"if the acquisition is fully or partly in stock the options don't just go away. they get converted to options on the new composite company. the details are everything"*

## 2. VIX options as a long-vol carry position: mechanics

William asked Euan to elaborate on a short-ATM-VIX-put trade mentioned in a prior AMA: specific DTE targeting, whether it's actively managed or held to expiration, and whether sizing is VIX-level-dependent.

Euan's answer, consistent with how he frames things elsewhere (see June's vol-book-club-chat digest, section 1), was careful to downgrade expectations first: **this isn't really a "strategy," just a way to be aware of positive-carry long VIX exposure with intentionally sloppy delta management.** The concrete mechanics:
- Only monthly VIX option expirations have real liquidity — no way to get more granular than that.
- He targets a rolling window between 2 weeks and 2 months of duration, rolling back periodically to maintain it rather than holding to expiration (avoiding unnecessary jump risk near settlement).
- Sizing is driven purely by how many deltas of long-VIX exposure he wants — the absolute level of VIX itself isn't a sizing input, since he may still want long deltas even when VIX is low.

**Sentiment:** Practical and deliberately unglamorous — Euan resists letting the trade be over-systematized, treating it as a flexible tool rather than a rules-based strategy.

**Pull quotes:**
- Euan: *"Don't read too much into this idea. It isn't a 'strategy' so much as a thing to be aware of... The only real liquidity in VIX options is in monthly expirations. So I trade those and try to stay between 2 months and 2 weeks."*
- Euan: *"My only sizing consideration is how many deltas I need... So the level of the VIX isn't a consideration"*

## 3. Should VIX options double as a futures-calendar hedge?

A direct follow-up from MidKnight: could the VIX options position from section 2 also serve as a pseudo-hedge for futures calendar positions, similar to weighting the long side of a UVXY/VXZ trade more heavily toward VXZ?

Euan's answer was a clear caution rather than encouragement: **"you could. But I would think twice."** The core risk is that layering a hedging objective onto a position whose whole point is harvesting a risk premium tends to hedge away the very premium you're trying to collect — the two goals pull against each other. His practical recommendation before attempting this: run simulations first with a few defined mechanical rules to see whether the combined position actually looks sensible, rather than reasoning about it qualitatively.

**Sentiment:** Cautionary — a good example of Euan flagging an intuitively appealing idea (getting "extra use" out of an existing position) as having a real, non-obvious cost.

**Pull quotes:**
- Euan: *"You could. But I would think twice. The danger is that you would hedge away the risk premium you want to collect."*

## 5. Dealer gamma / "call wall" skepticism: a full market-structure teardown

The month's most substantial single thread. halves shared a VolSignals thread and video (framed dramatically — "something wicked this way comes," "THIS IS A BIGGER PROBLEM") and asked Euan directly whether dealer-gamma/call-wall effects are a real problem or clickbait.

Euan's opening line set the tone: *"I don't know of any serious options traders who think 'dealer gamma' is anything other than click bait"* — with three compact reasons (you can't actually know dealer positioning, you don't know how they'll hedge, and dealers themselves understand the feedback dynamics better than any outside observer). When pressed by halves for more depth given data cited from UBS/BofA/CBOE, Euan gave a genuinely rich, historically-grounded explanation:

- **Feedback effects are real and old news to market makers.** Historically, floor traders would front-run their own hedges near expiration (when gamma concentrates) — e.g., pre-selling into an anticipated drop rather than waiting to be forced to sell, which paradoxically left them exposed more to *upside* risk than the naive "dealers must chase the move" story would suggest. Pit culture also self-policed this: traders who broke the informal non-panic accord got frozen out of future good trades.
- **The market structure that created this effect has fundamentally changed.** In the old single-trader/small-group era, positions were hedged stock-by-stock, so being exposed in one name meant being genuinely exposed. Today, five or so huge firms dominate volume and hedge everything as a portfolio — options get bucketed into dispersion books, stocks into stat-arb books — which spreads and dilutes the feedback effect considerably. Euan's framing: **no one is more aware of this issue than market makers themselves**, who've had quants working on mitigating it for decades.
- **The effect is real but small.** He cited Neil Pearson's research using 100%-accurate internal position data from an actual market-making firm: even in extreme cases, daily SPX vol was distorted by only a few vol points from what it would otherwise be.
- **Even if you could measure it, exploiting it is very hard.** You'd need to reliably estimate market maker positioning — genuinely difficult, since buys-on-bid/sells-on-offer and trade-initiator data don't reliably indicate dealer positioning, especially for the fragmented SPX complex (SPX, SP500, XSP, and SPY all need to be tracked and aggregated together).
- **Even a real, measurable effect is usually dwarfed by other noise.** His blunt closer: "Trump saying something dumb will move vol far more than any 'call wall.'" His overall verdict: a genuinely hard problem that yields a genuinely small edge, useful mainly in extreme cases rather than as a everyday trading signal.

He also referenced hands-on experience building similar market-maker-position-reconstruction heuristics for Amberdata in crypto (BTC on Deribit) — noting it was a comparatively *easier* problem than equities despite still requiring ~15 heuristics — as a real-world data point for how hard this class of problem is even in a simpler market structure.

**Sentiment:** Thorough, technically rich, and ultimately dismissive of the popularized "dealer gamma/call wall" narrative as a tradeable signal — while being fair to the *underlying* feedback mechanism as a real, historically well-understood phenomenon.

**Pull quotes:**
- Euan: *"I don't know of any serious options traders who think 'dealer gamma' is anything other than click bait: You can't know dealers gamma. You don't know how they will hedge. They understand the feedback effects better than anyone"*
- Euan: *"Neil Pearson has done a bunch of studies on this. He used the internal data set from a market making firm. He had 100% accurate position data. And he found that in extreme cases, the daily SPX vol was distorted by a few vols from what it would be otherwise."*

## Overall read

May was a lower-volume but unusually deep month for #options, and the throughline across almost every thread is **Euan repeatedly resisting the temptation to overstate an edge or oversimplify a mechanism** — whether that's merger arbitrage (section 1), dealer gamma (section 5), or the weekend-risk trade (section 4). His consistent pattern: acknowledge what's genuinely knowable, name the specific mechanism at play, and then honestly separate "is this real" from "is this worth trading given the payoff asymmetry."

1. **The dealer-gamma/call-wall teardown (section 5) is the standout piece of content this month** — a rare, fully worked-through explanation of both the historical origin of the feedback effect and why it's structurally diluted in today's market, grounded in real academic research (Neil Pearson) rather than popular finance-Twitter narratives. Worth referencing any time a "call wall" thread resurfaces.
2. **The small-account webinar thread (section 6) is the most actionable forward-looking item** — watch for the actual session and compare its content against the well-formed question list the community assembled here (account minimums, strategy selection, time-zone/availability constraints, position sizing, loss discipline).
3. **Two live-vol trades (VIX-as-carry, weekend-risk short straddle) both got the same treatment from Euan**: real but modest, asymmetry-favorable edges that he's willing to run despite not fully believing the popular narrative behind them — a useful template for evaluating other "everyone says X" trades that surface in the community.
4. **The time-zone/availability constraint raised independently by two members (liz, MidKnight) is a genuinely underweighted risk factor** in most strategy discussions — worth flagging explicitly in any future strategy write-up aimed at a global audience, since it's already caused at least one member to rule out an entire strategy category.
