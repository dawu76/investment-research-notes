## 7. Signal-combination methodology and the "Friday rebalancing effect" investigation

The most technically dense thread of the month, spanning Feb 18–19:

- Dan shared an X article arguing against combining signals into a single composite, favoring keeping strategies separate. **-k gave the most rigorous pushback**, breaking the debate into three distinct things people conflate: (1) combining signals before ranking, (2) ranking on signals independently, (3) netting positions after independently trading each signal — arguing you should almost never do (3) given turnover costs, and that the article's own evidence (Sharpe 0.157 vs 0.168) wasn't statistically significant on a single signal-pair example.
- -k then posed a live puzzle to the group (tagging Dan, mm, bdkoepke, robotkris): a "Friday rebalancing effect" where rebalancing weekly on Fridays (vs. Mondays or daily) produced meaningfully higher before-cost Sharpe ratios on the same underlying signal:
  - `daily_rebal_1day_pred: 2.81`
  - `daily_rebal_5day_pred: 1.64`
  - `friday_rebal_5day_pred: 2.54`
  - `monday_rebal_5day_pred: 1.73`
- The group worked through possible explanations: mm asked whether it survived `shift(1)`/`shift(2)` robustness checks (-k confirmed it did survive `shift(2)`, at 5.54% CAGR / 2.13% vol vs. 3.87%/2.22%) and whether the effect was vol-related rather than return-related. **robotkris's specific hypothesis** — a weekend risk-premium effect — became the leading explanation the group converged toward, with mm adding that Friday's forecast window always cleanly spans a full trading week (Mon-Fri) in a way midweek rebalancing doesn't.
- bdkoepke and -k also dug into fundamentals data-lag hygiene as a possible confound (EODHD vs. CRSP, point-in-time vs. revised fundamentals, filing-date lag) — -k noted the effect was a "sanity check" finding rather than something he was relying on for the final backtest.

**Sentiment:** High-quality, genuinely collaborative quant debugging — no one claimed certainty, but the group converged on a plausible weekend-risk-premium explanation via robotkris's prompt.

**Pull quotes:**
- robotkris: *"Could it be a weekend risk premium effect?"*
- -k: *"it's just spooky to see (before-cost) sharpes of daily_rebal_1day_pred: 2.81, daily_rebal_5day_pred: 1.64, friday_rebal_5day_pred: 2.54, monday_rebal_5day_pred: 1.73 — which is what made me ask"*
- -k: *"you should almost never do [netting after independent trading] — the only reason to do that would be ease of implementation and pnl attribution, but imo the costs outweigh the benefit if your turnover is even reasonably high"*

- **ASlan gave the most complete answer**: an extreme example (100x leverage decaying to zero every other day from vol drag alone) illustrates that leveraged ETFs are inherently "betting above Kelly" even at small portfolio allocations, since the ETF itself embeds excess leverage regardless of your sizing. But he noted the flip side — as long as your *portfolio* vol stays under full Kelly, leveraged products can make sense to hit a vol target you couldn't otherwise reach with unlevered instruments and limited capital. deal_me_in checked and found full Kelly on his positions was "like 2x."

## 12. The AI/LLM reliability and job-security mega-thread (largest thread of the month)

Sparked by a robotkris substack article ("More of the Disease, Faster") shared alongside the leveraged-ETF discussion, this became the month's biggest single conversation, running most of Feb 28:

- MattyIce, hac, NP, and MidKnight all independently converged on the same complaint: LLMs are being hyped as capable of "everything everywhere better than humans," but their actual experience is constant supervision, "pedestrian errors," and having to fix the LLM's output themselves rather than trust it — even on ostensibly simple tasks like Excel formulas.
- macrosteaks contributed a sharper diagnosis of *why*: a Kaggle poker tournament exposed models regurgitating bad "conventional wisdom" trading advice, and he argued this reflects a training-data problem specific to domains (like trading) where the public corpus is mostly low-quality — unlike, say, open-source code, which benefits from a large, peer-reviewed, self-correcting corpus (Stack Overflow, code review) that trading advice simply doesn't have.
- **robotkris gave the most developed response**, building directly on macrosteaks's framing with a concrete experiment: he built a vector store from TLAQ Bootcamp material and explicitly instructed an LLM to only answer using that corpus — it still confidently repeated bad conventional wisdom (e.g., treating "validate on an out-of-sample backtest" as standard practice when it isn't). His conclusion:
  - The model's priors are so deeply baked in that they override even deliberately curated, higher-quality context — it's not simple garbage-in-garbage-out, it's that the garbage is embedded deeply enough to survive correction.
  - This is specifically why LLMs are strong at coding (huge, self-correcting corpus) but weak at trading advice (tiny corpus, buried under noise, no correction mechanism) — the model can't tell the difference in confidence level between the two domains even though the underlying data quality is wildly different.
  - He also named the "productivity pressure" of AI-speed work as a real, visceral phenomenon he feels himself, drawing a parallel to his own past experience under TDD-mandated engineering practices that felt slow in the moment but paid off longer-term.
- The thread widened into two adjacent debates: **CLI/agentic coding tools vs. IDE-chat-helper tools** (Dan uncomfortable trusting an agent he doesn't watch write code line-by-line; Ben and MidKnight more comfortable using LLMs as a *speed-up* on code they can already read and verify, rather than a replacement for understanding it), and **AI as a scapegoat for layoffs** — akhan raised anxiety around Block's reported 50% staff cut, which Rachit reframed as more likely "free money fueled fattening of org structures" using AI as a convenient growth-story cover rather than genuine AI-driven efficiency, a read hac and TimExcellent both endorsed based on secondhand insider accounts of messy, unremarkable enterprise IT environments underneath the AI narrative.

**Sentiment:** Genuinely anxious but constructive — a real generational/professional-identity conversation (not just AI-skeptic venting), with robotkris's structured framing pulling it toward a substantive shared conclusion: AI is a productivity multiplier for *verifiable* work, not a replacement for domain judgment, and the group is broadly skeptical that AI is the true driver behind the current wave of tech layoffs.

**Pull quotes:**
- robotkris: *"The conventional wisdom is so baked into the model's priors that it comes through even when you deliberately feed it better material. So it's not just garbage in, garbage out… it's that the garbage is so deeply embedded that it overrides the good stuff you give it."*
- robotkris: *"I think that's why LLMs are so good at coding but surprisingly bad at trading advice. The corpus of good code is enormous, and it's self-correcting. Stack Overflow answers get ruthlessly peer-reviewed, open source code gets tested and iterated on by thousands of people. The signal-to-noise ratio is high. The corpus of good trading content is tiny by comparison, and it's buried under a mountain of noise and grift with no real correction mechanism."*
- Rachit: *"I don't think that is an AI thing as much of a free money fueled fattening of org structures under the guise of 'growth'. 'AI efficiency gains' just make a better story than 'we have been running the company like shit'"*
