# Jack Schwager on Timeless Lessons from the New Wave of Elite Traders

Detailed overview and analysis of the *Excess Returns* podcast featuring **Jack Schwager**, author of the legendary *Market Wizards* series, hosted by Matt Ziegler. The episode was released on July 16, 2026.

---

### I. Executive Summary
Jack Schwager discusses his newest book, *Market Wizards: The Next Generation*, which profiles the youngest, most risk-adjusted cohort of traders in the series' 38-year history. A notable influence on this generation is video gaming, with several traders citing gaming as key to developing their rapid pattern recognition and execution reflexes. Schwager outlines his vetting process, requiring at least a 10-year track record and either massive absolute returns from small starting sums or near-impossible risk-adjusted metrics (such as Sharpe equivalents of 10+ or zero losing months). He profiles key wizards: **Simon Rousseau** (who turned $40k into $500M but narrowly escaped bankruptcy on a $50M Carvana short squeeze), **Kristjan Kullamägi** (who blew up multiple accounts as a Nasdaq security guard before turning $5k into $100M+), **Phil Goetz** (an option seller with one minor down year in 20 years who hedged tariff risk in seconds), **Pan Daisy** (who beats bots in merger arbitrage by analyzing obscure state filings), and **Lance Breitstein** (who studied videotapes of his trading on weekends). Finally, Schwager explores trading psychology, noting that successful trading requires overriding basic human instincts.

### III. Detailed Key Takeaways

#### 1. The Video Game Reflex and Sharpe Ratio Critiques
Schwager notes a unique characteristic of this new cohort: video gaming. Unlike prior generations who had no access to computers, these traders grew up as video game fanatics. The skills developed—fast-paced pattern recognition, hand-eye coordination, and split-second risk execution—are highly compatible with short-term trading.
*   **Critique of Sharpe:** Schwager rejects the traditional Sharpe Ratio because it treats upside volatility (massive winning months) as risk in the denominator. He prefers downside-only measures (like Sortino or Gain-to-Pain ratios) that only penalize actual losses.

> "Video playing video games was... an influence... they were real fanatics about video games and... the skills were compatible... [And] I have a bias against the Sharpe... it uses volatility as a risk measure... it doesn't make a difference if you make 25% or lose 25%."
> — *Jack Schwager*

#### 2. Simon Rousseau and the Carvana Lesson
Under the pseudonym **Simon Rousseau**, Schwager profiles a former classical conservatory musician driven by an obsession with "mastery." After blowing up multiple accounts, a peer lent him $50,000, which he quickly grew and paid back, leaving him with $40,000 of capital. He grew this account to **$500 million in 9 years**. To protect his identity, Rousseau had his lawyers draft a 9-page NDA.
*   **The Carvana Squeeze:** Despite his success, Rousseau broke his own rules and went short Carvana, ignoring his stop loss. He took a **$50 million loss** before covering. Had he held on stubbornly, the short squeeze would have completely bankrupt his $200M net worth. He subsequently hard-coded automated risk rules to lock himself out of trades when down $15M to protect himself against his own impulses.

> "Simon Rousseau... turned $40k into half a billion... He went short Carvana... didn't honor his own stop... took a 50 million dollar loss... Had he stayed short and been stubborn, that short would have been enough to wipe out his whole 200 million. He would have been flat broke again."
> — *Jack Schwager*

#### 3. Kristjan Kullamägi: The Swedish Security Guard
Kristjan Kullamägi worked night shifts as a security guard at Stockholm's Nasdaq affiliate, using his free time to study charts. He blew up 3 or 4 consecutive accounts of $5,000. He remained confident because each blow-up took longer (first took 2 months, second took 6 months, third took over a year), which he recognized as progress.
*   **The $100M Peak:** Kullamägi turned $5,000 into **$100 million+** by late 2022. However, he was leveraged long during a market peak, and lost over half his net worth over several months by stubbornly trading his methodology in a hostile environment. He recovered, bought a boat, and now trades semi-actively.

> "[Kullamägi] blew up three times... Most people would look at it and say 'I blew up three times.' No, he looks at it: 'It's taking me longer and longer to blow up. Hence, I'm improving.' ... he built from 5,000 to over 100 million."
> — *Jack Schwager*

#### 4. Phil Goetz and Pan Daisy: Negative Asymmetry Edges
Schwager notes that this is the first book featuring traders utilizing **negative asymmetry** strategies (max gain is small, max loss is infinite).
*   **Phil Goetz:** Began as a small-cap short seller (max gain 100% when stock hits $0, infinite loss risk) and transitioned to option selling. He succeeded due to a reflex-like risk control: exiting immediately when a trade moves against him. During a market crash, he instantly offset $50M in short options by buying S&P index puts in seconds rather than slowly trying to close out illiquid options, capping his loss at under $1M.
*   **Pan Daisy:** Logged **11 years of verified statements with zero losing months** (estimated 15 years total) trading merger arbitrage. Despite algorithms executing headlines instantly, Daisy beats the bots by analyzing complex local legal structures. When a merger was announced, bots bought the stock, but Daisy read the filings and realized the firm was domiciled in Maryland (where takeovers are easily blocked) rather than Delaware, shorted the stock, and won.

> "Phil Goetz... option selling... is a negative asymmetry trade... He has this great reflex of getting out instantaneously if he's wrong... [And] Pan Daisy... has 11 years of verified statements with no losing months... he finds these situations which are too sophisticated for the bots... Maryland [legal details] as opposed to Delaware."
> — *Jack Schwager*

#### 5. Bill Eckhart: The Human Emotion Penalty
Schwager concludes with a lesson on trading psychology. Humans are naturally wired to fail at trading because our instincts tell us to avoid pain. When a trade goes into a loss, exiting immediately locks in the pain. Instead, humans hope and hold, hoping for a recovery. This natural human emotion is punished by the market. As Bill Eckhart famously noted, a dart-throwing monkey will outperform most traders because the monkey lacks human hope and fear.

> "Our natural instincts are to do the type of things which the markets actually punish... holding on to a losing position. If you get out, it's going to feel bad... you've locked in loss. If you say, 'I'm going to give it two more days,' you hope for two days and feel better... a monkey's going to do better because he's not inhibited by human emotion."
> — *Jack Schwager*

### IV. Portfolio Positioning & Action Items

1.  **Define Hard-Coded Automated Risk Rules:** Emulate Simon Rousseau by implementing automated, broker-level hard stops on account drawdowns to prevent human stubbornness from causing catastrophic losses.
2.  **Focus on Downside Risk Metrics:** Assess portfolio managers using the Sortino Ratio or Gain-to-Pain Ratio rather than the Sharpe Ratio to avoid penalizing positive upside volatility.
3.  **Exit Negative Asymmetry Trades Instantly:** If selling options or shorting small-cap stocks, cut losses immediately. Use broad index puts for instant portfolio-level hedges rather than trying to cover illiquid option legs during market shocks.
4.  **Seek Niche Structural Inefficiencies:** In arbitrage strategies, seek complex structural details (such as local corporate bylaws, tax codes, or domicile differences) that are too complex or low-volume for automated algorithms to exploit.
5.  **Audit Long-Term Track Records:** Ensure any selected manager has at least a 10-year track record to prove their strategy survives multiple market cycles rather than being a short-term trend-following fluke.
