# Market and Investment Newsletter Digest, September 25, 2026

Synthesis of investment-relevant newsletters received 2026-09-25. The search matched 71 messages; about 47 carried usable investment content. The rest were conference and subscription promos (Adam Taggart, Topdown Charts, Fintech Brainfood, Ray Dalio's course, Elm Partners' webinar recap, Deep Value Returns' onboarding notes), AI-engineering podcasts (Latent.Space x2, AINews), general tech and culture pieces (The Neuron, a16z, Not Boring, Pirate Wires, Lunch Box, The Chip Letter's Arm/SoftBank history), Readwise, a duplicate ChinaTalk send, and paywalled stubs with no data. Several of those were dropped on the subject line alone.

The day's story is a continuation: the bond selloff from [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]] extended into the 30-year and the 7-year auction, bond volatility jumped by a third in two sessions, and equities closed the week higher anyway. Two new threads matter more than the levels. First, several writers decomposed the selloff and found it is a real-yield move, with credit stress confined to CCC borrowers so far. Second, Meta's Muse produced the first hard data on what consumer agents do to pricing power, both for frontier AI labs (spend shifting to cheaper models) and for "consumer inertia" businesses (insurance, subscriptions, OTAs).

## 1. Rates: The Long End Takes Over, and Real Yields Are Doing the Work

* **Levels and the delta from the prior digest.** The 10-year closed Thursday at **5.18%, its highest close since July 2007**, up 7bp on the day (Simon Ree, Tao of Trading). Friday morning it touched **5.23%** before falling back as US-Iran Hormuz talks reportedly progressed and US crude dropped to about $92 (Bernstein). MacroVisor puts the Thursday close **within 10-15bp of its highest level since 2002**.
  * Running total across the digest series: 5.01% on September 16 ([[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-20]]), the first close above the July 2007 high on September 23 ([[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]]), 5.11% close / 5.14% intraday on September 23-24 ([[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]]), and now 5.18-5.23%. That is about 17-22bp in seven sessions, after Pollinate Trading had already called the prior 41bp/21-day move a 94th-percentile one-month move. The pace has not slowed after the break. Bernstein notes the 10-year is "up a full point this year"; George Noble frames it as **4% to 5.2%**.
  * MacroVisor: **a 20bp-plus surge in the 10Y over two days** (Wednesday and Thursday), with "nearly the whole Treasury curve" above 5% this week and **the 5-year breaking 5% Wednesday for the first time since 2007**.
* **The 30-year is now leading.** It rose 7bp to **5.47%, its highest since June 2004** (Ree), and James Bulltard reports it **crossed 5.5% for the first time since 2004** on Friday. The prior digest had it at 5.40-5.44%. Ree's test from the day before was whether the 30-year would outrun the 5-year, since that would mean the fiscal argument is winning over the Fed argument. It did: **30Y +7bp vs. 5Y +4bp, widening the 5s30s gap from 41bp to 44bp**. Ree is careful that a 3bp one-day widening is not a trend, but it matters because it came on the most hawkish Fed-speak day of the year (§2). A hawkish Fed plus a steepening curve is the market saying hikes alone may not fix the long end.
  * This reverses the pattern [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]] recorded from Macro Is Dead (the curve had *flattened* about 25bp since early September as front-end hike pricing outran the long end). Macro Is Dead predicted the flattening would turn into a steepening once hike pricing was "substantial." One day of data now points that way.
* **The 7-year auction was the third weak auction in the sequence.** Treasury sold **$44B at 5.085%, the highest 7-year auction yield since April 1993**, with a **0.7bp tail** and **indirect bidders (the foreign and institutional proxy) taking 57.2% vs. a 64.6% average** (Ree). QTR calls it the "biggest tail since March." Ree grades it "soft, but not the mess the 5yr was." Context: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]] covered the 5-year tailing about 3bp with dealers absorbing their largest share since 2024, and [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-21]] covered the 20-year leaving dealers 17% vs. an 11% average. Three softer auctions in nine days points to end-investor demand thinning at these yields, not to a one-off.
  * **Treasury's long-bond buyback took only $4.1B of the $10.5B offered for a second straight week** (MacroVisor). A 39% take-up means holders of old long bonds are not eager to sell them back even at Treasury's price, so the buyback program Fabian Wintersberger credits with dampening the long end (§6) has limited reach.
* **The key decomposition: real yields, not inflation expectations.** Ree's most useful detail: **TIP fell 0.5% while IEF fell 0.55%**, so inflation-protected bonds sold off almost as hard as nominals. "Investors aren't demanding extra compensation for inflation. They're demanding a higher real return, which is the discount rate on every stock in the S&P moving higher." This matches TSCS Research's finding in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-21]] that 96bp of the 104bp rise in the 10-year since February 27 was real yield and only 8bp was breakeven. Why it matters: a breakeven-driven selloff would argue for TIPS and commodities as hedges; a real-yield selloff hurts both bonds and long-duration equities through the same discount-rate channel, which is why Vincent Jansen (§6) now finds real yields competitive with equity shareholder yield.
  * George Noble and Danny Dayan (Macro Musings) add a structural reason to distrust breakevens. **Breakevens have sat near 2.5% the entire cycle** regardless of prints, and they argue the Fed bought far more 10-year nominals than 10-year TIPS during QE, mechanically compressing breakevens, then read that same compressed market as proof expectations are anchored. Their advice: "Stop plugging break-evens into your models."
* **Bond volatility caught up.** The MOVE index **jumped about 21% on Wednesday, then another 9.6% Thursday to 104.6, its highest since March** (QTR, Ree). Continuity matters here: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-21]] had MOVE at 81 for the week ending September 18, and [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]] had it at **78.6** on September 22, both flagged as unusually calm for the yield level (135 in October 2023 at similar 10-year yields). The "fear not priced" gap that TSCS Research flagged has closed by about a third in two sessions. MOVE is still below the 115 recorded in March 2026 and well below October 2023's 135.
  * **Equity vol has not followed.** VIX was 15.67 Thursday (Ree) and 17.65 Friday morning (MacroVisor). MacroVisor: the VIX "says equity traders aren't pricing much stress into a bond market that just repriced by 20bp in two days." Bernstein's pre-market version: stock swings "near their calmest of the year" while bond swings "just hit their highest since March."
* **Global confirmation.** The **German 10-year hit 3.59%, its highest since 2009** (Ree), extending the European repricing [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]] covered via the 110bp French-German spread. The **dollar index is above 100** and the Bloomberg Dollar Spot Index is **up about 2% in two weeks, its best run in six months**, after the Fed's first hike in three years caught speculators short per CFTC data (MacroVisor). Dollar risk reversals have jumped at all three tenors since the hike, which MacroVisor reads as "a second market confirming this rally." Morgan Stanley publicly said **"we were wrong"** on its weak-dollar call and now expects a stronger dollar into 2027 (Bernstein). The yen rose as much as 0.6% to 157.95 after Japan's finance minister said Trump raised concerns about yen weakness; MacroVisor treats it as a bounce until MOF or BoJ acts, with a MOF primary-dealer meeting Monday.
  * **Mortgage rates: the average 30-year mortgage is 7.49%, highest since April 2024** (Bernstein). That feeds the housing-weakness point Kyler Johnson makes in §2.
* **Why yields are here, three competing explanations:**
  * *Oil.* Treasury Secretary Bessent argues this is an oil story that fades when the war ends. Jay Pelosky (Tri Polar World), quoting Daily Chartbook: **the 3-month rolling correlation between the 10-year yield and WTI hit +65%, above the Covid and 2011 Arab Spring highs and just under the 66% record set at the start of the 1990 Gulf War**. WSJ's James Mackintosh says yields track oil "more closely than ever" (via Bernstein). Bernstein: "a Hormuz headline is a rate headline." Piper Sandler: the correlation of the average stock with 10-year yields hit its **most negative reading on record (since 1960)**.
  * *Nominal growth and deficits.* MacroVisor disagrees with Bessent: **nominal growth above 6% and a deficit near 5.5% of GDP** justify these yields without help from oil. Its bias is higher yields; a sustained 10-year close back below 5% would challenge that. Noble/Dayan: the Fed is now watching nominal GDP, which went **from 5% to 6.6% as of Q2**. Michael Howell's 7-8% nominal-GDP framing in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]] points the same way.
  * *Fiscal dominance.* Ree cites CBO: **the deficit for the first 11 months of the fiscal year was $1.97T, and interest on the debt ($1.05T) now exceeds military spending ($833B) by about 26%**. In that world every hike pays bondholders and money-fund holders more income, which the BIS conceded in June can blunt tightening. This is the "Brazil playbook" argument (Russell Napier: "If you're hiring a money manager today, hire one from South Africa or Brazil"). Ree's rebuttal is the more persuasive part: an emerging-market fiscal scare shows up as a *falling* currency, rising breakevens and rising gold. This week had a 2-month dollar high, falling gold, and TIPS selling off with nominals. "The market traits this week belong to a Fed that is not done hiking." He also deflates the Brazil trade itself: the Ibovespa is 2.5x its 2008 peak in nominal reais, but **7% below it in real reais and 21% below it in dollars**, with Brazilian cash compounding about 5.5x over the same stretch.
  * Danny Dayan's paywalled "The Myth" argues the post-WW2 "inflate away the debt" episode cannot be repeated today and that YCC or QE "cannot and would not work here." Only the premise is public.

## 2. The Fed: Hawkish Talk, Contested Data, and a 1999 Analogy

* **Three officials talked up more hikes on Thursday** (Ree): NY Fed's Williams, "It's likely that another rate hike may be appropriate by the end of the year"; Cleveland's Hammack, inflation risks "tilted to the upside"; Philadelphia's Paulson, "some modest further tightening may be warranted." Paulson's shift from the hold camp was first noted in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]]; Williams putting a year-end hike on record is new and carries more weight because the NY Fed president is a permanent voter and usually signals the leadership's view.
* **Market pricing held, it did not extend.** October hike odds are "about two-in-three" (Bernstein, Friday), "roughly 70%" (The Prime Report, Thursday night) and 66% (Pelosky, after the midweek selloff). [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]] had 71%, up from 54% on September 22 and under 10% a month earlier. So the jump is intact but stalled. MacroVisor says **swaps fully price three more hikes over the next year**. A paywalled Credit From Macro to Micro note reads **SOFR September 2027 as implying a terminal rate around 4.85-4.90%**. Those two framings need reconciling against the current policy rate, which neither piece states, so this digest does not compute an implied path.
* **The 1999 analogy (Noble and Dayan, Substack live, "Back to June 1999").** Their claims: Warsh called the neutral rate "an academic exercise" and described the hike as *removing accommodation*, which means the Fed thinks policy is still loose; there is no forward guidance, so the market cannot price the endpoint; and Greenspan ran the same playbook in 1999, when "stocks and bonds played ping-pong until the 10-year hit 6.75% and things broke in March 2000." The relevance: if the analogy holds, the current 5.2% 10-year is not the stopping point, and the equity damage comes late. Their supporting data:
  * **Household wealth is up nearly $70T this cycle, and the savings rate is 3% vs. 8% last cycle** even with much higher rates. Boomers are drawing down savings instead of adding to them, which reverses one of the forces that held rates down after 2008.
  * **The 2-year has gone from 4.3% to 4.93%, and import prices are running about 7%.** That is why they think Bessent's last lever (a weaker dollar) would feed inflation and hurt bonds anyway. Noble says he read Bessent's doubling of the buybacks as "a call to short more bonds." Data conflict: Ree has the 2-year at 4.87% Thursday; The Prime Report's September 18 snapshot had 3.98%, a figure [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]] already flagged as likely wrong.
  * **Credit spreads have not moved while the 10-year went from 4% to 5.2%, and stocks are up over 80% since the Fed stopped hiking in 2023.** Their conclusion: "The market is screaming that policy is too loose." Positioning advice: own gold, gold miners, energy and copper; avoid consumer, tech, long duration and 60/40. Dayan thinks gold is dead money until the Fed cuts; Noble disagrees.
  * They cite **University of Michigan inflation expectations above 7%**. That conflicts with The Prime Report, which has the September preliminary one-year expectation at **4.6%**. The 7% may refer to a different horizon or subgroup; treat it as unverified.
* **The dovish side.** Jim Paulson (via Kyler Johnson, DeepValue Capital) argues the hike rested on "recent rear-view mirror" assessments, pointing to slow job growth, **August core CPI at its lowest year-over-year rate since 2021**, and soft retail sales. Johnson adds that **year-over-year job growth bottomed at only +0.073% in December 2025**, a level historically associated with recession, though it never turned negative, and that rising mortgage rates are pushing down housing, about **40% of core CPI**. Tom Lee (Fundstrat, via Podcast Alpha) calls the Warsh hike a mistake: **core CPI 2.35% (down from 2.5%) vs. core PCE 3.4%**, a full-point gap he blames on mechanical fee components and an AI-driven memory-chip price spike. He expects a **September 30 BLS methodology change to cut core PCE by 20-40bp**. That is a dated, falsifiable test: if core PCE does not drop and the Fed stays hawkish, Lee's macro case (and his ETH targets in §11) weakens.
  * Apollo, quoted by Johnson, takes the opposite side on energy pass-through: diesel "is an intermediate input embedded in the delivered cost of nearly every physical good," so it "migrates with a lag into core goods and services, which is exactly the kind of pass-through the Fed cannot dismiss as transitory." This is the mechanism behind the hawks' patience: core CPI can look benign today while the diesel shock (record $6.53/gallon on Tuesday per Bernstein) is still working through.
* **The labor half of the mandate (Ed D'Agostino, Mauldin Economics, "The Optics of Low-Hire, Low-Fire").** August payrolls were **+162K, unemployment 4.1%, 7.0M unemployed vs. 7.3M openings**. The structural point: an April Fed note projected **labor-force growth could fall below 10K/month in 2026**, so the payroll gain needed to hold unemployment steady drops from about **155K (2023-24) to 85K (2025) to under 10K (2026)**. CBO projects deaths will exceed births every year from 2030.
  * Why it matters: weak payroll prints no longer imply rising unemployment, which weakens the Paulson/Johnson recession read above. One tension the piece does not address: if breakeven is under 10K and August added 162K, unemployment should be falling, yet it held at 4.1%.
  * **Productivity is carrying growth: since Q4 2019, nonfarm business output is up 17.4% on hours up only 3.3%, so output per hour is up 13.7%.** **Labor's share of nonfarm output fell to 52.8% in Q2, a record low.** D'Agostino invokes Solow's 1987 line ("You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics") to argue AI has not yet shown up in the numbers. His Amazon datapoints (WSJ): **$230B+ invested in robotics manufacturing since 2010, robots in 300+ facilities assisting 75% of customer orders**, and a new $100M Indiana robotics plant creating 300 jobs at nearly $100K average salary.
  * Jobless claims came in at **197K vs. 201-204K expected** (Ree, Prime), consistent with D'Agostino's "low-fire" description.
* **Consumer data.** University of Michigan sentiment fell to **48.1 in September (final), a four-month low**, with gas above $4 for more than two months; year-ahead inflation expectations hit their highest since June (Bernstein). The preliminary read was 47.8 (Prime). **Costco comps rose 6.7%**, which MacroVisor reads as "spending hasn't cracked and gives the Fed little reason to ease off." New home sales ran at **684K vs. about 615K expected**, the fastest pace this year (Ree).
* Sources: Simon Ree (Tao of Trading), J.L. Bernstein (Pivot & Flow), The Prime Report, Jay Pelosky (The Tri Polar World), MacroVisor, Credit From Macro to Micro, George Noble (The Noble Update), Kyler Johnson (DeepValue Capital), Podcast Alpha (Tom Lee episode), Ed D'Agostino (Mauldin Economics Global Macro Update).

## 3. Credit: Stress Is in the Basement, Not the Whole Building

* **The scary chart and why most of it is bond math (Simon Ree).** HYG closed at **77.89, its lowest since April 2025**, while SPY sat about 1.4% off its record. In Ree's 11 years of daily data, HYG had **never closed at a 52-week low with SPY within 5% of its high; it has now done it 6 times since September 10**. His decomposition: **over the past year HYG is down 4.1% in price, while HYGH (the rate-hedged version) is down only 0.4%**, so about 90% of HYG's decline is Treasury duration, not default risk. Adding back $4.36 of distributions since the October high, a holder is still up about 1%.
  * **Spreads confirm it.** The ICE BofA high-yield spread was **2.73% Wednesday, 13bp off its 3-year tight of 2.60% (August 28)**, and investment-grade spreads are **0.77%**, a few basis points from their tights. Pollinate Trading cited the same 2.73% in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]] as "near the tightest of this century," so the reading has held for at least two sessions through the auction stress. Ree also notes that at the last two big SPY tops (January 2022, February 2025), HYG was within 1% of its high and gave no warning, so HYG's track record as a top signal is weak.
* **Where the suspicion is justified: CCC.** The CCC spread hit **10.93% on Wednesday, its widest since April 7, 2025 (Liberation Day)**, up almost in a straight line from **9.46% at the start of June (about +147bp)**, while BB spreads sit at **1.59%**, near 3-year tights. Kyler Johnson makes the same observation (CCC widening while AAA and BBB stay flat) and ties it to small caps: IWM falling while SPY is flat.
  * **Torsten Slok (Apollo, "Higher for Longer Hits the Lowest Rated") gives the mechanism.** With **CCC yields around 15%**, every month rates stay high pushes more CCC borrowers from the **2021-22 vintages** into the refinancing wall with less cash to service debt. Pain is sharpest in **heavily levered, PE-backed technology, healthcare and consumer discretionary**, where floating-rate debt, thin margins and AI disruption risk combine. "Monetary policy is working with a lag and working unevenly": strong balance sheets locked in cheap fixed-rate debt, so transmission is "running mainly through the bottom of the credit stack." His recommendation is to move up in quality, since high-grade credit still offers attractive all-in yields.
  * JPMorgan equity strategists (via Ree) make the matching equity argument: margins are high, **net debt is 20-40% below historical norms and corporate debt has 5-6 years to run**, so higher rates take a long time to bite. Thursday tested it (30-year at a 22-year high, three hawkish Fed speakers, Houthi missiles on Saudi Arabia) and SPY closed basically flat.
* **Ree's tripwires.** Either the overall junk spread through **3.00%** or HYGH breaking its March low of **84.78** would mean the "rot is starting to spread" upstairs. "Credit tops tend to start in the basement and work their way upstairs." His overall rating: "a dim yellow warning light."
* Cross-reference: [[ai-compute-commencement-wall-and-refinancing-trap]] for the refinancing-cliff framework, which the CCC data now shows operating in the general leveraged-loan market and not only in AI.
* Sources: Simon Ree (Tao of Trading), Torsten Slok (Apollo, The Daily Spark), Kyler Johnson (DeepValue Capital).

## 4. AI Financing: Project Jupiter's Details, and Demand That Keeps Signing

* **What is new on Oracle since [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]].** That digest covered the force majeure notice, Oracle's -3.28% day, debt below 90 cents, and Craig Shapiro's CDS ladder (Oracle about 224bp). The new details:
  * **Size and lenders.** Project Jupiter is a **2.45GW, $165B campus** (Ree) backed by an **$18B bank loan from roughly 20 banks** (MacroVisor). Blue Owl has **about $3B of equity** in the project (Ree). The notice went to Blue Owl's Stack Infrastructure (The Prime Report). Oracle's **credit gauge hit a record** Thursday (MacroVisor, QTR, Ree), and the shares are **down 29% this year** (MacroVisor).
  * **Timing.** The gas pipeline feeding the site is delayed **nearly six months, to February 1, 2027**, after repeated permit denials (MacroVisor); Reuters says the project is **now about a year late** (via Ree).
  * **What the clause does, with a conflict.** MacroVisor: if both sides agree force majeure applies, **Oracle could win up to a three-year delay on rent**, though it would still owe the full lease term once payments start. WSJ (via Bernstein): Oracle **can't get out of the lease and owes rent whether or not the site has power**. The Prime Report adds that **securing power is Oracle's own job under the contract**, which weakens the force majeure claim. These are reconcilable if the dispute is about deferral rather than exit, but the economic gap between "three-year deferral" and "owes rent regardless" is large for the lenders, which is why MacroVisor says to watch "the credit gauge and the loan price ahead of the shares."
  * **Contagion on the day:** Blue Owl -3.7%, Bloom Energy -3.1%, Apollo -3.0%, and **SB Energy postponed its IPO** (Ree). Arthur D. Little's Sean McDevitt: "The financing side of the AI buildout is starting to ask much harder questions than the demand side."
* **The demand side keeps signing, which is the point MacroVisor makes: "The problem isn't demand... It's power and permits."**
  * **Akamai signed a seven-year, $11.6B compute deal with Anthropic that could grow to about $20B**, and Anthropic gets a warrant for **up to 5% of Akamai at $111.33/share** (Bernstein, Parekh citing Reuters). The contract is worth **about two-thirds of Akamai's entire market value** at Thursday's close. Akamai opened sharply higher and gave back most of it, closing **+4.44%** (Bernstein's pre-market ticker shows -6.78% next to text saying "sharply higher," likely a data error). The muted close says the market is discounting a large single-customer contract from a lab whose own funding depends on the IPO window.
  * **xAI plans to more than double the Nvidia chips in Colossus 2** (MacroVisor).
* **Concentration risk in the backlog (Kyler Johnson, citing Apollo).** "The credit story in hyperscalers rests on a single consensus assumption, that **operating cash flow triples from $600 billion to $2 trillion**." Johnson adds that **over half of the current ~$2.3T backlog comes from OpenAI and Anthropic**. He uses the 1990s telecom and 1840s railway buildouts: the rails got used, but most rail companies went bankrupt because demand arrived later than financed. This connects directly to Shapiro's transmission chain in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]]: if two private labs are half the backlog, their funding (IPO timing, §7) is the credit variable for the whole chain.
* **The rates channel.** Vincent Jansen (Artificial Alpha) argues the long-end bond imbalance is rooted in private issuance: hyperscalers "have run out of cash flows to finance their investments," and **hyperscaler debt issuance now competes in size with long-duration Treasury issuance**. Bernstein's pre-market brief makes the same link: data-center borrowers "are competing with government bonds for investors' cash." Brij Khurana made this crowding-out argument in [[ax-brij-khurana-wellington-ai-capex-crowding-out-bonds-20261001]]. Jansen names a **capex retreat** as one catalyst that could make bonds outperform.
* **QTR's bear case, restated.** He counts **8-10 major rallies or reversals in six months** rescued by a positive Iran/Hormuz headline, none of which produced durable progress, and calls Thursday's Reuters-report rally "another pit stop on the road to... much lower." His core claim: the headline "didn't make $18 billion of debt... magically healthier" and didn't lower the cost of capital for AI infrastructure. Expect "more projects that run into delays, more financing that suddenly doesn't look as attractive with Treasury yields above 5%, more debt that banks thought they could easily distribute but wind up holding."
* Cross-reference: [[ubp-financing-ai-build-out-2026-09]] and [[brookings-financing-ai-buildout-van-nieuwerburgh-2026-09]] for the off-balance-sheet structures (Beignet, Stack) that Jupiter now stress-tests.

## 5. Oil and the War: Paper vs. Physical, and a Market That Has Stopped Modeling

* **Prices.** November Brent rose 3.4% to **$106.60** Thursday and December Brent closed back above $100 at **$100.22**; November WTI rose 2.7% to **$94.61** after Houthi missiles were fired at Saudi Arabia's Taif and Yanbu (Ree). By Friday morning Brent had slipped to about **$105** on reports of a phased US-Iran deal and a seven-day ceasefire offer (MacroVisor), and US crude fell to about **$92** (Bernstein). MacroVisor's line in the sand is **Brent below $106**: "a bounce back through it would revive the yields problem." The roughly $6 gap between November and December Brent is the steep backwardation Open Insights describes below.
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]] had Brent at $103.08 close / $105.59 futures and Lance Roberts's WTI down nearly 15% from a $106 peak on September 15. Today's range is the same, which supports Pelosky's "both oil and bonds stay within their ranges" call.
* **Open Insights ("When All You Can Say is Wow").** JPMorgan's Natasha Kaneva: **"For the first time since the start of the Iran conflict, we don't have a baseline view. We simply don't know how to model the endgame."** Open Insights' point: the sell-side is paid to have a view, so a public shrug from the best-resourced oil desk is itself the signal.
  * **Paper vs. physical:** about **$90/barrel in the paper market vs. $120 physical**. Product prices are about **$200/barrel**, which Open Insights describes as the crack spread "breaching $200" as Russian and Middle East refinery outages linger. This is a different measure from the **$58/barrel crack spread** (99.8th percentile since 2006) Roberts reported in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]]; the two describe the same refined-product tightness from different reference points and should not be compared directly.
  * **The 2008 benchmark:** crude topped at **$145 in 2008, about $215 in today's dollars, or roughly $245-250 with an equivalent crack spread**. Products are near that level; crude lags. Open Insights reads this as demand destruction already under way: **trucking and airline traffic are starting to contract**, and airlines have issued warnings. Oil equities have stalled because "the market just doesn't want to give producers credit" for prices it thinks are temporary; the futures curve effectively **caps everything around $75**.
  * **The military argument for a stalemate:** cheap drones and missiles have shifted advantage to the defender (Open Insights uses the WWI machine-gun and 1930s armor cycle as the analogy), so dislodging Iran from Hormuz "means a ground war," which the US is unlikely to start before the midterms (about 1.5 months away). Iran's offer to reopen in seven days returns to the June MOU, which "stipulates that Iran controls the SoH." Their question: "what's really changed?"
* **Supply events in the last two weeks (Open Insights, Bernstein, Pelosky):**
  * Iraqi proxies disabled Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline to Yanbu; repairs take weeks and the Saudis **canceled shipments to European refineries for a month**. Pelosky reports the Saudis are already testing the pipeline to reopen it. Kpler (via Bernstein) has Saudi exports at **about 6M barrels/day this month, the most since the war started**, even with the pipeline shut, which implies more volume routed back through Hormuz's southern Oman lane.

## 7. Consumer Agents: Muse's Real Numbers and the Pricing-Power Question

* **Adoption is fast; usage and revenue are thin so far.** Muse launched September 8 and has held **No. 1 on Apple's US App Store since September 18**, ahead of ChatGPT (WSJ via Parekh). Downloads: **2.5M by Monday (1.5M iOS, 1.1M Android; CNBC/Sensor Tower)**, **2.8M within 12 days (Reuters, via StockOpine and Net Interest)**, **3.4M+ (Bernstein)**. In their first 13 days ChatGPT had 3.1M, Claude 400K and Grok 200K (Sensor Tower via Parekh), so Muse's launch roughly matches ChatGPT's. But:
  * **Daily active users are below 1 million** (Alex Kantrowitz, Big Technology), a small fraction of downloads.
  * **Appfigures estimates Muse netted about $27,000 in its first 11 days vs. $175,000 for SpaceXAI's Grok Bot** (Parekh). Muse is free for a large token allowance, with $20 and $100 plans.
  * **Rosenblatt estimates a typical user would need to buy more than $1,000/month through Muse for it to be profitable at today's compute costs** (The Information via Parekh). Since Meta's announced model is a small cut of purchases ([[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]]), that threshold explains the gap between the stock reaction and the unit economics.
  * **Trust gap:** an Oppenheimer survey found **8% of US consumers would trust Meta with their passwords, vs. 30% for Google**. Rest of World reports (citing Reuters) that some Muse calls to businesses are actually handled by human contractors.
* **The stock reaction.** Meta is **up about 27% since Muse launched** and 18% YTD (The Information), up about 30% in September and on pace for its **best month since 2013** (WSJ), and came within reach of a **$2T market value** before falling **3.33% Friday** (Bernstein). Parekh reads the rally as the first visible return on **$130-145B of 2026 capex** and expects Meta may use the momentum to raise equity, since it is "aggressively spending down the free cash flow from its ad business" and leaning on debt "to the limit, both on its balance sheet and off." The Information's advice was to sell some. A Truist analyst projects Muse could add **$28.5B of revenue by 2030**, which Parekh calls "a tad generous."
* **The bigger finding: spend is moving away from frontier models (Kantrowitz, citing Ramp).** Kantrowitz expected Muse to fail because it runs on non-frontier models; he now says that was wrong. **The share of AI dollars going to frontier models fell from 53% at the start of August to 45% at the start of September.** Ramp chief economist Ara Kharazian: "The revenue growth path for OpenAI and Anthropic has weakened due to competition... from *other* US models, not China / open source." Price gap: **OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra costs $10/$50 per million input/output tokens vs. Meta's Muse Spark at $1.25/$4.25**, about 8x cheaper on input and 12x on output. Model routers and harnesses let "next-level-down" models do most work, and buyers "are done 'tokenmaxxing.'"
  * Why it matters: the AI-financing story in §4 rests on lab revenue growing into a ~$2.3T backlog. An 8-point shift in one month away from the labs that make up over half that backlog is the most direct evidence yet against that path. This digest cannot judge from one month whether it is a trend or noise; Kantrowitz notes the ratio "has vacillated over time." FUNDA's enterprise survey (§8) shows the same routing behavior from the buyer side.
  * [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-21]] (Stratechery) flagged Muse as bearish for OpenAI/Anthropic because a personal agent has bank-like switching costs. Kantrowitz's data adds a second, faster channel: pricing pressure on tokens.
* **Who loses the "interaction layer" (Jamin Ball, Clouded Judgement).** When the internet arrived, OTAs took the customer interaction from GDS systems (Amadeus, Sabre) and pushed them down to middleware; whoever owns the interaction captures most of the profit. Agents may now do the same to OTAs. **Expedia fell almost 10% on the week after announcing a Muse partnership.** Amazon took the opposite approach and blocked Muse to protect its ad-driven interaction ([[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-22]]). Ball's prediction: challengers (Uber Eats vs. DoorDash) will embrace agents to take share, incumbents will resist, then adopt later "with inopportune structures." He also flags how many businesses "rely on consumers being lazy": insurance billing, subscriptions, never-shopped car insurance.
  * **Newcomer names it a new "SaaS-pocalypse": the "consumer inertia" sector.** Banks, brokerages and mobile carriers fell on the prospect of costless switching; OpenTable and DoorDash on bypass risk; subscription media on agents rationalizing purchases, with **New York Times Co. down 15% since late last week**. Newcomer thinks it is an overreaction, as with the original SaaS selloff ([[saaspocalypse-dispersion-2026-04-09]]).
  * **Net Interest (Marc Rubinstein, "The Agents Revolt")** shows the mechanism in finance: Rubinstein's own car-insurance premium jumped **83%** at renewal, and the Instinct agent shopped carriers and offered to switch him overnight. A Muse user reported saving **$3,500/year** switching from Liberty Mutual to Progressive. a16z's Angela Strange: "Insurance companies (and banks) should be TERRIFIED... Inertia and information asymmetry will no longer be enough to keep customers." The paywalled remainder ranks exposed firms.
  * **Booking (StockOpine) is less exposed than Expedia in StockOpine's view**, because its value lies in aggregating fragmented European hotel and alternative-accommodation supply plus payments, reviews and loyalty. The risk it names is losing where the journey starts: **app bookings rose from just over 25% of bookings in 2019 to over 50%**, and agents could reverse that.
* **SaaS multiples, with the prior reading.** Clouded Judgement: **overall median 4.2x EV/NTM revenue, top-5 median 39.7x, high-growth 18.0x, mid-growth 6.7x, low-growth 3.3x**, with a 10-year at 5.2%. The September 18 digest had 4.2x overall, 17.5x/6.5x/3.5x by bucket, at a 4.90% 10-year. Despite a 30bp rise in the risk-free rate, the median multiple is unchanged and high-growth names expanded slightly while low-growth compressed. Operating medians: NTM growth 13%, LTM 16%, gross margin 76%, FCF margin 21%, net retention 110%, CAC payback 31 months.
* **Competition and capital.** Instinct, which just raised at **$2.5B**, is reportedly seeking money at **$10B** (Newcomer); Muse's Shopify deal sent Shopify **up 10%+** and is likely not exclusive. Meta added Walmart, Sephora, Best Buy and Gap as partners, opened its connector platform (1,500+ applications in under a week), and announced the **Muse Charm** keychain device for December (Parekh). OpenAI hired OpenClaw's creator and is expected to ship a consumer agent within weeks; Google has Gemini Spark; Apple's iOS 27 Siri (built on Gemini) processes personal data on-device. Microsoft (**+3.67%**) unified Copilot into one app with a persistent agent ("Autopilot") that runs on usage-based credits and is off by default; Every's hands-on found permission settings blocked a Word-to-Excel handoff in the demo. Microsoft runs about **16,000 internal evals** (Every).
  * **Muse's security design** (Parekh): each user gets a cloud VM; a separate "Sentinel" agent is the sole gatekeeper for outbound actions; the agent sees stand-in tokens, not real credentials; purchases use single-use card numbers via Stripe Link. Meta's bug bounty pays up to **$300,000**, including up to $130,000 for a single-user prompt injection. A stronger "Confidential VM" (built with Moxie Marlinspike) is due later this year.
* **Anthropic's governance and the private-market tone.** Anthropic's IPO is now more likely in **late October or November**, and it is asking shareholders to give its **seven co-founders (about 2% each) a share class with 50.1% of voting control** as a group, while the Long-Term Benefit Trust still elects a board majority (The Information via Parekh). **TypeSafe**, whose agent-oriented model "Jev" answers with numbers and probabilities at up to 200x frontier speed, raised at about **$200M last week and is now in talks at $10B+** (Parekh). Parekh's read: $5-10B is now the default ask for hot AI startups because VC preference protections make the markup cheap to give, but it leaves "very little room to execute."

## 8. Enterprise AI Spending: Cheaper Per Call, Still Growing in Total (FUNDA Survey Vol. 5)

* **Sample and headline.** FUNDA interviewed 10 companies (payments, telecom, payroll/HR, e-commerce, used cars, media agency, digital marketing, pharma, a diversified holding company, industrial software). Most still plan to spend more on AI, but **unit costs are falling through routing to cheaper models, price negotiation and caching**. That slowed total spending growth without stopping it; only the media agency's spend was flat (about **$9M/month**, with API costs down 10-20% after tighter approvals for top-tier models).
* **The cost-cutting numbers, which line up with Ramp's data in §7:**
  * The industrial software company negotiated one API program **from about $250,000 to $50,000** with no quality loss, yet total AI spend still rose **from about $170K to $200K/month (+18%)**.
  * The digital marketing company cut its **~$100K/month token bill by more than half** through model selection a year ago; spend has since grown again at **2-6% per month**. Its expert: "the most expensive model, or the quote-unquote best model, is not the answer. Maybe it's the cheap one who behaves better with our use case." It adopted DeepSeek after its own evals beat the GPT model it was using.
  * The payroll company's European arm estimates a planned LLM gateway plus routing could **cut total spend about 50%**, with small or open-weight models costing **one-third to one-sixth** as much on suitable tasks. It **disabled Fable after a 14-day developer trial** because credits ran out too fast.
  * The telecom uses frontier models only for planning, Sonnet/Haiku for text-to-SQL and lower tiers for summaries. Its expert thinks current API prices are subsidized by the labs: "it will not be forever like that."
* **Open-weight share is rising in production.** The industrial software company runs **about 40% of API workloads on open-weight models, plans 60% next year and targets 80% within five years**. No company in the sample tied usage to Fable's zero-data-retention change; frontier use is limited (e.g., the media agency's frontier share is about 15% of AI spend).
* **ROI evidence is thin but specific.** One company replaced a marketing-automation license of **$200-250K/year with an in-house tool costing $3,000/month**. The industrial software company reports **about 35% ROI** on its own formula with a **30% hurdle** per solution. The used-car marketplace has **35-40% of projects positive and 40-45% near breakeven**. Most others report time savings and more clients per head without converting them to dollars.
* **Why it matters for the AI trade:** the survey describes demand that is real and growing, but with buyers pushing hard on price and moving volume to cheaper and open-weight models. That is good for token volume and inference hardware, and bad for frontier-lab pricing power, which is the variable the §4 backlog depends on. Cross-reference: [[ai-inference-costs-accounting]].

## 9. China: The Datacenter Buildout Nobody Measured, Alibaba's Cloud, and the Summit

* **SemiAnalysis China Datacenter Model (preview).** Building-level tracking of **1,000+ facilities across 60+ operators** puts China's fleet at **over 24GW**, vs. **56GW in the US (2026 year-end)**, about 15GW APAC ex-China, 14GW EMEA and 2GW Latin America. That excludes ~20GW of dated pipeline and ~30GW announced. Published estimates of China's capacity had differed by 15x, so this is the first hard number.
  * **Capex inflection:** in **2Q26, Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu spent a combined $20B, more than double year over year, and all three posted negative free cash flow for the first time**. Including ByteDance, combined capex went from **about $35B (2024) to $50B+ (2025) and is tracking toward $100B in 2026**. US hyperscalers turned FCF-negative earlier in the cycle ([[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-22]] tracked construction-in-progress above $374B); China is now following the same pattern a few quarters behind.
  * **ByteDance occupies about one-fifth of China's delivered capacity and rents nearly all of it**, making it the most important customer for every wholesale colocation provider. GDS and VNET signed **about 1.3GW of wholesale orders in 1H26 (vs. under 10MW retail)** but captured only about a third of ByteDance and Alibaba orders in 2024-2026 YTD.
  * **Why vacancy and shortage coexist:** legacy retail racks (about 90% of early facilities ran under 2kW) sit at 30-50% utilization and can't host AI densities, while wholesale AI buildings in the west fill fast. At VNET, wholesale utilization is back above 70% vs. about 60% for retail. Wholesale prices **halved from about $80/kW/month**, and GDS expects another 18 months to digest the reset.
  * **Speed and cost:** China delivers a **100MW facility in about 12 months** (down from ~18 months in the cloud era) with **3-6 months of permitting**, vs. 12-18 months of construction plus 12-13 months of permitting in the best US modular case. The US buildout is power-gated; China's is chip-gated. Grid-company capex for the **15th Five-Year Plan (2026-2030) is over $746B (¥5T)**, 40% above the prior plan. Inner Mongolia, with power at about half Tier-1 city prices, has become "China's Johor." Overseas leasing by Chinese hyperscalers is set to double to about 4GW by 2029, before counting GPUs rented from Western clouds.
  * Relevance: this is the counterpoint to the US permitting story in §4. Project Jupiter is late on a gas-pipeline permit; China's binding constraint is chip supply under export controls.
* **Alibaba FY1Q27 (Outperforming the Market).** Revenue **+8.6% to Rmb 269B**, in line. **AI cloud and compute revenue +45% to Rmb 48B, the ninth straight quarter of acceleration**, with adjusted EBITA margin up 4.4 points to **12%** (from 10% last quarter). AI-related products are **Rmb 12.4B per quarter, a $7.3B run rate that management guides to $10B next quarter (+37% sequentially)**. MaaS ARR passed **Rmb 16B in August with a Rmb 30B target by year-end**. Management sees a **capex payback under three years** and targets **$100B of external cloud revenue by 2031 at a 20% EBITA margin**. The cost side: net income **fell 38% to Rmb 21B vs. Rmb 25B expected**, EPS Rmb 8.52 vs. 10.60, and the AI labs and applications segment lost Rmb 13.9B. Core commerce (CMR) fell 7% while quick commerce grew 45%.
* **The Trump-Xi summit.** Mostly ceremony. The one deliverable, a **two-month truce extension (to January 10 per Bernstein; Bessent said from November)**, has not been confirmed by Beijing (MacroVisor, Inside China). USTR Greer said AI chips were not discussed and export rules stay as they are; he promises "a lot more details" Monday, and the first thing to check is farm purchases, where **China is behind on about $17B of commitments** (Bernstein). China Beige Book reports US companies ordered more from Chinese suppliers in September; Eurasia Group puts the odds of stable ties at their highest since Trump returned.
  * Fred Gao (Inside China) reads the short extension as leaving room for another leader-level announcement, and reads China's "highly constructive" (富有建设性) description of the trade talks as stronger than the usual "constructive." AI dialogue was the largest new agenda item, but both sides treat AI as a competitive domain, so expect only something like a risk-notification mechanism. Xi asked the US to "oppose Taiwan independence."
  * Rest of World: China rejected Dario Amodei's call to slow frontier development as an attempt to preserve the US lead, and **Beijing is investigating DeepSeek and Moonshot** after Anthropic alleged they routed users' requests through Claude. GSMA warns rising memory-chip prices could cause "a sharp drop in people connecting to the internet" in developing countries by making cheap smartphones harder to build.
* **Tooze, "Walled-in wealth #2."** The number of mainland Chinese in the Forbes top 100 has fallen to **less than half of the 20 in 2021**, while top US fortunes are "well above the centi-billionaire level." His term is "oligarchic divergence": decoupling that trade and balance-of-payments data miss. Separately, Brad Setser notes **UK-custodied holdings of Treasuries hit $998.3B in July (from $939.9B)** and may soon pass Japan's; the UK figure is mostly a custody location, not British demand.

* **Tom Lee's ETH ladder (Podcast Alpha).** **$3,000 near term, above $5,000 by year-end, and a $60,000 long-run scenario** based on a repeat of ETH's 2018-2021 17x run (10-12x its prior high).
