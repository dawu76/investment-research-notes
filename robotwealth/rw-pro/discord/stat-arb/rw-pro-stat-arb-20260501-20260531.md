## 1. The month's headline: expanding triangulated stat arb beyond equities (FX, ETFs, crypto)

The single most consequential thread of the month, opened by robotkris on May 14.

robotkris credited hac with "the most excellent idea" of taking the triangulated stat-arb framework — built and tuned on equities — and applying it to an entirely different asset class: currencies. His reasoning, in his own words, is a generalizable principle worth remembering: *"take a framework or idea that works in one market and apply it elsewhere. Nearly always beats trying to squeeze another 5% out of somethign you're already running. Diversification, additional source of edge."*

The build: he took a basket of G10 currency crosses, converted them into "spreads" the same way the equity version does, ranked them with the existing LSR/ED approach, and flattened the z-scores into currency-level views (AUD, NZD, CAD, JPY, GBP, EUR, CHF vs. USD). The result surprised him — he didn't expect the approach to scale down to a universe this small, but he and hac reached "broadly the same conclusion" independently, using two different data sets. He flagged this as a first-pass result (no costs modeled yet) and promised a deeper walkthrough in the following week's webinar.

The reaction was immediate and enthusiastic:
- William and MidKnight both said they'd been sitting on similar ideas (bond carry, broader diversification) but had dismissed them, assuming the universe would be too small to work — a mistake in hindsight.
- Matt G and Euan debated whether the smaller FX universe makes this a *simpler* entry point for smaller accounts, or scales the other way for *larger* accounts via derivatives — Euan's view: "or bigger account sizes. scales to infinity (and beyond)."
- ppaolo immediately asked about crypto. robotkris confirmed it's already in the works, but flagged it as a "bigger infra project than currencies due to universe size" — i.e., not a quick copy-paste like FX was.

The idea then took on a life of its own beyond robotkris's original FX build:
- **mm independently extended the same pipeline to country ETFs** (~6,000 ETFs parsed down to a country-level universe, `max_positions = 20`, no vol targeting or parameter tuning — literally just swapping the asset list) and separately to **crypto**, prompting robotkris's most enthusiastic reaction of the month: *"Holy shit, I had an inkling that we could port this around, but I honestly didn't expect it to be this good. This is super exciting."*
- The crypto variant surfaced its own asset-class-specific problems that don't exist in equities or FX: **SatoriNakaMoto** found it "very susceptible to In-sample-Out Sample Divergence," calling crypto "a very narrative driven thing" where "'value' is a very transient concept." **-k** reframed funding-rate exposure as a feature rather than a bug — you can bake funding into the signal's expected-returns mapping and effectively run a combined funding-arb + stat-arb position — and the group spent the back half of May 20 iterating on funding-gate filters (excluding pairs with divergent recent funding behavior) with SatoriNakaMoto and gc comparing notes against a separate crypto-pairs approach from an outside source ("James's" substack, cited as achieving 2.83 OOS Sharpe on a hand-picked 10-pair universe).
- **Dan** flagged futures/indices/commodities as another natural extension (30+ liquid global index futures for capital-efficient long/short exposure), with the caveat that futures introduce a size/granularity problem — most commodity pairs only have liquid micros in the index space, not in related-commodity pairs like Platinum/Palladium or Cocoa/Coffee.

**Sentiment:** Genuinely exciting and validating — this reads as the month's real "edge expansion" story, moving from a single-asset-class strategy to a multi-asset framework in the space of two weeks, with crypto's data/regime challenges as the one sobering caveat.

**Pull quotes:**

## 2. Earnings amplify the stat-arb edge (robotkris research)

Early in the month, robotkris shared standalone research on how earnings announcements affect the strategy. Using the full suite of spreads available via the API (not just the filtered-down set used in the standard implementation), he found the strategy earns higher returns around earnings — expected, since price action is more active and the underlying mean-reversion edge gets a stronger chance to realize. More notably, **risk-adjusted returns were also higher around earnings, and this held up on an annual basis in 5 of 7 years** — a persistence result, not a one-off.

**Sentiment:** Informational, well-received — a data point worth remembering when thinking about position sizing or timing around earnings season.

**Pull quotes:**
- robotkris: *"The strategy earns higher returns over earnings announcements, which is expected. The strategy has edge, and over earnings things are moving around more, which leads to higher realisation of the edge. But risk adjusted returns are also higher."*
- robotkris: *"Interestingly, the high risk adjusted returns thing is also quite stable on an annual basis (higher in 5 of 7 years on earnings days)."*

## 6. Universe size, commissions, and portfolio construction tradeoffs

A recurring practical question as multiple members size up their live books: how many tickers should you actually run?

- MidKnight was weighing a 60-ticker basket, floating a "dynamic depth filter matrix" concept. mm pushed back gently — no strong reason to prefer exactly 60 over a smaller high-conviction set — and settled the group on a rough heuristic: **cap around 60–80 tickers**, with 30–40 being perfectly fine on any given day since the universe naturally fluctuates.
- Rachit went the opposite direction, deliberately starting with a 20-ticker universe. mm and MidKnight both validated this for smaller accounts, with MidKnight's rule of thumb being **roughly $1.5k per ticker minimum** (achievable with leverage).
- ASlan made the more general point that portfolio size interacts with universe size — smaller accounts suffer disproportionately from a larger ticker set, which is why he also targeted 20.
- Euan offered the most experience-weighted take on the debate, noting that in his experience most of the edge comes from the "best" stocks — the long tail of additional tickers mostly just lowers volatility rather than adding real signal.

**Sentiment:** Practical convergence — no single "right" universe size, but a workable range (20 for smaller/newer accounts, up to 60–80 for larger ones) emerged from lived experience across several members.

**Pull quotes:**
- Euan: *"In my limited experience, most edge comes from the 'best' stocks. The rest just lower vol."*

## 7. Execution mechanics: MOC orders, trade buffers, peg orders, and fill quality

The month's most execution-heavy thread, spanning May 12–14 as several members went live simultaneously.

- **-k** gave the most detailed practical guidance: on IBKR, near/mid-peg orders are a reasonable default since the signal decays slowly and is fairly robust even to a one-day-stale fill; for anyone relying on MOC (market-on-close) orders without computing signals independently, the underlying data is necessarily stale (based on 3:30pm prices) but that's likely fine in practice. His suggested hybrid: send peg orders as soon as signals arrive from the API, then MOC-fill only what's left unfilled right before the NYSE's 3:50pm cutoff.
- **MidKnight** reported a concrete data point on MOC fill quality: ~20% of his orders on one day didn't fill to the exact quantity specified, some over- and some under-filled, which he traced to NYSE's "Significant Imbalance" flagging mechanism. He and Rachit cross-checked fills on the same tickers (CVBF, HBAN, NWBI, TFSL) that day and found genuine over/under-fill discrepancies of a few cents on each — useful shared evidence that MOC imbalance risk is real, not just theoretical.
- **Rachit**, using midpoint/peg-mid execution on a 20-ticker universe instead, reported clean fills with no imbalance issues over his first two days live — a useful natural A/B comparison against MidKnight's MOC approach.
- Separately, **opm** made a strongly-held case for testing execution quality *before* going live at all — using a paper/demo account with live L2 data to simulate realistic fills, because slippage that isn't visible in a paper backtest is where strategies that "look" profitable actually fail in the market.
- **whiplash** raised a tangential design question — using MVO (mean-variance optimization) on signals rather than raw returns, given Euan's stated skepticism of MVO's return/covariance instability in the webinars. Euan deferred a substantive answer pending review.
- **Quarry614** contributed a well-received artifact: a full Excel walkthrough of the stat-arb pipeline's upstream steps, praised by whiplash as "brilliant."

**Sentiment:** Highly practical and collaborative — this is members actively trading live and comparing real fill data in near-real-time, which is more concretely useful than backtested theory.

**Pull quotes:**
- -k: *"the thing about MOC is that if you're not computing the signals yourself, you're getting them based on 3:30 prices... the answer is that yes, they are stale, and that it's probably okay anyways."*
- opm: *"I think it's really important to know the potential slippage before trading anything live. For me it's part of the edge. I've wasted plenty of time on strategies that make money on paper, then don't in the market - purely from an execution perspective. You don't have an edge if you can't get a fill..."*

## 10. M&A ticker exclusion workflow, including an LLM-based approach

Prompted by NextEra/Dominion merger talk (which forced Andre and MidKnight to manually close out short D positions), the channel converged on a shared practice: maintain a running blacklist of M&A-involved tickers (both target and acquirer) and exclude them from the tradeable universe until the deal resolves. robotkris confirmed the house strategy does the same — pairs data for D and NEE were excluded from RW's own universe.

The most substantial contribution here came from **Michael TW 88**, who spent a week building an LLM-based classification workflow to replace manual keyword filtering (which he'd found insufficient — still required manually reading articles). His prompt frames the LLM as a "blacklist governance analyst" that weighs market-data evidence, acquirer/acquiree distinctions, and market-reaction size, producing a daily-refreshed table (ticker, status, verdict, role, confidence, current read) for tickers on the watchlist. robotkris called this "excellent and probably the most efficient" approach, and flagged it as a candidate for eventual first-class API support — though for now, "some sort of LLM-based workflow is probably going to be hard to beat."

## Overall read

May was the busiest month on record for #stat-arb (270 messages), and the volume tracks two distinct forces: a wave of members going live with real capital for the first time (driving the execution-mechanics, universe-sizing, and cross-checking threads), and a genuine strategic breakthrough from robotkris and hac in extending the core triangulated stat-arb framework beyond equities.

1. **The FX/crypto/ETF expansion (section 1) is the month's most important development.** robotkris's own framing — "take a framework or idea that works in one market and apply it elsewhere. Nearly always beats trying to squeeze another 5% out of somethign you're already running" — is a philosophy worth remembering beyond this specific strategy. Watch for the promised webinar deep-dive and for whether the crypto variant's in-sample/out-of-sample fragility gets resolved via the funding-gate filtering approach -k and SatoriNakaMoto were iterating on.
2. **Earnings amplify the equity strategy's edge, persistently across 5 of 7 years** — a data point worth incorporating into position-sizing or timing decisions if not already accounted for.
3. **Execution quality is a live, unresolved arms race.** MOC orders carry real fill-quality risk (NYSE "Significant Imbalance" flagging caused genuine over/under-fills this month), while peg/midpoint orders performed more reliably for at least one member on a smaller universe. No consensus best practice yet, but concrete comparative data is accumulating.
4. **Community-built tooling is outpacing official infrastructure** in at least two areas: Michael TW 88's LLM-based M&A blacklist workflow, and opm's independently-built AWS/Yahoo-Finance pipeline — both filling gaps robotkris acknowledged the platform doesn't yet cover natively.
5. **A borrow-cost calculation bug and a historical-API-vs-CSV discrepancy were both surfaced this month but only partially resolved** — worth checking back on in June's digest for resolution.
