## 1. CFDs vs. futures for European traders (VIX/VXX exposure)

Matt G asked how CFDs compare to CBOE futures for Europeans, specifically VIX exposure (VXM futures vs. a VIX CFD). The consensus: futures are more cost-efficient (lower transaction, financing, and roll costs) when accessible, but CFDs fill a real gap for Europeans who can't easily trade US ETPs like VXX directly. Sam gave concrete numbers on the tradeoff — CFDs offer far more leverage (**up to 500x on stock indices** vs. ~20x at Interactive Brokers) and better cash-management flexibility (uninvested margin earns interest), plus a UK tax angle: spread bets (a CFD "wrapper") are tax-free in the UK, but CFDs held outside that wrapper are not and do attract capital gains tax.

**Sentiment:** Practical, consensus-driven — no strong disagreement, more a "here's the tradeoff matrix" conversation, useful reference for anyone routing VIX/vol exposure through European brokers.

**Pull quotes:**
- Sam: *"far more leverage (up to 500x for stock indices e.g. June S&P 500 compared to 20x with Interactive brokers)... if in the UK, TAX FREE"* [via spread-bet wrapper].
- Edux: *"futures tend to be more cost‑efficient because they offer lower costs per unit of risk across all major dimensions (transaction costs, financing, and roll costs)."*
- Sam (clarifying the tax point): *"Spread bets are CFDs wrapped in a spread bet wrapper and are tax free. CFDs not in a spread bet wrapper are not tax free."*

## 4. Portfolio construction: strategy allocation & seasonal/inactive strategies

Matt G asked how to handle a seasonally-inactive strategy (BOIL — natural gas ETF, winter-focused) in portfolio allocation when it's off-season: keep a notional allocation warm, or drop it entirely? Per MidKnight, pointing to Euan's portfolio construction webinar (~33min and ~1:01:00 marks), the answer is to **exclude inactive strategies entirely and treat them as a separate portfolio** rather than carrying a notional weight. Euan separately described his general allocation approach: start from whatever subset of strategies he wants live *right now*, then allocate across just those — the active set can be as small as 3 or as large as 8 strategies at different times.

**Sentiment:** Clear, resolved answer — a good concrete methodology note (exclude-when-inactive) worth remembering for any seasonal strategy.

**Pull quotes:**
- MidKnight: *"Euan says to exclude it when not active and thus it is to be treated as a separate portfolio than when it is active."*
- Euan: *"I start with a group of strategies that I want to trade right now, then allocate between those. Sometimes that is 8. Sometimes it is 3."*

## 5. QVR blowup / correlation surprise — the month's biggest discussion

Sparked by halves sharing Benn Eifert's (QVR Advisors) own debrief thread on X about a **28% drawdown this year** in a multi-strategy absolute-return program, cross-referenced against a related thread from David Orr describing a **~20% drawdown (-15% after [recovery/further loss, per context])** in his own hedge fund. The group's read: the core lesson wasn't leverage per se (though Marco/halves agreed leverage likely amplified the damage) but an **unexpected correlation breakdown** — previously uncorrelated positions/strategies became correlated simultaneously, and it caught the manager off guard despite being described as *not* a novel risk. Rachit connected this directly to the group's own practice: factor correlation risk into portfolio-level vol targeting rather than treating strategies as independent. -k offered a market-context read on the same period: a spike in momentum/buyback-premium factors, short-vol and low-vol strategies bleeding, and Japan underperforming the US. **Sentiment:** Serious, reflective — treated as a real risk-management case study, not schadenfreude; directly informs how the group thinks about correlation assumptions in their own vol-targeting/portfolio construction.
