# We Asked a $1 Billion Quant Manager Why Concentration Isn't a Warning — and Small Caps Aren't Dead

Detailed overview and analysis of the *Excess Returns* podcast featuring **Matt Zenz**, founder and Chief Investment Officer of Longview Research Partners, hosted by Jack Forehand. The episode was released on July 7, 2026.

---

### I. Executive Summary
Matt Zenz discusses evidence-based investing, explaining that factor premiums are fundamentally driven by discomfort: "no pain, no premium." He evaluates current market concentration, arguing that concentration alone does not forecast lower market returns. However, concentration exposes S&P 500 investors to heavy sector risk in technology/AI, which must be managed through global diversification (international and small caps). In factor research, while high capex (70-100% asset growth) typically penalizes expected returns (the "investment factor"), the massive AI capex budgets of trillion-dollar tech firms do not cross this threshold relative to their overall assets. Zenz outlines Longview's equity ETF strategy (**EBI**), highlighting how a smaller manager's trading agility avoids the "premium drag" of market impact that hampers trillion-dollar quants. He details why the small-cap premium is alive if filtered for "junk" (unprofitable firms and IPO lockups), and introduces Longview's fixed income ETF (**LVIG**), which rotates out of underlying ETFs before dividend dates to avoid forced distributions, eliminating the **1.6% annual tax drag** for high-income investors through tax-deferral.

### III. Detailed Key Takeaways

#### 1. No Pain, No Premium
Zenz emphasizes that evidence-based expected returns are fundamentally compensation for risk. Using Cory Hoffstein's phrase "no pain, no premium," he explains that if an asset or strategy is comfortable to hold, its expected return will be bid down. Volatility, downside drawdowns, and multi-year underperformance (such as value's 15-year underperformance prior to 2026) are the very catalysts that create future premiums. If investors panic and exit during uncomfortable periods, they fail to capture the premium.

> "If it didn't feel bad, if it wasn't painful to keep holding it, you wouldn't get rewarded with higher returns... that discomfort is what actually drives the return."
> — *Matt Zenz*

#### 2. The Investment Factor vs. AI Capex
In factor literature, the **Investment Factor** shows that companies with aggressive asset growth (70% to 100% year-over-year asset growth) tend to deliver significantly lower future expected returns. This is because capital is being reinvested in the business rather than distributed to shareholders, and management often overinvests at peak cycles. Zenz points out that while the dollar amount of AI capex ($600B+) is massive, for mega-cap tech (Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, Amazon) it represents a small percentage of their overall trillion-dollar balance sheets. Thus, they do not cross the factor threshold that penalizes future returns.

> "Companies that do a ton of investment tend to have lower returns... 70 to 100% growth in assets... A lot of these AI companies... they are investing a lot into capex... But as a percentage of their overall company, it's actually not that much... they aren't hitting that threshold."
> — *Matt Zenz*

#### 3. Trillion-Dollar Quant Premium Drag
Zenz highlights a structural advantage that smaller asset managers have over trillion-dollar quants (e.g., Dimensional Fund Advisors). Massive quant managers own 5% to 10% of the outstanding shares of smaller value companies. When a stock's characteristics change and the model dictates a sale, it can take these massive funds up to a year to slowly wind down the position to avoid market impact. A smaller manager (like Longview) needs to trade a tiny fraction (e.g., 0.05% of float) and can execute the trade in a single day. This trading agility has allowed Longview's EBI ETF to outperform comparable giant portfolios by **2.5%** over the first year and a half.

> "If you're a trillion dollar manager, you might own 5 to 10% of every small value company... it takes them a year [to sell]... Whereas, if you're a smaller manager... it takes a day... that's going to impact returns... we've outperformed a similar type portfolio by about 2.5% [in EBI]."
> — *Matt Zenz*

#### 4. The Small-Cap Premium and the Russell 2000 Junk Filter
Zenz argues that the small-cap premium is not dead, but it must be applied correctly. Small caps do not outperform simply because they are small; they outperform because they contain a higher density of extreme valuation spreads. However, the Russell 2000 contains a significant amount of "junk" (unprofitable biotech/tech, falling large caps, and recent IPOs). When investors buy the broad index, the junk drags down the return, making the small-cap premium disappear. Filtering out the unprofitable names and recent IPOs restores the premium. He notes that Longview waits **6 months** post-IPO (until lockups expire and float normalizes) before considering adding a company to their universe, which is why they are currently avoiding SpaceX.

> "They're not doing better because they are small. They're doing better because they have really good discount rates... when you factor out the 'junk' then the ones remaining do better... small cap value... outperforms because you can find more value nits."
> — *Matt Zenz*

#### 5. LVIG and the 1.6% Fixed Income Tax Drag
Traditional fixed income is highly tax-inefficient for taxable accounts. A high-income investor (40% marginal tax rate) holding a bond fund yielding 4% loses **1.6% annually** to ordinary income taxes. Over 20 years, this tax drag erodes nearly half of the compounded wealth.
*   **The LVIG Solution:** The Longview Advantage Fixed Income ETF (**LVIG**) seeks returns through NAV/capital appreciation rather than interest distributions.
*   **The Reinvestment Rotation:** LVIG holds low-cost underlying fixed income ETFs and rotates out of them just before their dividend distribution dates, reinvesting in other ETFs to avoid receiving the distribution.
*   **After-Tax Outperformance:** This process defers the tax until the investor sells the ETF, converting ordinary income taxes into lower capital gains rates. This strategy recaptures **50 to 100 basis points of annual after-tax outperformance** and lowers current-year taxable income, creating planning opportunities like Roth conversions or avoiding Irma surcharges.

> "A 4% [yield]... you're paying 1.6% a year in taxes... you're losing half the return to taxes... LVIG's goal... rather than receiving income, we want to capture the return through capital appreciation... We rotate between [ETFs]... by avoiding that dividend, we avoid getting that distribution... you control when you pay the tax."
> — *Matt Zenz*

### IV. Portfolio Positioning & Action Items

1.  **Tilt to Value and Profitability via Agile ETFs:** Allocate to rules-based value and profitability ETFs (like EBI) that utilize a discount rate framework, favoring managers small enough to avoid market impact trading drag.
2.  **Filter Small Caps for Profitability:** Avoid passive Russell 2000 index funds (`IWM`). Utilize active or rules-based small-cap strategies that screen out unprofitable firms and recent IPOs.
3.  **Defer Fixed Income Taxes in Taxable Accounts:** Replace traditional bond funds (`AGG`, `BND`) in taxable accounts with tax-deferred fixed income structures (like LVIG) to eliminate the 1.6% annual ordinary income tax drag.
4.  **Wait Out IPO Lockups:** Do not buy new IPOs (e.g., SpaceX) immediately. Wait at least 6 months for lockups to expire, allowing lockup sellers to normalize supply and establish true price discovery.
5.  **Utilize High Valuations for Financial Planning:** Do not use high S&P 500 valuations to time the market, but reduce expected returns to ~6% in long-term financial plans.
