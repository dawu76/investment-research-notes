# Wellington's Brij Khurana: AI Capex Crowding Out Bonds, the Wealth-Effect Inflation Thesis, and Why TIPS Are Underowned

Overview and analysis of the *Alpha Exchange* podcast featuring **Brij Khurana**, Fixed Income Portfolio Manager at Wellington Management (runs an unconstrained, go-anywhere bond strategy), hosted by **Dean Curnutt** (Founder, Macro Risk Advisors). Released October 1, 2026, runtime 54:51. Notes are built from the full YouTube caption transcript. ### I. Executive Summary

Khurana argues that two decades of near-zero and negative real rates failed to lift real (non-residential) investment but did financialize the economy, and that today's much higher rates are not slowing AI capex either, because hyperscalers, in his characterization, expect 30-40% returns that make a 6-8% borrowing cost beside the point. He reads current inflation as a wealth-effect, sticky-services phenomenon rather than a labor-market one, and the recent long-end selloff as a Fed repricing plus an AI real-growth story rather than a Treasury-supply or inflation-expectations story. He also sees growth headwinds building (tight policy plus an oil shock squeezing real wages, fiscal front-loading fading) and thinks markets are too optimistic on nominal growth.

His stated "three areas of excitement" are inflation-linked bonds (TIPS, "close to the top of the list"), non-US rates markets priced for hikes he considers mistakes, and EM local-currency bonds. He gives no explicit call on US Treasury duration: he says "a lot is priced in" to both 10s and 30s, while also arguing AI capex is structurally crowding bonds out.

### III. Detailed Key Takeaways

#### 1. Low Rates and Investment: Investment Has Decoupled From Rates, and Financialization Is the Cost
The Fed's models assume lower rates drive more non-residential investment. Khurana says that link has been weak since the dot-com crash: investment was muted in the 2000s (capital went abroad, to China and elsewhere), muted after the GFC (bank balance sheets were repairing), and the AI capex boom is happening despite rates he considers "really high." When cuts fail to stimulate investment, central banks that miss their inflation targets cut too far, and the cheap money goes elsewhere: household leverage in the early 2000s, buybacks and private-equity roll-ups after the GFC (which he says reduced competition and lowered the structural real rate), then government borrowing after Covid, which funded consumption rather than investment.

> "Interest rates in my mind are really high, and yet there is almost no correlation to the AI capex investment that we're seeing."
> "There's this lack of correlation between investment and interest rates [that] has caused central bankers, when they miss their inflation target, to drop rates too low. And then I would actually argue that increase in financialization has decreased the potential growth rate of the economy."
> Brij Khurana

If investment did not respond much to rates on the way down, he sees no reason it should respond much on the way up. That feeds the crowding-out argument in theme 6.

#### 2. QE's Balance-Sheet Channel Runs Backward
The conventional view is that QE lowers yields because the Fed's purchases lift bond prices, and stocks rose because discount rates fell. Khurana argues the opposite: large QE episodes coincided with rising yields, because a much larger balance sheet raises fears of eventual debt monetization, so investors sell bonds and buy reflationary assets such as stocks. He adds that the US (capital-markets-based) responded to QE and low rates with reflationary impulses, while bank-centric Europe saw negative rates squeeze bank profits and reduce lending.

> "Whenever they did the excessive amount of QE, yields would rise... when markets see a much larger balance sheet, they get very worried about the potential for debt monetization to take place at some point."
> "Ironically, QE in my mind causes bond yields to move higher because people sell bonds."
> Brij Khurana

He labels this a "relatively strange view." It is an argument from observed co-movement, and he offered no data in the interview.

#### 3. Inflation Is a Wealth-Effect Story, Not a Labor-Market Story
Khurana says core CPI excluding shelter (which he treats as closer to an asset price) has run close to 2% for three years. He argues wages are not the source of residual inflation: unemployment is low only because labor supply is weak while labor demand is also weak, which means little new nominal income. The Fed's 2022-23 hikes to roughly 5.25-5.5% already brought the labor market back into balance. The remaining inflation sits in sticky services (medical care, restaurants, travel) and relative-price distortions left by fiscal spending (used vs. new cars), tied to the wealth effect. His supporting claims, unsourced in the interview: baby boomers hold 50-60% of US wealth and allocate about 70-75% to stocks, and for the first time the country holds more wealth in stocks than in real estate.

> "That's where the inflation is, this almost like sticky services inflation, which is much more tied to the wealth effect than it is to just demand side labor inflation."
> Brij Khurana

His implication is that the policy rate is a poor tool for this kind of inflation and that the Fed's other tools (its balance sheet) are better matched, which ties to his unfinished comment on how Warsh is "changing the definition" of restrictiveness (he never elaborated). He also says the most desirable outcome would be real wage growth rising into current asset price levels.

Unreconciled in the interview: his ~2% (three-year average, ex-shelter) and the host's "4ish%" current inflation (see caveats). He did not dispute the host's figure.

#### 4. Growth Is More Fragile Than the Nominal-Growth Consensus
This theme was missing from the first version of these notes. Asked where the Fed stands, Khurana says there is a lot priced in already: about 100bp of further hikes toward a ~5% policy rate (host said "three-ish" additional tightenings), with Europe pricing roughly 80bp, and Australia, New Zealand and others also pricing coordinated tightening. He notes that only one hike has happened so far.

His framing: the two classic recession ingredients are tight monetary policy and commodity shocks that cut real wages, and "we're getting both." Consumer balance sheets are weaker than in 2022-23, and real wages excluding transfers are growing at a negative year-over-year rate, "very rare outside of recession." A year and a half of global loosening and low commodity prices supported growth, and both are reversing. He thinks "we saw the peak amount of nominal growth over the last year," that fiscal support was front-loaded by the "One Big Beautiful Bill" in the first half, and that a divided government after November could be a bond-market catalyst, since markets quickly price in less stimulus.

> "The two main reasons you cause a recession are tight monetary policy and... commodity price shocks that temporarily cause real wages to go down. And what's interesting to me is we're getting both of them."
> Brij Khurana

There is a tension he does not resolve: he says the recent rise in yields reflects an AI-driven real-growth story (theme 5), yet he also sees nominal growth peaking. The two can coexist if AI capex supports aggregate growth while consumers are squeezed, but he did not spell that out.

#### 5. The Curve: Fed Repricing, and a 10y10y Fair-Value Framework
The front end follows the Fed. The spot 2s10s curve has not inverted, but the forward curve one year out has, which he reads as the market saying that if the Fed follows through the curve will invert and policy will be tight. On the back end, he says the rise in yields "has been a repricing of the Fed," not Treasury supply and not inflation expectations (which he calls very low, assuming the Fed regains credibility).

His fair-value framework uses the **10-year, 10-year forward rate (10y10y)**, because it strips out the near-term Fed path, and says it should look like long-run nominal growth. He estimates about 0.5% population growth plus about 1% productivity gives roughly 1.5% real growth; add 2.5% inflation for about 4% nominal growth; add roughly 100bp of term premium because of higher debt than in the 1990s, for a fair 10y10y near 5% (captions are garbled here: "fiveish, 5 and a 4%"; I read it as about 5-5.25%). He says the 10y10y is "close to around 6.2%," though he hedged ("I have to check today"). That is roughly 100bp above his fair value.

> "Most of it has been a Fed repricing and real growth story emanating out of AI."
> Brij Khurana

Important clarification: the 100bp gap applies to the **10y10y forward**, not the spot 10-year. The wiki digests put spot 10Y at about 5.11-5.14% in late September, which sits inside his 5-5.25% fair-value range. So his framework does not say spot long Treasuries are 100bp too cheap or too rich on its own. He added that "a lot is priced into both" the 10s and 30s.

For a back-end rally on a Fed "mistake" signal, he points to the 2018 analog (a hawkish December 2018 Powell press conference, a 20%+ equity drawdown by the December 26 low, then a Fed U-turn). He says the stock market, not just bonds, has to push back, and as of the interview it was not.

#### 6. AI Capex Financing Looks Circular and Crowds Out Bonds in Two Ways
Khurana says some AI chip-financing deals look "fairly circular," recalling pre-GFC lending that "didn't look as much bank driven as it should have been." The Fed's apparent view is that lending outside the banking system is good because losses will not infect banks the way subprime did. He disagrees: bank loans to non-depository financial institutions (NDFIs) have grown by close to $1 trillion in six to seven years, so banks are effectively lending to private credit, which lends onward in "more questionable ways," attracted by favorable capital treatment.

**On the regulatory direction, his expectation is the opposite of what he thinks is right.** He expects the Fed to lower, not raise, the capital charge on these loans, because historical defaults have been low and the loans have low LTVs from the bank's perspective. He thinks the Fed has the capability to raise them and should, to bring the activity into the better-regulated banking system.

> "I think that actually the Fed is probably going to go the opposite way. They're going to reduce the capital ratio on these loans because historically they have had very low defaults."
> "The Fed has regulatory capability to raise those capital ratios and certainly bring it more in the banking system where you can at least have some amount of oversight over it."
> Brij Khurana

On crowding out, the host cited a viral JPMorgan Asset Management chart (rendered "Michael Symbolis" in captions; likely Michael Cembalest, my inference) comparing AI capex with Treasury borrowing, noting others dispute how it was calculated and say crowding out is small. Khurana answered that it is "absolutely" a big reason for the firmness of the back end, through two channels, but he offered no quantification:

> "Their belief is that the return on capital on AI is going to be 30-40%, and so whether or not it's 6% or 8% it doesn't really matter... obviously as a bondholder it really matters, because 200 basis points on a 30-year instrument or 20-year duration, whatever it is, is a lot of losses."
> "Companies are borrowing so much that there's no way that you're going to see a nominal growth slowdown. And so therefore bonds become even less attractive, because they're not going to hedge a slower growth environment."
> Brij Khurana

First, record investment-grade issuance by hyperscalers pressures other borrowers' funding costs. Second, AI-funded growth raises stock-bond correlation, so bonds lose value as a growth hedge and demand for them falls. Note that the 30-40% return is his characterization of what hyperscalers believe, not a sourced figure. The host added that a Goldman credit strategist sees plenty of capital available at a price, and observed that Meta, as the issuer, is effectively long a cheap option.

#### 7. Global Opportunities: Hiking "Mistakes" Abroad, Hedged Yields, and EM Local
Two areas. First, countries where markets price substantial hiking cycles that "almost" look like mistakes, which he compares to the ECB's 2008 hike. He names Australia and New Zealand: weak data, rising unemployment, inflation "temporarily sticky" but supply-side (oil) driven. The size of the priced-in hikes is garbled in the captions ("8,500 basis point"); elsewhere he gives about 80bp for Europe and 85-100bp for the BOE. Variable-rate mortgage markets mean fast pass-through to consumption (unlike the fixed-rate US), which is why over-hiking would bite and then reverse quickly. He also points to stronger federal balance sheets and steep yield curves abroad, so that "in some cases even when you hedge it back to dollars you get better yields than you can on treasuries or corporate bonds." He frames the expanded mandate to invest globally as a luxury that helps generate returns from here.

> "I think the economic data in countries like Australia and New Zealand is not good. Unemployment's rising. Inflation is temporarily sticky, but it's supply side driven like it is everywhere, in terms of oil prices."
> Brij Khurana

Second, EM local-currency bonds as a structural inflection: EM inflation is higher but supply driven; EM never ran the same fiscal expansion as developed markets; real policy rates are roughly 4-8% depending on the country; currencies look cheap in real terms; and several countries have moved in a market-friendly, lower-spending direction (Argentina, Colombia, Hungary, Chile; Brazil's election pending).

> "Last year EM local bonds were up almost 18%. Even this year, in a world where fixed income returns are flat to negative, EM local bonds are actually still higher on the year."
> "I think we're still early innings in the EM local bull market potentially, and investors are still not very heavily allocated to the space."
> Brij Khurana

(An earlier version of these notes merged those first two sentences into "up almost 18% even this year." The 18% is the 2025 return.)

#### 8. China: Low Yields, Balance-Sheet-Recession Dynamics
Five-year Chinese yields near 1.5% look low even against traditional safe-haven markets such as Swiss bonds, despite healthy nominal growth and an export-led boom. He reads the bond market as pricing a balance-sheet recession like the US and Europe after the GFC: housing deflation embedded in an opaque banking system and difficulty stimulating household consumption. He sees "not that much to do" there and says the rates still look too low to him even given that dynamic.

#### 9. Japan and the Yen: Bessent's Possible Bet on Ending the "One-Way Trade"
Khurana says the yen carry trade used to mean shorting yen to buy high-yield EM assets, but the money now goes mostly into US equities, not EM and not Treasuries in proportion to US debt growth (foreign Treasury holdings have fallen relative to total debt over the past decade). He says the August 2024 unwind correlated with US stocks far more than with bonds or EM, and that EM did fine in it. Japanese holders of US assets have been largely unhedged and have won twice (US stocks up, yen down), which suppressed demand for yen and Japanese bonds. This is **his inference** of Secretary Bessent's aim: make the yen trade less one-way (hedging costs make US bonds punitive for Japanese buyers if the yen stabilizes) so capital comes home to JGBs.

> "If they can stabilize the yen, the argument is then they would actually repatriate capital to the Japanese bond market... Now there is another side of that, which is if the Japanese are buying less US bonds someone else has to buy those bonds, and so I think there is a little bit of cognitive dissonance there."
> Brij Khurana

He concludes the bet is less about flows and more about stabilizing yields "at already high levels," and that Japan matters more for stocks now than for bonds. Both Japan and the UK have also cut long-end issuance, he says.

#### 10. UK vs. France
**UK:** positioning is cleaner than in the 2022 LDI episode, the UK has cut long-end issuance (about 6 to 12 months ahead of the US, he says), and it was one of the few countries that actively sold bonds in QT, which is no longer happening to the same degree. Even left-leaning UK governments are focused on being receptive to the bond market, unlike the US, which "continues to spend money regardless of the level of yields." The front end has a lot priced (BOE hikes of about 85-100bp), but he sees more reason for optimism on the back end than in recent years.

**France:** the OAT-Bund spread is the widest since before Draghi's 2012 "whatever it takes," and wider than 2011. He finds that understandable, because France had better debt metrics in 2011, was seen as a safe haven, and has since had a 15-year credit boom (bank lending and government borrowing) while Italy and Greece deleveraged. He rejects an Italy-2011 comparison (no euro-exit risk, more ECB tools) and says investors "shouldn't price in some of the tail risk scenarios."

> "France has actually gone through a bit of a credit boom over the last 15 years... it's not surprising to me that the spread is much higher than it was in 2011."
> Brij Khurana

#### 11. TIPS: Cheap Inflation Insurance, Underowned, With a Growth-Slowdown Caveat
His case: "we have much more inflation risk now than we've had over the last few decades," yet breakevens sit at about 2.3% across the curve, so "there isn't a lot of inflation risk premia in inflation linked bonds," while real yields are the highest "except for when the asset class was first created in the '90s," when a large liquidity premium was demanded. A low breakeven means inflation protection is cheap: TIPS beat a same-maturity nominal Treasury if realized inflation averages more than the breakeven.

> "It'd be silly not to say that we have much more inflation risk now than we've had over the last few decades. And yet... break evens are basically at 2.3% throughout the whole curve. And so there isn't a lot of inflation risk premia in inflation linked bonds."
> Brij Khurana

Why the market clears at 2.3% (his answer to the host): TIPS are less liquid than Treasuries and "trade like a risk asset when you need them" not to; they disappointed in 2022, when they outperformed nominals but still had negative total returns; investors have rotated away over time, so the asset class is underowned relative to real-yield levels.

Caveats he states himself:
* In a growth slowdown, "you're going to have wanted to own nominal bonds instead of inflation linked bonds"; breakevens would fall further. TIPS are "a little bit safer" given the current level of real yields, not a hedge against slower growth.
* The carry case is conditional: "if we keep realizing these levels of inflation given what's priced in," TIPS are a more attractive carry asset than credit with better liquidity.
* He ranks TIPS "close to the top of the list" and calls them one of three areas of excitement. He did not rank the three or forecast an inflation rate above 2.3%.

Cross-checks against the wiki: the breakeven arithmetic holds. [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]] has nominal 10Y at 5.11% and real 10Y at 2.76% after the September 23 move, a breakeven of about 2.35%. [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-20]] has 10Y TIPS at 2.68% and frames it as "Lehman-era real yields," so how close current real yields are to the 1990s launch-era peak, versus 2008-like levels, is Khurana's claim and is not quantified in the interview (the host's only level was the 3.5% 5-year TIPS yield at launch).

### IV. Portfolio Positioning & Action Items

1. **TIPS over nominals for core duration, with the condition attached.** Breakevens near 2.3% make inflation protection cheap, and the 2.3% number is consistent with the nominal-minus-real arithmetic in the wiki digests. The edge exists if realized inflation stays above the breakeven, and the host cited ~4% current inflation while Khurana cited ~2% core ex-shelter, so the inflation outlook is not settled by this interview. If you expect a growth slowdown, nominals hedge better.
2. **Build a global sleeve rather than a US-only bond allocation.** Look at markets such as Australia and New Zealand where priced-in hikes look excessive against weak data, and where variable-rate mortgages make over-tightening bite and reverse fast. Per Khurana, hedged-to-USD yields in some markets exceed Treasury and corporate yields.
3. **EM local-currency bonds as a multi-year structural position.** Real policy rates of roughly 4-8%, cheap real currencies, improving politics in several countries, +18% in 2025 and still positive in 2026, and light investor allocations.
4. **US long-duration Treasuries: no explicit call from Khurana. Do not read this interview as an underweight.** His 10y10y framework puts the forward rate about 100bp above fair value, while spot 10Y (about 5.1% in the wiki digests) is inside his 5-5.25% fair-value range, and he says a lot is priced into 10s and 30s. The negatives he does give are structural (AI-driven crowding out and higher stock-bond correlation). Net: mixed, undecided.
5. **Expect bonds to hedge growth scares less well while AI capex stays large and rate-insensitive.** Reassess nominal bond hedge ratios instead of assuming the historical stock-bond correlation regime.
6. **Regulatory watch: bank capital treatment of NDFI and private-credit loans.** Khurana expects the Fed to lower these capital charges, which would keep bank funding flowing to private credit and AI financing structures; he thinks they should raise them. A tightening would be the surprise.
7. **Differentiate within Europe.** UK back end looks better than in recent years (cleaner positioning, QT sales ended, fiscal discipline), but the front end already prices about 85-100bp of BOE hikes. France's wide OAT-Bund spread reflects a real credit-boom deterioration, not an Italy-2011 redenomination risk, so avoid pricing the tail case.
8. **Treat the yen as a cross-asset signal, mostly for US equities.** The 2024 carry unwind transmitted through US stocks. Bessent's possible aim of stabilizing the yen is Khurana's inference, and a successful push has a two-sided effect on future Treasury demand.
9. **Dated catalysts and indicators:** the November midterms and a possible divided government (less fiscal stimulus priced quickly); real wages excluding transfers (negative year over year); equity weakness as the trigger for a Fed "mistake" signal like December 2018; whether the Fed cuts bank capital charges on NDFI loans.
