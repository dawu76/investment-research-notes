## 1. Rates: The Auction Detail, the Buyback Counterfactual, and "Higher for Good"

* **The 5-year auction, in full (James Lavish, The Informationist, "Are We Witnessing a Bond Apocalypse?").** Treasury sold **$70B** of 5-year notes with a **3.1bp tail**, against an average of "just over half a basis point" across the last six 5-year auctions. **Bid-to-cover was 2.21, the lowest since December 2018. Indirect bidders took 54%, the smallest share in about five years, leaving dealers with almost 16%.** The stop-out was **5.033%, the highest 5-year auction yield since June 2006** ("the last time the government paid that much to borrow for 5 years, there was no such thing as an iPhone"). The 10-year closed that day at **5.11%, its highest since 2007**.
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-24]] had the **5.033%** yield and a tail of "about 3bp"; [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]] had the highest yield since June 2006. Lavish adds the demand split. The 7-year auction the next day "cleared the highest yield since that maturity was reintroduced in 2009" (Lavish); [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] had it as the highest since April 1993. Not a conflict: the 7-year was discontinued in 1993 and reintroduced in 2009, so both statements hold.
  * Why it matters: a tail measures price; bid-to-cover and the indirect share measure who showed up. A record-low indirect share with dealers absorbing about a sixth of the issue is what a supply problem looks like, as opposed to a pure inflation repricing. Lavish's read: "a little bit of both." The paywalled half asks "Who's going to buy all these bonds?" and notes that "on Thursday the Treasury did try buying some of them itself."
* **The week's levels.** The 10-year ended the week "around **5.18%**, roughly **24bp** higher on the week" (Laurentiu Chisca, Wall Street Trader). Top Traders Unplugged has the **2-year at 4.87%** and the **30-year up 19bp on the week to 5.50%**, with the dollar up about **1%** and gold down more than **2%**. Sharpe Two: TLT lost **another 2.85%** as the 30-year hit its highest since **2004**. Financial Samurai: "the 10-year bond yield surging to 5.2% last week."
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-26]] had Gayed's par curve (2-year **4.81%**, 10-year **5.17%**, 30-year **5.49%**) and Roberts's 10-year move from **5.01% to 5.18%**. The 2-year figures differ by 6bp (par yield vs. market quote). Wall Street Trader's +24bp weekly change is larger than the **17bp** implied by Roberts's 5.01% start; the windows likely differ.
* **The buyback counterfactual (Eric Wallerstein, Where's the Tail Wagging?, "Intervention Intentions").** Critics mock Treasury buybacks because the 30-year "hasn't been this high in decades." Wallerstein's answer is to look at what buybacks target. They concentrate in the **20-30 year sector**, and since they started the **10s30s curve is half as steep as it was earlier this year** and **30-year swap spreads have widened by 10bp**, while French and German 30-year swap spreads have tightened sharply over the past month. His estimate: "I don't doubt that the 30-year yield would be closer to **6%** had it not been for Treasury's action."
  * Why it matters: if he is right, the long end's fair value on fundamentals ("strong growth, a continued primary deficit, a more hawkish central bank, and high energy prices," with oil near **$100**) is about 50bp above the **5.50%** print, and the buyback program is the main thing holding it there. That raises the stakes on any change in buyback size.
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] had George Noble reading Bessent's doubling of buybacks as "a call to short more bonds." Wallerstein's framing is the opposite: markets are testing a revealed policy preference, and "fighting two sovereigns with very large balance sheets is a fool's errand" (he applies the same logic to the yen, §2).
* **"The easy money era" is over, in several voices.** QTR (Fringe Finance, "The Easy Money Fairy Tale Is About To End Violently"): "Higher rates restore consequences... Suddenly, the hurdle rate exists again," and "Cheap money does not merely inflate asset prices. It extends the expiration date on bullshit." His follow-up post: "If the bond market keeps moving in the direction it is moving... I think we are going to have an outright stock market slaughter," and "we are essentially seeing a bank run in private credit right now." Michael Green (Yes, I give a fig) pushes back on the moral framing: Howard Marks's memo quotes Stanley Druckenmiller describing a **5.5% 30-year yield** as an "invoice," but "the thirty-year Treasury does not know what Congress spent the borrowed money on."
  * Why it matters: Green's point is about method, not direction. A rising yield fits a fiscal-punishment story and a growth story equally well, and a model asked to explain one will supply the explanation (§13). The auction detail above is the kind of evidence that separates the two.
* **Research titles on the same question (via Top Traders Unplugged and The Idea Farm).** Goldman Sachs, "Competition for Capital" (AI investment and government borrowing competing for savings); Allianz Research, "How The AI Supercycle Is Reshaping Global Interest Rates"; AllianceBernstein's "The Everything Trade" (AI's capital demand linking asset classes and weakening diversification); Carlyle's "Rules of relevance." Steven Bavaria (Income Factory, on Adam Taggart's Thoughtful Money) argues higher yields are "a net positive for long-term holders because floating-rate holdings reprice quickly and fixed-rate holdings gradually roll into higher coupons."

## 2. Japan and the Yen: A $2 Trillion Carry Trade Under a 160 Ceiling

* **The size of the trade (Adam Tooze, Chartbook, quoting the FT).** The current value of the yen carry trade "may far exceed **$2tn**, making it probably the biggest it has ever been," regulators heard in Tokyo; the worry at the top of their list is that "the cheap yen may be anchoring US Treasuries and may have helped inflate a bubble in AI-related shares." Until this month the BoJ's benchmark rate "had not risen above **1 per cent** since **1995**"; Japan raised it to **1.25%**. The FT's broader point is who uses the trade now: alongside hedge funds and "Mrs Watanabe" retail investors, "hundreds of major companies" funding a **$2tn** overseas transformation "much of it with cheap yen." Mizuho's Nakajima: "Japan is by far the biggest source of foreign direct investment into the US. So if those flows reversed, it would probably have a significant effect."
  * Bessent's warning last month, quoted in the piece: "disorderly yen markets can trigger forced unwinds, which could destabilise global markets," the justification for a joint Japan-US intervention at the end of July after the yen hit a **40-year low**. The 2024 unwind, which followed the BoJ's first move above zero, "helped send global markets into a tailspin."
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-22]] had the BoJ's hike to **1.25%** on a 7-2 vote. [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-26]] had TSCS Research's BIS figures (a surprise 25bp BoJ tightening lifts the yen about 10% when shorts are large) and speculators unwinding about **120,000** of **160,000** contracts in the first week of August. The FT's $2T is a gross estimate of all carry use, not speculative positioning, so the two describe different layers of the same exposure.
* **Why the yen stays near 160 despite intervention (Wallerstein).** After "several sizable FX interventions, as well as coordinated rate checks and some buying from the US side," the yen "remains close to **160**," and each rally pares back. His read: Tokyo does not want a strong yen. PM Takaichi's government are "reflationistas" competing to reindustrialize, so interventions are "biding time until energy prices fall. While USDJPY **165** is untenable, going back to **115** probably isn't aligned with their broader goals." Positioning has flipped: calls for "USDJPY 170" have been "doused," longs "have mostly vanished," and risk reversals show options investors "the most bearish USDJPY (aka, bullish yen) as they've been in a long time."
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-25]] had the yen bouncing **0.6% to 157.95** after Trump raised yen weakness with Japan; Wallerstein places that conversation on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly.
  * Why it matters: options pricing for a stronger yen plus a record-size carry trade is the combination the FT's regulators fear. A yen spike forces carry holders to sell foreign assets, and the FT names Treasuries and AI shares as the assets the cheap yen has supported. QTR lists a paywalled piece, "Japan's Life Insurers Look Like Silicon Valley Bank And 2008 AIG Combined," on the same exposure.
* Cross-reference: [[yen-carry-trade-unwinding-2025]] for the 2024-25 unwind mechanics.

## 3. Cash and the Consumer: Two Bear Arguments Gayed Says Are Out of Date

* **The $8 trillion "sideline cash" (Michael A. Gayed, The Lead-Lag Report, "The Eight Trillion Dollar Myth").** Money market fund assets reached **$8.44T in Q2 2026** (Fed Z.1), up from **$4.73T in January 2020** and **$7.48T in Q2 2025**; ICI's weekly count was **$7.94T** for the week ended September 23, up **$15.0B** on the week. Fund assets are **up 48% since the start of 2023 while the S&P 500 nearly doubled**.
  * **The RRP test.** The Fed's overnight reverse repo facility drained from **$2.55T at its December 30, 2022 peak to under $1B on September 25, 2026**, as funds rotated into bills, and "total fund assets never flinched." Gayed: "If this were sideline money waiting for the right moment, the moment to move was somewhere during that drain."
  * **Why it stays:** the **3-month bill yields 4.24% and the 1-year 4.50%** against a **3.75-4.00%** fed funds range; "Cash leaves when the yield leaves, not when the narrative does." The counterpoint he grants: a **10%** reallocation is more than **$800B**, but the trigger would be front-end yields falling "toward 3% and below," and even then the first move would be into short Treasuries and IG, not equities.
  * Different measure: the Z.1 total ($8.44T) and ICI's weekly count ($7.94T) use different definitions; Gayed cites both.
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-26]] had Gayed's same 4.24%/4.50% bill and 1-year yields and Mauldin's money-fund range of **3.5-3.7%**. Financial Samurai today puts money-market rates at "only about **3.65%**" and is raising cash "from all-in **0%** of portfolio to **6%**" while considering a bond ladder. Why it matters: with the RRP at zero, money-fund growth now depends on bill supply alone, which ties the "cash pile" to Treasury's issuance mix (§1) rather than to equity sentiment.
* **The consumer credit cycle already turned (Gayed, "The Delinquency Cycle Already Turned").** The commercial bank card delinquency rate (FRED DRCCLACBS) peaked at **3.22% in Q2 2024, the highest since 2011**, after eleven straight quarterly increases from a trough near **2.1%** in 2021. Gayed's quarterly path since the peak: **3.20** (Q3 2024), **3.08** (Q4), then **3.05, 3.04, 2.99, 2.95, 2.91** (Q1 2026) and **2.85% in Q2 2026**.
  * Data note: Gayed calls Q2 2026 "a fifth consecutive quarterly decline," but his own series falls in every quarter since the Q2 2024 peak (eight in a row). Either count supports the same direction.
  * **What did not change:** card balances hit a record **$1.263T in Q2 2026, up $54B** year over year; average card rates hit a record **21.59%** at the end of the Fed's series (February 2024); unemployment drifted to **4.1%**. Total household debt fell **$13B to $18.8T**, "the first quarterly decrease in the current series." The flow into 90+ day card delinquency was **6.97% annualized vs. 6.93% a year earlier**: "Elevated, yes. Deteriorating, no."
  * **The mechanism:** banks cut limits and closed accounts at the risky end in 2023-24, so "the healing was engineered in part by exclusion," and the labor market never produced the income shock delinquency models assume.
  * **His caveats:** 2.85% is still above the **2019 average of 2.59%**; serious auto delinquency rose to **3.00%, up 7bp** year over year; and "a delinquency cycle that ended at 4.1% unemployment would restart quickly at 5%."
  * Continuity: [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-26]] had Lance Roberts rebutting a viral "worst since 2008" card-delinquency claim with New York Fed data; Gayed supplies the series. The same digest had Freddie Mac multifamily delinquencies at **0.64%**, above the 2011 peak, and one in five renters paying late. Taken together, stress has moved from revolving credit (healing) to rent and multifamily (worsening) and autos (edging up).
  * Why it matters: consumer-stress positioning "has been priced against a deterioration that stopped in mid-2024," so Gayed sees the surprise risk running "toward resilience," with the marginal borrower "one payroll shock from relapse." That makes Friday's payroll print (§4) the swing factor for this argument too.
* Sources: Michael A. Gayed (The Lead-Lag Report, 2 dispatches), Financial Samurai.

## 13. Global Equities and Process Notes

* **Indonesia (Michael Fritzell, Asian Century Stocks, "Hidden Champions of Indonesia").** The index trades at **9.3x** P/E, "one of the lowest multiples in the world," on an aggregate market cap of about **$600B**; consumer stocks' P/Es fell from about **30x to 10x** over ten years. The political risk: all **889** SOEs moved into the Danantara fund reporting to President Prabowo, "Patriot Bonds" at **2%** sold to select tycoons, a planned export monopsony for coal and palm oil, and central government spending **+29%** in H1 2026. MSCI may downgrade Indonesia to frontier at its **November 2026** review; the OJK is raising minimum free float to **15%**. Fritzell sees expropriation risk highest in license-based sectors (plantations, coal) and lower in consumer names.
* **Other regions (The Idea Farm).** Deutsche Bank: the US-Europe forward P/E gap has narrowed **from 9.0x to 4.7x** and Europe's rerating "has further to run." Citi: Latin America has "its best growth setup in decades." Dave Iben (Meb Faber): "The World Is on Sale While America Trades at a Premium."
* **Judgment and models (Michael Green).** When a model blocks a legitimate transaction "the loss tends to be charged to the control"; when a person overrides a correct warning, "the loss attaches to a person." That asymmetry, plus cheap AI search of every record, pushes institutions toward obedience: "If I follow the machine and the result is bad, the system failed. If I override the machine and the result is bad, I failed." His LLM test: asked why the bond selloff proves debt/GDP is too high, and why high stocks prove conditions are easy, the model argued both, because "I had supplied the causality in my framing of the question." "The model has not independently discovered my judgment; it has made my judgment sound independently discovered."
  * Why it matters: it is a direct warning about using AI summaries (including this digest) to settle causal questions like §1's "supply vs. inflation" debate.
* **RW Pro on research vs. operations (Kris Longmore).** Only **8 of 212** published anomalies have a negative mean return over their full history; the median effect drops from about **0.7%** a month before publication to about **0.3%** after; and five principal components explain half the variance of **158** anomalies, where independence would need **48**. Most are equal-weighted deciles with no size screen or costs. His "policy layer" example: a **$250k** account giving a strategy with **8.84%** native vol **2%** of portfolio risk holds about **22.6%** of native size.
* **AI and work (Stephen Clapham, Behind the Balance Sheet, on Daniel Susskind).** The Stanford "Canary in the Coal Mine" paper finds no widespread displacement, but **22-25 year-olds** in AI-exposed jobs face a **19%** employment shortfall, driven by lower hiring rather than layoffs. In Korea about **60%** of the unemployed have a degree. Susskind proposes a "with AI" and "without AI" paper in every subject, like the calculator debate. Clapham's premium pick is a physical-infrastructure company at **12x** earnings whose majority holder has taken a similar business private before (name paywalled).
* **Process reminders.** Daniel Rasmussen's *The Humble Investor* (via Top Traders Unplugged): opportunities come "when other investors have become too confident in a particular view of the future." Kaastrup-Larsen's question for portfolios: AI-supplier scarcity can raise the inflation that makes bonds a weaker hedge, so "if you had to justify each holding's job again today, which would be hardest to defend?"
