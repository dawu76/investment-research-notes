# UBP: Financing the AI Build-out (September 16, 2026)

Author: Filipe Alves da Silva, Head of Fixed Income Advisory & Strategy, Union Bancaire Privée. 27-page PDF; most tables sourced to CreditSights (Aug 20, 2026), Morgan Stanley, JPM and Bloomberg. Written from a bondholder's seat, which matters: the question is "do I get par back", not "does AI win". ^[ubp]

## The argument in four lines

1. The five hyperscalers (MSFT, AMZN, GOOGL, META, ORCL) now spend more than they generate: ~$820B capex vs ~$750B operating cash flow in 2026, $1.0-1.3T capex vs ~$900B in 2027.
2. Balance sheets are not the constraint. The four AA-or-better names can add ~$400B of debt in 18 months without a downgrade, ~$600B before single-A. The constraint is the *price* the bond market charges.
3. The risk sits in the order book, not the borrowers: $2.3T of cloud backlog, ~40% owed by OpenAI and Anthropic, two private companies.
4. The bear case is dated: signed leases and take-or-pay contracts start billing in 2027-28 whatever lab revenue is by then, while capex growth decelerates (UBP: +111% 2026, +45% 2027, +11% 2028).

## Takeaways for investors

1. **Where losses would land.** An overbuild "destroys the marginal builder and the operator that borrowed against a single contract", not an AA company with a second business. The ranking of fragility runs: unguaranteed single-site HY vehicles and neoclouds > private credit construction loans > Oracle > Meta > the other three. This matches the conclusion in [[ai-compute-commencement-wall-and-refinancing-trap]] (solvency risk for neoclouds and private credit, multiple risk for tier-1 hyperscalers).
2. **For hyperscaler shareholders the risk is margin, not default.** UBP expects lab payment shortfalls to show up as "renegotiation dressed as partnership": deferrals called rephasing, and sub-leased capacity hitting a market sold as scarce.
3. **Equity and credit are one trade.** ~35% of 2026 funding came from equity, and the group holds $600B+ of stakes in OpenAI, Anthropic and SpaceX that could be sold. If the equity de-rates, the bond market must fund a bigger gap just as its appetite is weakest. Six of these names are ~17% of MSCI ACWI, so most portfolios already own the equity that would need to be sold.
4. **Oracle is the pivot credit.** BBB-, 4.3x lease-adjusted leverage, FCF -$24B in FY26, roughly half its backlog owed by one loss-making customer (OpenAI). It is the one name where a lab renegotiation reaches the rating, and a downgrade to HY would trigger forced selling by holders with index caps.
5. **OpenAI exposure is mostly indirect.** It arrives through ORCL, CRWV and project paper written against sites built for it. Anthropic's structures put an IG balance sheet (Alphabet guarantee of Fluidstack leases, Broadcom backstop) between lender and lab; OpenAI's do not.
6. **The underlying demand signal.** Microsoft's backlog grew 84% y/y, but only 25% excluding OpenAI, close to its historical rate. That is the cleanest single data point in the report for separating organic demand from lab commitments.
7. **What to watch (UBP's list):** the concession on the next jumbo deal (UBP weights this most), Microsoft's first bond in nine years, the Anthropic listing documents, Oracle's $20B at-the-market programme. Issuance should pause in H2 2026 and resume in H1 2027.

## Checking the claims

### Internal arithmetic

- **Off-balance-sheet total checks out.** 1,164 + 1,569 + 41 + 75 = $2,849B. On-balance-sheet debt + leases = $853B. Ratio 3.3x, matching "more than three times".
- **Backlog total checks out.** 496 + 684 + 520 + 638 = $2,338B.
- **The ~40% lab share checks out from UBP's own counterparty table.** OpenAI at Oracle/MSFT/AMZN (300 + 250 + 138 = $688B) plus Anthropic at GOOGL/AMZN/MSFT (200 + 100 + 30 = $330B) = ~$1.02T, or ~44% of backlog. Caveat: some announced commitments (e.g. MSFT's "incremental" $250B) may not yet be booked in RPO, so the true share could be lower.
- **Interest is not the constraint: checks out.** $600B at ~5% ≈ $30B a year vs ~$790B EBITDA (under 4%).
- **Cumulative capex does not reconcile.** Page 4 says the five spend "USD 6.5 trillion" between now and 2030; page 24 says "USD 5.6 trillion" over 2026-30. Applying UBP's growth path to $820B gives ~$1.19T in 2027 and ~$1.32T in 2028. Getting to $5.6T then requires capex to *fall* to ~$1.1T a year in 2029-30, which the report never says. $6.5T implies ~$1.6T a year, a re-acceleration. Likely a digit transposition, but the path is unexplained either way.
- **Oracle's index-cap headroom looks overstated.** UBP says Oracle is "some USD 60 billion" of issuance from the 1.5% cap for low-IG names. Oracle has ~$130B of debt at a 1.17% index weight, which caps the index at about $11T. On that base, the 0.33pp gap is roughly $35-40B, not $60B. If so, forced-selling mechanics are closer than the report implies. (My calculation; depends on how much of Oracle's debt is in the index.)

### Against other data in the wiki

- **Off-balance-sheet figures agree with Burry**, but not independently. Burry's per-company numbers (MSFT >$300B uncommenced leases vs UBP $329B; Meta ~$700B vs $737B; Alphabet ~$900B vs $977B; Amazon ~$267B vs $285B) line up closely. Both come from the same filings, so this confirms the arithmetic, not the interpretation. See [[burry-ai-capital-cycle-oracle-jupiter-2026-09]].
- **Guarantees are probably undercounted.** UBP tabulates $41B (Meta RVGs) + $75B (Alphabet) + $29B (Broadcom) because other companies do not quantify them. The FT (via [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-23]]) puts residual value guarantees issued by Big Tech at up to $300B in under a year. UBP itself calls guarantees "the smallest and the most dangerous" category. The most dangerous category is also the least measured.
- **Oracle stress arrived after publication.** On Sept 24, eight days later, Oracle sent a force majeure notice on the 2.45 GW Project Jupiter site; ~$18B of Jupiter loans traded at 89-91 cents and Oracle CDS hit a record. This is the construction-delay risk UBP describes for vehicles where "a delay means no rent and the debt is serviced from reserves". It supports UBP's view that Oracle is the weak link.
- **OpenAI's burn may be higher than UBP uses.** UBP: >$200B through 2029. Leaked planning documents (MBI Deep Dives, in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-19]]) show a revision from $180B to $278B through 2030. Different horizons, so not a direct contradiction, but the revisions are going up.
- **Anthropic IPO timing may slip.** UBP treats an October listing as a near-term watch item. The Sept 19 digest reports a second IPO delay, and the Sept 23 digest notes the IPO window narrowing (SB Energy slowed a ~$50B IPO). The "first audited view of a lab" may come later than UBP expects.
- **Anthropic's profit is adjusted.** UBP says "adjusted operating profit". The Sept 19 digest has it as positive operating income excluding stock-based compensation in Q2, tracking to GAAP profit in Q3. ARR of $65B is a July exit rate; recognized Q2 revenue annualizes to ~$46B.
- **Power as the binding constraint is well supported.** About $130B of US data center projects blocked or delayed by local opposition this year ([[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-22]]), and $374B+ of construction in progress across hyperscalers and neoclouds (Zitron). Delays push the commencement wall later, which helps the labs' cash flow but hurts single-site vehicles that get no rent until completion.
- **Chip residual values: undecided.** UBP: A100 rental rates rose and H100s re-contracted at ~90% of original rates. Oracle says 4-year-old GPUs re-let at a 20% premium. Against that, SemiAnalysis: "no one will take on the risk of assessing a residual value on 4, 5, or 6 year old GB300's today" (Sept 23 digest). UBP concedes every bullish data point "was gathered amid a shortage".

### Methodological points

- **Backlog-to-lease coverage (1.5x-4.5x) is less comforting than it looks.** It sets gross contracted revenue against building lease payments only. Chips and servers are ~60% of site cost (UBP's own figure) and are not in the denominator, nor are power and operating costs. Oracle's 2.0x coverage sits next to FCF of -$24B. A measure of backlog against total cost to deliver would be much thinner.
- **"20bp on a 5.5% coupon changes very little" is true for the issuer, not the holder.** On a 2063 bond with ~15 years of duration, 20bp of widening is a ~3% price loss. With the 10-year at its highest close since 2007 (Sept 23 digest), long AI paper carries both spread and rate duration.
- **Purchase commitments ($1.57T, 55% of the $2.9T) overlap with capex forecasts.** Adding them to capex projections double counts. UBP ranks them the least alarming for this reason.
- **Reclassifying take-or-pay as a loan book is the report's strongest idea.** Rating agencies have capitalised take-or-pay in pipelines, shipping and power for 30 years but not yet for compute. If they start, lab leverage looks very different, and so does the ~$1.25T of commitments against ~$105B of combined ARR (about 12x).

## Historical comparisons

| Episode | Scale | Financing share | Outcome | What carries over |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **UK railway mania, 1840s** | Investment peaked at ~7% of GDP (Odlyzko) | Equity-led, partly-paid shares | Average dividends 1.83% in 1850 vs 10% expected; the network survived | Largest tech mania by GDP share. AI capex is ~2.6% of US GDP (my estimate: $820B on ~$31T), large but well below this |
| **Telecom/fiber, 1996-2002** | US telecom capex ~$120B in 2000 (~$213B today), ~1.2% of GDP | Telecom 5% → 23% of HY 1995-99 (UBP); ~1/3 of the HY index by 2000 (AllianceBernstein) | Moody's HY default rate peaked at 10.7% in Jan 2002, driven by telecom; <5% of fiber laid was ever lit | The glass lasted decades and demand grew into it, but the borrowers were gone first. GPUs last 3-7 years, so idle capacity expires instead of waiting |
| **Shale/energy E&P, 2009-2017** | Energy 10% → 17% of HY 2009-14 (UBP) | HY and reserve-based loans | Double-digit sector defaults 2015-16 and 2020 | Theme-driven access to HY for issuers that normally couldn't get it |
| **Largest single IG issuers** | Ford 4.51% of IG index (2001), GE 3.30% pre-GFC, average 2.6% since 1997 | | Both later downgraded or restructured | Amazon today is 1.20%, so no single-name crowding yet |
| **Sector crowding in IG** | Banks 26% of IG pre-GFC, utilities 10-15% | | | AI at 4.6-8.0% is large and rising (+2.2pp in two years), but not extreme |
| **US 5G build-out** | Carriers spent to defend share | IG | Connected-device revenue never came | Hyperscalers may earn their return as defended share, not new revenue |

**A pattern worth tracking.** Brookfield's study of the three sector default cycles above 10% since 1997 (telecom 1998-2004, homebuilders 2006-11, energy E&P 2014-17) finds the same three warning signs each time: a capex ramp, growth of B- and lower issuance, and negative free cash flow, with defaults spiking about three years later. Today: the capex ramp is here; negative FCF arrived in 2026 at Meta, Oracle, Amazon (partly) and Alphabet (one quarter), and deeply at the labs and neoclouds; low-rated issuance is growing (AI ~4.5% of HY, CoreWeave B-rated) but much of it sits in private credit outside the index. Three years from 2026 is 2029, which lines up with UBP's 2027-28 commencement wall and Burry's 2028-29 write-off window.

**How this cycle differs.** The central borrowers are AA/AAA with large non-AI cash flows, which was not true of WorldCom or Global Crossing. Current utilization is high (rental rates on old GPUs rising), where fiber was mostly dark. The risk has moved into structures (SPVs, guarantees, take-or-pay) that indices and rating agencies do not yet fully count.

## Comparison to Brookings' "Financing the AI Buildout" (Van Nieuwerburgh, Sept 2026)

A second paper with the identical title was released the same month: Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh (Columbia Business School), "Financing the AI Buildout," BPEA conference draft, September 24-25, 2026 (full write-up in [[brookings-financing-ai-buildout-van-nieuwerburgh-2026-09]]). Same title and general subject, but a different genre of document. It is not a duplicate, and the two are complementary rather than redundant. Full PDF read directly (not secondhand summary). ^[brookings]

**Genre difference.** Brookings is an academic paper built on original models: a capacity-pipeline estimate from a proprietary project-level database (Cleanview, 2,972 projects, ridge-regression imputation for missing capacity, R²=0.846) and a formal "revenue required to earn a normal return" calculation (Appendix D). UBP is a sell-side-style credit note assembled from third-party data (CreditSights, Morgan Stanley, JPM, Bloomberg) into a company-by-company bond-market fragility ranking. Brookings asks "does the capex make economic sense"; UBP asks "do I get par back, and who loses first if not."

**Where they corroborate each other:**
- **Same central case study.** Both use Meta's Hyperion/Beignet JV with Blue Owl as the template financing structure. Brookings: $27B Beignet debt, A+ (one notch below Meta), 90% debt-to-asset, 1.12x debt-service coverage, issued at 6.58% (>100bp over what Meta's own unsecured debt would likely cost, implying >$5B of extra interest over the life of the deal). UBP: $27.3B, T+225, A+. Independent near-exact match. Both also cite the related Sopaipilla (Meta/BlackRock) JV (Brookings $12.3B bonds July 2026; UBP $12.6B, T+288).
- **Same capex/cash-flow pivot.** Brookings: 5-hyperscaler capex $800.5B vs. $707.1B operating cash flow in 2026 (2026 is the first year capex exceeds OCF). UBP: ~$820B vs. ~$750B. Same story, slightly different guidance vintage.
- **Similar off-balance-sheet magnitude.** Brookings cites a WSJ tally of $2.4T off-balance-sheet for 4 hyperscalers ($904B future leases + $1.52T purchase commitments, mostly chips) against $604B on-balance-sheet ($356B long-term debt + $248B current lease obligations) (roughly 4x; "only one-fifth of the $3T in datacenter-related obligations are currently recognized"). UBP: ~$2.9T off-balance-sheet vs. ~$0.9T on-balance-sheet, roughly 3.3x. Same order of magnitude and the same structural point, reached independently.
- **Same conclusion on where losses land.** Both put the investment-grade parents themselves well down the risk list; both locate the real fragility in project-level SPVs, single-tenant vehicles, and chip-collateral structures where reported (low) hyperscaler leverage obscures much higher asset-level leverage.
- **Same historical analogies** (railway mania, telecom/fiber), though Brookings sources its comparisons far more rigorously, Cranmer (1960) for canals, Gallman for railroads, Ulmer for electrification, BEA national accounts for telecom, each converted to a harmonized %-of-GDP series (Table 1: canals 0.66%, railroads 2.24%, electrification 0.50%, highways 1.13%, telecom 1.10%, AI buildout 3.63%).

**Where they diverge:**
1. **Only Brookings computes what has to be true for the buildout to pencil out.** This is Appendix D and the paper's real contribution. Capital base at commissioning for the 182.7GW built by 2032: **$9.61T**. At a **10% required unlevered return**, with IT equipment (68% of cost) given a **6-year economic life** and buildings/power (32%) a **20-year life**, the weighted annual capital-recovery charge is **19.37% of the capital base** → **$1.86T required annual operating cash flow**. At a **50% operating cash-flow margin**, that is **$3.725T of mature annual revenue by 2032** (9.2% of that year's GDP), against OpenAI + Anthropic's combined ~$100B today, implying **~80%/year revenue growth sustained for ~8 years**. Sensitivity: $2.29T required revenue at a 5% return/67% margin, up to $6.72T at 15% return/33% margin; if GPU economic life is 3 years instead of 6, required revenue rises to $6.0T (14.8% of GDP). In pricing terms: $5.5/GPU-hour at full utilization, $6.9 at 80%, $7.9 at 70%, which the paper calls "not implausible" against today's $6-10/hour market rates, reframing the real uncertainty as utilization and pricing power holding up across ~180GW of new supply, not the headline price being absurd. **UBP has no equivalent calculation**: its closest analogue is the qualitative ~12x commitments-to-ARR ratio (§Methodological points above) and spread-widening as a market signal, not a built-up return model. This is the single biggest gap in UBP relative to Brookings, and the reason this comparison section exists: a bondholder's-eye view (UBP) doesn't answer whether the thing being financed can work; Brookings' framework does, and can be applied to UBP's own figures.
2. **Granularity is inverted.** UBP gives company-by-company lease-adjusted leverage (MSFT 0.6x, ORCL 4.3x), backlog, capex/revenue, and lab-commitment tables, exactly what a credit analyst needs, which Brookings doesn't provide (it treats "the five hyperscalers" in aggregate except for the Hyperion case study). Brookings gives the macro capacity/investment pipeline model UBP doesn't attempt.
3. **Findings present only in Brookings, absent from UBP:**
   - Data-center REIT beta with the S&P 500 rose from **~0.5 to ~1** since 2018 (Figure 3); their exposure to the non-AI component of the S&P 500 is now comparable to their exposure to AI stocks. Data centers have stopped behaving like a diversifying/defensive holding.
   - A **$500B NVIDIA-backstopped credit facility** (Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, KKR; Aug 2026) in which NVIDIA provides a 25% residual-value guarantee (up to $125B exposure), flagged explicitly as vendor-financing circularity, the chip supplier backstopping its own customers' purchases of its chips. A parallel Google/Broadcom TPU financing arrangement is also noted. UBP's chip-backed-debt section (CoreWeave TL4/TL5, Broadcom AI XPV) covers adjacent but distinct structures, not this facility.
   - The $10.3T headline figure **excludes future GPU refresh cycles**: 2025-26 vintage chips likely need replacing by ~2030 even though the buildings/power infrastructure remains usable, so the true lifecycle capex is understated.
4. **"% of GDP" figures are not apples-to-apples.** Brookings' 3.63% is the paper's rigorously-derived central estimate, averaged 2025-2032, covering the full buildout (data centers + power + IT equipment/chips). UBP's page in this wiki carries a 2.6% figure, but per the arithmetic check above that was *this wiki's own estimate* from UBP's single-year 2026 hyperscaler capex ($820B / ~$31T GDP), not a figure UBP's report itself states as a GDP share, and not measuring the same thing (single-year hyperscaler capex vs. multi-year full-buildout average). The two numbers should not be quoted against each other without this caveat.
5. **Stance.** Brookings is deliberately non-committal: "it would be premature to conclude AI infrastructure already poses systemic risk," closing with a call for better measurement and disclosure rather than a directional view. UBP is willing to rank names by fragility (unguaranteed single-site HY vehicles/neoclouds > private credit construction loans > Oracle > Meta > the other three) and name specific near-term watch items (next jumbo-deal pricing concession, Microsoft's first bond in nine years, Anthropic listing documents, Oracle's ATM programme).

**Net read.** The two papers are not competing accounts of the same event. They corroborate each other on the facts they share (Hyperion/Beignet economics, the 2026 capex/OCF crossover, the ~3-4x off-balance-sheet multiple) while answering different questions. Brookings supplies the "does this pencil out" framework this wiki's earlier coverage of UBP was missing; applying its 10%-return/50%-margin required-revenue test to UBP's own $2.3T backlog and ~$105B combined lab ARR figure (§Methodological points, "Reclassifying take-or-pay as a loan book") makes UBP's ~12x commitments-to-ARR ratio look less like an informal red flag and more like a specific, methodologically-grounded distance from the $3.7T annual-revenue bar Brookings computes as the requirement for the buildout as a whole.

## Verdict

The report's core claims hold up: the arithmetic on balance sheets, off-balance-sheet obligations and backlog reconciles, and events since publication (Oracle's Jupiter notice, rising OpenAI burn estimates) support its risk map rather than contradict it. The weak spots are a cumulative capex figure that doesn't reconcile, a probably overstated Oracle index-cap buffer, undercounted guarantees, and a coverage metric that leaves out chip costs. All of these point the same way: the report is, if anything, slightly too comfortable about Oracle and the guarantee layer. The main conclusion (hyperscaler IG is safe at wider spreads; the fragile layer is leveraged single-contract structures) is consistent with this wiki's own [[ai-compute-commencement-wall-and-refinancing-trap]] assessment.
