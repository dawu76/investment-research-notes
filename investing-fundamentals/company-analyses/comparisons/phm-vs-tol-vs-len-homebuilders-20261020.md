# Homebuilders at the Bottom of the Cycle: $PHM vs. $TOL vs. $LEN, plus $OC

**Prices:** PHM ~$112 and TOL ~$134 on October 7 (Barron's); LEN ~$76 and PHM ~$111 around October 19 (Morningstar quotes via search, approximate). OC ~$114 (Barron's).
**Latest reported quarters:** PHM June 2026 (Q3 due late October); LEN August 2026 (fiscal Q4 due December); TOL fiscal Q3 (July 2026).
**Built from** the three articles in `sources`, the two earnings releases, and the rates data in [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-26]] and [[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-27]]. Ratios marked "this page" are new arithmetic.

## Bottom line

* **Not yet for the group.** Even the analyst with Outperforms on PHM and TOL (Stephen Kim, Evercore ISI) says "I don't think this is the right time to buy this group," and may change his view "in the next six months or so." The October NAHB data deteriorated after his interview.
* **PHM is the builder to accumulate early,** but as a multiple re-rating on flat-to-lower earnings, not an earnings-growth story. Size it for a 2027 EPS decline.
* **LEN: hold or avoid on a 12-month view.** The floor at tangible book (~$78, Evercore) is near the price, so downside is limited, but there is no catalyst and land-option financing adds a margin headwind specific to Lennar.
* **Balance sheets (§6):** PHM (net debt ~3% of capital, nothing due before 2031) and TOL (15.6%, ~$3.3B liquidity) are insulated from debt problems in a longer slump. LEN is solvent but burning cash while paying out $1.36B and has $7.33B of land deposits at risk. OC is the most levered (2.4x net debt/EBITDA, ~$1.0B due within 12 months against $271M cash).
* **ETFs (§7):** ITB is the concentrated bet (~70% new-home construction, half in five builders, D.R. Horton 18%) and the right vehicle for the AI-capex hedge. XHB is only ~37% builders, with ~23% in commercial and data-center building systems and ~14% in home-goods retail, so it hedges less and adds AI exposure.
* **OC is the cleanest risk/reward in the set:** earnings are depressed by weather, not the housing cycle. Evercore's $185 target is ~62% above ~$114 (this page).

## 1. The industry backdrop (October 2026)

* **Builder sentiment at a 14-year low.** NAHB/Wells Fargo HMI fell 3 points to **29** in October, below April 2020 (30) and the 32 consensus; lowest since June 2012. Current sales 33 (-2), six-month expectations 32 (-5), buyer traffic **18** (-5).
* **Price cuts and incentives.** **43%** of builders cut prices in October, the highest share since NAHB began regular tracking in 2020; average cut **6%** for a seventh straight month. **65%** used incentives, the 19th straight month above 60%. More than a third reported immigration-enforcement activity causing construction delays.
* **Mortgage rates.** Freddie Mac 30-year **7.40%** in the latest week, from **6.66%** at the end of August; Pantheon puts the typical rate near **7.5%**, from ~6.6% in early July. Kim quoted **7.28%**, from **5.98%** at the start of the year (an earlier reading). The 10-year is near 5.2% and the market prices ~70% odds of an October 27-28 Fed hike ([[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-26]]).
* **Affordability.** Oxford Economics: a median-income household earns less than three-quarters of what it needs to afford a median-priced home, and this year's rate rise priced out another **5 million** households. No meaningful improvement expected until around end-2027.
* **Margin expectations.** Evercore's Q3 investor survey (October 14): operating margins expected **112bp** lower in a year, gross margins **74bp** lower. The fear is impairments. Kim: "We still think the builders can avoid hefty write-downs, but the way things are going, it just might be by the slimmest of margins."
* **Stocks.** XHB/SPY closed at **0.1206** on October 14, lowest since April 2020, vs. ~0.218 at the late-September 2024 peak: builders down ~43% relative to the S&P 500 in two years (XHB -24% vs. SPY +34%). Year to date: XHB down almost 9%, PHM/TOL/DHI down 2-5%, LEN down about 26%. ITB set a 52-week low on October 14.
* **Long-run demand.** Kim: household formation falls from about **1.25M** a year (plus ~250K demolitions, so ~1.5M starts needed) to **755K** in the 2030s and **420K** in the 2040s. Evercore forecasts the FHFA house price index falling **1-2% a year** for the next few years. Implication: returns come from capital allocation (buybacks), not volume. Toll has bought back about **50%** of its shares since 2015.

### PulteGroup: a re-rating bet on lower earnings

* **Q2 2026:** revenue $3.98B (vs. $4.40B), EPS $2.48 (vs. $3.03), closings -8%, ASP -3% to $544K, SG&A 10.1% of home sales (vs. 9.1%), backlog value -1% to $6.80B. Bought back 5.5M shares (~3%) for $681M in H1. Management: "market conditions remain highly competitive," with "early signs that conditions may be stabilizing in select geographies."
* **What Evercore's target implies (this page):** $112 ÷ 9.75 ≈ **$11.50** of forward EPS; $154 ÷ 14.5 ≈ **$10.60** of next year's EPS. If "forward" is the next twelve months and "next year" is 2027 (the article does not define them), the target assumes EPS *falls* and all of the upside comes from the multiple. That is consistent with Kim's view that margins bottom in 2027 at the earliest.
* **The real question is the valuation method.** Kim: the large builders "should be trading on a price/earnings basis" because they are land-light, less levered and higher-margin than 35 years ago; the industry has long traded at book ("liquidation valuations") because returns don't beat the cost of capital predictably. On book value PHM at ~1.6x has no margin of safety (ROE ~14% on Q2 annualized vs. a ~9% cost of equity, this page). On earnings, 9.75x is cheap.
* **Why Kim prefers it:** move-up and baby-boomer buyers have less rate sensitivity; margins high enough that cash flow stays strong; low leverage; cash goes to buybacks, not growth.
* **Sensitivity (this page):** each 1 point of gross margin is worth about $0.60 of annual EPS ($3.81B of quarterly home sale revenue, 24% tax, ~188M shares).

### Toll Brothers

* Same thesis as PHM at the high end: margin 23.9% (from 25.6%), Outperform, $184 target at 13x next year's EPS vs. 10.9x forward. Here the implied EPS rises ($134 ÷ 10.9 ≈ $12.30 forward, $184 ÷ 13 ≈ $14.15 next year, this page), so the TOL target relies less on the multiple alone than PHM's.

### Lennar: floor without a catalyst

* **FQ3 2026:** revenue $8.0B, EPS $1.19 ($1.23 adjusted, vs. $2.29), deliveries 20,840 (-3%), orders 20,879 (-9%), ASP $372K, incentives ~12%, net margin on home sales 6.6%. FY deliveries cut to 80,000-81,000 from 82,000-83,000. Q4 guide: gross margin 15.5-16.0%, deliveries 22,000-23,000. Land: ~488,000 homesites controlled, under 2.5% owned. Management: rates "approximately 6.8% at quarter end and even higher since"; consumers "slow their purchase decision."
* **Kim's case against:** Lennar held volume while demand weakened, at "a steeper margin price than we have seen historically." Its land is optioned largely through land bankers such as Millrose Properties (a Lennar spinoff), whose options "carry more restrictive terms and are more costly." He estimates financing cost alone adds **more than 100bp** of margin pressure.
* **This page's margin math:** each point of gross margin is worth about $1.00 of annual EPS (~$7.75B quarterly home sale revenue, 24% tax, ~238M shares) on an annualized base near $5. So the 100bp option-cost headwind roughly cancels one year of a normal margin recovery.
* **The bull counterpoint:** Berkshire Hathaway's ~$2.2B stake (above 11%). Kim reads it as a long-horizon bet on Stuart Miller's four decades of navigating cycles, which doesn't conflict with a one-year Underperform.
* Annualized ROE ~5% (EPS ~$4.8 on ~$91 book, this page): on current earnings Lennar would be worth ~0.5x reported book at a 9-10% cost of equity, so 0.85x already prices part of a recovery.

## 3. Owens Corning: the weather trade

* Evercore's top pick: target **$185** vs. ~$114. Sum of parts on EBITDA: **11x** roofing, **8x** insulation, **6x** doors. Two-thirds to three-quarters of profit is roofing.
* Thesis: roofing demand is driven by storms, not construction. Last year no named hurricane made landfall for the first time in many years, and this is expected to be a weak storm year. Kim: "put a higher multiple on depressed earnings, not a lower multiple on lower numbers." Buying window "in the next three to six months."
* Risks: weather timing is unknowable; insulation is tied to new construction, which is weakening.

## 4. Catalysts and what would make the group investable

Kim's sequence: builders stay "reserved, quiet, and unhappy" and cut production through winter, enter spring 2027 with lean inventory, then mortgage rates ease. He needs rates **below about 6.5%** for demand to improve materially, which "seems far away." He does *not* want demand to pick up early and get builders "overexcited into the late fall," repeating the overproduction of the past two winters.

**Watch list:**
* NAHB price-cut share falling back from 43%, and incentive use below 60%.
* Starts and spec inventory staying low through winter.
* Freddie Mac 30-year moving toward 6.5%.
* PHM Q3 (late October) and LEN FQ4 (December): margins stabilizing, no large impairments.
* An AI-capex scare that pulls the 10-year sharply lower (see §5).

## 5. Homebuilders as an AI-capex hedge

22V Research's Dennis DeBusschere: "Real Private Residential Fixed investment has not contributed to GDP in 4 years as private fixed investment (AI capex) has accelerated." AI capex and consumer spending have kept growth above trend and the 10-year high. If AI capex fell, the Fed would cut aggressively and yields would fall, so "Long Homebuilders is an interesting AI capex decline hedge."

* This is the crowding-out argument in [[ax-brij-khurana-wellington-ai-capex-crowding-out-bonds-20261001]] and Goldman's "Competition for Capital" ([[market-newsletter-digest-2026-09-27]]). The closest precedent is 2000-2002, when builders rallied as the Fed cut and tech fell.
* **Where the hedge breaks:** Kim says an "unprecedented number" of top-third earners fear losing their jobs within five years. If an AI-capex decline also hits white-collar employment, rates fall but buyers still stay away. The hedge works through the rates channel and fails through the jobs channel.

* **PulteGroup: most insulated.** Net debt is about 3% of capital. Notes total $1.8B in five issues maturing 2031-2036 (4.250% 2031, 7.875% 2032, 6.375% 2033, 6.000% 2035, 4.900% 2036); nothing is due within 12 months, and the revolver is undrawn. Capitalized interest was $28.5M in Q2, so roughly $115M a year against well over $2B of annual pre-tax profit. H1 land charges were only $27.6M ($16.1M impairments, $11.5M deposit write-offs). Exposure: $1.29B of option deposits against $9.69B of remaining purchase price (walk-away options, not debt); $3.1B of surety bonds and $343M of letters of credit; Pulte Mortgage's $625M repurchase facility (due August 12, 2026, $478M drawn at June 30).
* **Toll Brothers: insulated, with a land caveat.** Net debt-to-capital 15.6%, ~$3.3B of liquidity, ~13x coverage ($30.3M of interest incurred in Q3) and the least rate-sensitive buyer. The weak point is assets: Toll owns 31,817 of its 75,486 lots (42%), versus under 2.5% at Lennar, and booked $61.8M of inventory impairments and write-offs in nine months plus $39.6M of joint-venture impairments in Q3. Falling prices would hit book value before debt service. The maturity schedule for $894M of loans payable was not in the release.
* **Lennar: solvent, but the cushion is eroding.** The ratios are sound. The trend is not: operating cash flow of -$822M over nine months against $1.36B of buybacks and dividends; homebuilding cash down from $3.44B (November 30, 2025) to $1.15B, half of it escrow; $650M drawn on the revolver. Lennar filed new senior-note offerings (424B2/424B5) in October, likely terming out the revolver at today's higher rates (use of proceeds not confirmed). The larger exposure is off the debt line: $7.33B of land deposits and pre-acquisition costs, about a third of equity, with $224M already written off in nine months. Creditors are safe; the pressure falls on buybacks, the dividend and tangible book (~$77 a share, this page, matching Evercore's $78).
* **Owens Corning: the least insulated balance sheet.** Net debt/adj. EBITDA 2.4x (inside its target range). About $1.0B is due within 12 months against $271M of cash, so it must refinance or roll commercial paper. H1 payouts were supported by $370M of glass-reinforcements sale proceeds. Coverage of ~8x on EBITDA (2026 interest guided at $255-265M; trailing adj. EBITDA ~$2.05B implied by the 2.4x ratio, this page) is comfortable, and roofing (storms, replacement) makes it less exposed to new construction than the builders. The risk is refinancing in a 5%+ 10-year market. Credit rating and revolver size not verified.

**If housing keeps underperforming** (margins down another 300-500bp, volumes down 10-15%, prices falling 1-2% a year):

| | Debt risk | What gives first |
|---|---|---|
| PulteGroup | Very low | Buyback pace; small write-downs |
| Toll Brothers | Low | Write-downs on owned land and JVs |
| Lennar | Low for creditors, moderate for shareholders | Option-deposit write-offs, buybacks and dividend, tangible book |
| Owens Corning | Moderate (refinancing timing) | Buybacks; leverage rises if roofing stays weak |

Builders usually generate cash in a downturn because land spending stops and inventory is sold down. PHM and TOL are set up for that. Lennar's land-light model leaves less inventory to liquidate and more deposits at risk. Owens Corning has no such buffer, but its demand is less cyclical. Not covered: the small-cap builders trading more than 20% below tangible book, where debt risk usually concentrates in a long slump.

* **ITB is the concentrated bet.** About 70% moves with new-home construction, half the fund sits in five large builders and D.R. Horton alone is 18%. It is a call on mortgage rates and builder margins, close to a basket of the PHM/LEN/TOL names above. It tilts to the strongest balance sheets (§6) and carries little small-cap credit risk.
* **XHB is a buildings-and-home-spending basket.** Builders are about 37% of the fund (large, mid/small and manufactured). About 23% is commercial and data-center building systems (TT, JCI, CARR and MOD sell cooling and building systems into data centers; ALLE, CSL and WMS are commercial and infrastructure), and about 14% is home-goods retail. Equal weighting raises its share of small builders and small caps, where debt risk concentrates in a long slump. Wayfair, SharkNinja and Williams-Sonoma lean on Asian sourcing, so tariffs matter (import shares not verified).
* **Factor exposures:**
  * Mortgage rates and new-home demand: ITB very high (~70%); XHB moderate (~46%).
  * Existing-home turnover and remodeling: ITB ~26% (HD, LOW, SHW depend on home sales and home-equity spending); XHB ~13%.
  * AI capex, data centers and commercial construction: ITB none; XHB ~23%.
  * Consumer goods spending and tariffs: ITB negligible; XHB ~14%.
  * Concentration: ITB high (one stock 18%); XHB low (no position above ~4%).
* **The AI-capex hedge (§5) only works through ITB.** In an AI-capex decline with Fed cuts, ITB's builders benefit and it has no AI exposure. XHB is mixed: its builders rise while its ~23% in data-center and commercial systems falls. For a portfolio already heavy in AI, XHB adds AI exposure rather than hedging it.

| Scenario | ITB | XHB |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage rates fall below ~6.5%, housing recovers | Bigger winner | Gains, diluted |
| Higher for longer, AI capex keeps booming | Weak | Holds up better |
| AI capex falls, Fed cuts, yields drop | Works as a hedge | Mixed |
| Consumer recession and job losses | Builders hit hard; remodeling holds relatively better | Furnishings retail and small builders hit; commercial systems partly offset |
| Credit stress in small caps | Less exposed | More exposed |

* **Which to use:** ITB for a direct call on housing turning or as the AI-capex hedge; XHB for housing exposure without a strong rate call, accepting more small-cap credit and tariff exposure.

## 8. Risks

* Rates rising further (Fed hike, 10-year near 5.2%, bond-supply pressure in [[bond-supply-tsunami-2026]]).
* Impairments turning book value from a floor into a moving target, most for land-heavy small caps, least for land-light PHM and LEN.
* No visible margin bottom; investors expect gross margins 74bp lower in a year.
* Labor delays from immigration enforcement and higher gas prices hitting traffic.
* Shrinking household formation capping multiples after any recovery.
* Rental and multifamily stress competing for the marginal buyer ([[multifamily-credit-stress-and-rem-2026-09]]).
