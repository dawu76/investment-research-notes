## 1. Portfolio construction: signal-weighted risk parity vs. Sharpe-weighting

deal_me_in opened the month asking the group to name/critique his approach — "Discretionary Substrategy Weighting with vol targeting" — where signals are equally weighted but assets get bigger or smaller allocations depending on how many signals fire for them. -k gave the most technical read of the thread, working through it in stages:

- It's really **active-weighted risk parity**: you're implicitly using your signal vector to express views on expected returns, even if you don't think of it that way.
- Equal-weighting signals still means equal-weighting each substrategy — just at the signal-vector level instead of the final-allocation level.
- You can formally convert a binary/tri-state signal into an asset-return prediction via `r_hat = signal_ic * asset_volatility * signal`, or by regressing asset returns on the signal — the two are roughly equivalent if the signal is standardized (derivable from `beta = corr_x_y * stdev_y / stdev_x`).

ASlan then described an alternative he's actively building (not yet fully deployed): weight each substrategy by its Sharpe ratio, then use trailing vol + the correlation matrix to compute a leverage ratio applied across all signals. He was upfront about the weak point — "the sharp assumption in isolation is provably a bit rubbish," but argued the *relative* differences between strategy Sharpes are what actually carries the signal, and in practice it manages a portfolio of ~6 strategies reasonably well (e.g., SPY/TLT allocation collapses appropriately when correlation spikes, as in 2022). -k pointed out this Sharpe/vol approach is mathematically a mean-variance/max-Sharpe optimization with extra steps — the same answer is obtainable in closed form via `np.linalg.inv(np.cov(returns)) @ returns.mean(0)` if unconstrained long-short. Matt G closed the thread by connecting it to the Kelly criterion, noting he's tried an expected-Sharpe-based version himself but found allocations "still lead to very unstable strategy allocations" — fun in theory, not practical.

**Sentiment:** Genuinely technical, collaborative — several members converged on the insight that most of these approaches are variations on mean-variance optimization, with instability as the shared practical pain point.

**Pull quotes:**
- -k: *"i think this amounts to active-weighted risk parity, where you are implicitly using your signal vector to express your views on expected returns."*
- -k: *"this is essentially a mean variance/max sharpe optimization with extra steps... it'll get you the exact same answer."*
- ASlan: *"the more important element is the relative differences between sharps for strategies, which I think is vaguely correct so it works out."*
- Matt G: *"[an expected-Sharpe approach is] fun theoretically but I don't use it practically because of the instability."*

## 3. Intra-month / end-of-month momentum: replication and skepticism

halves kicked this off sharing a paper (via a Dan Nathan tweet) on intra-month momentum concentrated in a specific pre-end-of-month window. **robotkris** committed to incorporating it into that week's webinar, then delivered a thorough two-part follow-up:

- **-k's initial replication** (same day, several messages): a tradeable T-9-to-T-4 implementation neutralized against SPY is "okay... about on par adjusted for time in position"; his own 2-12 momentum factor IC/backtest work broadly lines up with the paper's claims but concluded it's "a nice observation but not really worth trading" outright, since restricting to only T-9–T-4 halves the number of firing days and roughly halves expected Sharpe.
- **robotkris's follow-up** (April 17, after a group webinar session): the group could broadly replicate the paper's results but found the mechanism suspect. Their own T-3/T-2 came out negative while T-1/T were positive — the *opposite* pattern from the original authors — which robotkris called out directly: "it's hard to make a case that those last four days are genuine." His more robust reframing: **the second half of the month beats the first half in 8 of 11 years** in their sample — a noisier but more honest signal than surgically trading a specific day-window, which "has the whiff of cherry picking a block of days." He shared frictionless equity curves comparing always-on, paper-window, second-half, and first-half-of-month variants to make the case visually.

mm and Alex separately flagged the same cherry-picking concern independently before robotkris's post — mm noting the result was "very sensitive to shifting that window forward/back by even a single day." -k suggested robotkris also look at 7-12 momentum (`mom_252_126`, aka "intmom" in the open asset pricing factor zoo) as a related factor worth checking.

**Sentiment:** Rigorous, self-correcting research culture — the group's default posture toward a promising-looking paper was to try to break it before trusting it, and robotkris's own replication disagreeing with the source paper's exact window was treated as the more important finding, not the headline result.
