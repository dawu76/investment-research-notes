⭐ **therobotjames** (2023-10-29): module 1 recap

- the main point of module 1 is that financial markets are extremely competitive. there's massive potential upside in being able to trade financial instruments at scale at consistently attractive prices. this means that there is huge competition for good prices.

- if something is obviously mispriced, it won't stay that way under normal market conditions, becuase it's very profitable to exploit that mispricing. if something that should be trading at $100 is trading at $90 - smart traders will rush to buy it and the excess demand from those traders will act to close that mispricing pushing the price up towards $100. those that got in early might expect a $10 expected return. those that got in later, less. so "trading at good prices" is a highly competitive business. and, unless you have a very good reason why you are trading at good prices (and the other side of the trade is prepared to trade at bad prices)  then you probably aren't trading at good prices.

- and if you aren't trading at good prices you're going to experience either: a slow painful noisy grind-down death from trading fees, a quick noisy death from oversizing or other acts of ill-discipline, so your first mission is to not do dumb stuff that exacerbates these problems

- if you are trading all the time, slow down. you need a large edge to get away with that. if you are sizing too big, turn it down. it's only a matter of time, if you are trading random stuff you hope will make money (rather than understanding a robust causal mechanism why it makes money) it's extremely likely you are noise trading at (slightly) bad prices. you want to get more curious about the market and whether an inefficiency actually exists or you're just engaging in wishful thinking.,

- so our plan is to look to do "useful things that suck" essentially... when can we step in and take advantage of obvious mispricings? it requires a combination of the two things i talked about: large, price-insensitive, or "forced" trading,a lack of willingness for other traders to get on the other side of it - either because they can't, or because it's unattractive for them to do so.

  Trying not to over-complicate the reasoning behind it: this is behavioural. Imagine a panic starting: small group panic and rush to one exit, now everyone is rushing there, damaging each other. This is catastrophic selling. I would think 70% of sell-offs are to such extend of "catastrophic" nature.
  Kahneman/Tversky example also:
  Formulation 1: Gain Frame
  Option A: Receive $50 for sure.
  Option B: A 50% chance to win $100 and a 50% chance to win nothing.
  Formulation 2: Loss Frame
  Option C: Lose $50 for sure.
  Option D: A 50% chance to lose $100 and a 50% chance to lose nothing.
  In both formulations, the expected value of Options B and D is the same: $50 (0.5 * $100 + 0.5 * $0). However, people often choose differently in these two scenarios due to risk aversion and the framing effect:
  In the Gain Frame (Formulation 1), people are more likely to choose the sure gain of $50 (Option A), showing risk aversion.
  In the Loss Frame (Formulation 2), people are more likely to choose the gamble (Option D), preferring to take a risk to avoid a certain loss.
  This phenomenon is a demonstration of what's known as "prospect theory," which posits that people value gains and losses differently: losses typically have a more significant emotional impact than an equivalent amount of gains (loss aversion). Moreover, how a choice is framed (as a gain or a loss) can significantly influence decision-making.

- ⭐ **robotkris.** (2024-08-31): Regarding the second bit, yes that's all correct in that taking bigger swings increases the probability that you'll hit zero.
  But there's another effect as well - and it's probably more damaging (unless you're taking absolutely huge lottery style bets).
  That effect is the drag that volatility has on your ability to compound your returns.
  The key point is that while volatility increases linearly with size, total returns do not. And risk adjusted returns will decrease with size.

- .stooart (2024-10-24): Mortal Sins of Trading: Notes
  ## Mortal Sin 1: Death by a thousand paper cuts (trading too often)
  - Instant opening/closing positions is reliable way to lose money trading.
  - Each trade incurs commissions, fees, spreads, and other costs, making buying more expensive than selling.
  - Large positions can push prices against you, increasing your overall costs.
  - Trading frequently without strong justification can significantly reduce your account balance.
  - Only execute trades when it's truly worthwhile, as market makers charge for providing instant prices.
  - You incur a loss of fees and bid/ask spread with each trade.
  - Example, if the total fees are 5 basis points and the bid/ask spread is 10 basis points, a trader could expect to lose 20 basis points on a round trip (buying and then selling) because they buy at a higher price and sell at a lower price, plus transaction fees. To break even, the asset would need to be mispriced by at least 20 basis points.
  - "Do a bare minimum amount of trading to harness the edge."
  - Restrict trading to times when you are confident there is heavy mispricing, or when you need to reduce risk.
  - Don’t be a hero. Minimizing your costs, through trading less often and avoiding marginal bets, is nearly always the correct move for the individual trader.
  - As a part-timer, it would be quite hard to profitably trade something that turns over more than once a day.
  - Concentrate on certain cost over uncertain benefit.

⭐ **therobotjames** (2023-10-29): [From Module 1 recap] second, you want to understand that most of what happens in markets is unknowable. markets are not a thing you will ever "figure out", however hard you try.

- the massive majority of price changes you observe are unpredictable before the event. after the event, they can sometimes be understood as a reaction to some news, or a reaction to moves elsewhere in the market. but often they can't. markets are just people buying and selling. sometimes people just do that in size for whatever reason and price moves and nobody really knows why.

- lots of this seeming randomness is a consequence of markets being massively competitive. if somebody knows something about the future, they'll trade on it, and that will quickly get incorporated in the price today. markets are forward looking - people are betting all the time on what they think will happen. now, this sounds like pretty bad news - but one (kinda positive) impact of this is that it's actually pretty hard to trade at bad prices even if you're bad at trading.

- the best traders in the market are basically setting the prices, which means that it's very likely, if the market is in a normal state, that liquid assets will be trading near "where they should be". the result of this is it's actually hard to find "negative alpha". it's hard to intentionally lose money trading beyond the time-tested strategies of making too many trades or trading too big.

- some other good news results from this... it also means that we don't have to do all the work to work out roughly where the equilibrium "fair value" of assets should be. the aggregate market kinda does that for us. most of the time, it's going to be roughly the current price.

- so, just like a sports bettor can often take the probability implied by market odds as a reasonable baseline forecast of match probabilites, so we can do that with the current market price. that then frees us up to look for angles - occasions / edge cases when things tend to be priced wrong.

⭐ **therobotjames** (2023-10-31): as an approach, i think that's ok for high turnover HFT-style trading styles. to have some success with this kind of thing you really need some good organizing ideas about when/why things would be mispriced.

- so your friend has given you a pretty good structure for playing competitive trading games. but you probably don't really need to play competitive trading games. you can probably pick your spots a bit more and _only look to get involved when more extreme things happen._ those tend to be more forgiving than trying to get things "right enough" all the time that you don't die adverse selection death. because you can afford to only optimize to one thing - rather than everything that can possibly happen.
