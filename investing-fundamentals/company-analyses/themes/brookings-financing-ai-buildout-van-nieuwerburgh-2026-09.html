Brookings: Financing the AI Buildout

An economist works out how big the AI buildout is, how it's being paid for, and how much revenue it must earn to justify itself. The paper doesn't predict a bust. It gives you a bar to measure against.

Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh, Columbia Business School. BPEA conference draft (title page dated Sept 4, presented Sept 24-25, 2026), 28 pages, not yet peer reviewed. No rating, no trade recommendation, no disclosed conflicts. Full PDF read directly. Wiki marks checks and estimates made in these notes, not claims the paper makes. Notes confidence: high.

The argument in five lines

01 · PHYSICAL CAPITAL
3.63% of GDP
A 1GW AI campus costs about $41B. A 183GW US buildout by 2032 needs investment averaging 3.63% of GDP a year over 2025-32, bigger relative to the economy than canals, railroads, electrification, highways or telecom.
02 · OUTSIDE MONEY
$800.5B > $707.1B
2026 is the first year the five hyperscalers' capex exceeds their operating cash flow, pushing funding into leases, joint ventures, project debt, private credit, securitization and SPVs.
03 · HIDDEN LEVERAGE
~90% debt
That expands funding capacity but raises asset-level leverage, obscures contingent obligations, and concentrates tenant, obsolescence and execution risk, as Meta's Hyperion/Beignet deal shows.
04 · THE BAR
$3.725T
At a 10% return and 50% margin, the buildout needs $3.725T of annual revenue by 2032: roughly 80% a year growth for OpenAI and Anthropic combined from ~$100B today.
05 · NO BUST CALL
"Premature"
"It would be premature to conclude that AI infrastructure already poses systemic risk." The main ask is better measurement and disclosure while the capital structure is still forming.

How it's being paid for

The template deal: Meta's Hyperion / Beignet

How a hyperscaler builds a $30B campus without putting the debt on its own books, and what that costs.

  • 2.0GW campus, ~$30B total investment, joint venture with Blue Owl
  • Meta sold an 80% equity stake to Blue Owl for ~$2.5B
  • The JV (Beignet) raised $27B of debt in Oct 2025, the largest single IG corporate bond deal in US history
  • Rated A+, one notch below Meta itself
  • Debt-to-assets ~90%; debt-service coverage only 1.12x
  • Priced at 6.58%
100bp+ · $5B+

Beignet pays over 100bp more than Meta's own unsecured debt likely would, which adds more than $5B of interest over the life of the deal.

In plain terms: that premium is the market price of keeping the asset off Meta's balance sheet. A 1.12x coverage ratio leaves only a 12% cushion between the cash coming in and the debt payments due.

Already copied: Sopaipilla (Meta/BlackRock, El Paso, 960MW) issued $12.3B of A+ bonds in July 2026 on a nearly identical structure.

Does it pencil out?

What it means: one falsifiable bar you can track every quarter. If OpenAI and Anthropic's combined revenue isn't compounding near 80% a year, the buildout as a whole is earning less than a normal return.

Compared with past booms

The paper's own caveat matters as much as the numbers. Canals, railroads and fiber served for decades. GPUs may be obsolete in three to six years. So AI creates less lasting capital per dollar than most of these booms, even though it is the largest by GDP share. Newsletters that compare it straight to the railroad or telecom booms skip this.

Takeaways for investors

1. One falsifiable bar. OpenAI + Anthropic revenue must compound ~80% a year to 2032 from ~$100B. Track it every quarter against reported ARR. Sharper than "is AI demand real."
2. Beignet is a template with a visible price. Sopaipilla already copies it. Watch whether the 100bp+ premium over the parent's own debt widens or narrows as more of these deals price.
3. Reported leverage is the wrong measure. Off-balance-sheet obligations run 3-4x on-balance-sheet debt (UBP independently gets 3.3x). Track the ratio quarter over quarter as a gauge of how fast risk is leaving GAAP balance sheets.
4. GPU life drives much of the answer. 6 years to 3 years takes required revenue from 9.2% to 14.8% of GDP. Changes in depreciation schedules, useful-life assumptions or GPU resale values tell you more than headline capex growth.
5. Data center REITs no longer diversify AI exposure. Beta ~1 and comparable AI and non-AI exposure. The "lower-correlation picks and shovels" case isn't supported by the paper's data.
6. Watch NVIDIA's backstop like 2000-01 vendor financing. A supplier guaranteeing 25% of the residual value of its own product, up to $125B, shows how much balance-sheet support it thinks customers need. Compare Cisco, Lucent and Nortel.
7. The restraint is itself information. The most rigorous model-based treatment available, from a source with no commercial stake, declines to call systemic risk. Weigh that against louder bearish newsletter calls without comparable numbers.

Checking the claims

Every reported total was recomputed and reconciles. That's a contrast with the UBP note, which had a cumulative-capex error and an overstated Oracle buffer.

Internal arithmetic

  • ChecksTable 5. 1,153 + 1,432 + 1,399 + 1,098 + 791 + 940 + 1,408 + 2,058 = $10,279B. GDP sums to $283,448B. 10,279 ÷ 283,448 = 3.627%. The simple average of the eight yearly ratios also gives 3.63%, so both methods agree.
  • ChecksTable 6. 182.6GW (stated 182.7), $8,537.9B cost ($8,538.0B), $9,613.7B capital ($9,613.8B), $1,862.4B cash flow (exact), $3,724.7B revenue ($3,724.8B). All within rounding.
  • ChecksConstruction multiplier. 0.10(1.1)³ + 0.25(1.1)² + 0.40(1.1) + 0.25 = 0.1331 + 0.3025 + 0.44 + 0.25 = 1.1256, matching 1.126x ($8.54T → $9.61T).
  • ChecksCapital recovery. At 10%: 6-year factor 0.1772 ÷ 0.7716 = 22.96%; 20-year factor 0.6728 ÷ 5.7275 = 11.75%. Weighted 0.68 × 22.96% + 0.32 × 11.75% = 19.37%.

Against other data in the wiki

  • AgreesSecondhand citation is accurate. Michael Parekh's "$10.3 trillion… about 3.6% of US GDP per year for eight years" (Sept 24 digest) matches the paper exactly.
  • DefinitionsMoody's vs UBP lease figures. Moody's ~$970B (~$660B off balance sheet) vs UBP's $1.16T of "leases signed not commenced." Not a contradiction: there's no settled definition yet, which is the paper's measurement-gap point.
  • RisingMcKinsey overtaken. April 2025's "$7 trillion race" is ~30% below the $10.3T derived ~18 months later. Outside estimates keep moving up.
  • Borne outOracle's Jupiter force majeure postdates the paper and isn't in it, but it's a live case of the execution risk (interconnection, permitting, hardware delays) the paper flags.

Methodological points

Return and margin are held constant to 2032. Falling per-unit GPU prices with a volume response, or margin squeeze from competition, could move the $5.5-7.9/GPU-hour range, and the framework can't say which way.
$10.3T and $3.7T are the central case, not a stress case. How much of the 509GW pipeline gets built is a judgment call built into the scenario, not a conservative floor.
The model is unlevered. Real projects run ~90% debt (Beignet). The paper doesn't translate its hurdle into equity returns under realistic leverage, where revenue shortfalls hit much harder.
Hardware refresh is excluded. 2025-26 GPUs likely need replacing around 2030, so lifetime gross investment exceeds $10.3T.

Verdict

The most rigorous model-based treatment of AI-buildout financing in the wiki. Every arithmetic result reconciles on recomputation. The capacity and investment estimates come from a disclosed project-level database with a documented method, not a top-down assumption. And it answers a question no other source in the wiki's AI-financing coverage (UBP, Burry, the digests) computes directly: what revenue does this need to earn a normal return?

Its conclusion is appropriately narrow. It defines the bar (~80% a year growth, ~$3.7T by 2032) without forecasting whether it will be cleared, and declines to call systemic risk.

Two caveats: it's a conference draft, not a peer-reviewed paper, and its results are sensitive to its own build-out and GPU-life assumptions, which it states openly but doesn't resolve.
