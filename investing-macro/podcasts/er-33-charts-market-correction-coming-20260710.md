# 33 Charts that Show Why a Market Correction May Be Coming | Jim Paulsen

Detailed overview and analysis of the *Excess Returns* podcast featuring **Jim Paulsen**, publisher of the *Paulsen Perspectives* Substack and former Chief Investment Strategist at Wells Capital Management, hosted by Justin Carbonneau and Jack Forehand. The episode was released on July 10, 2026.

---

### I. Executive Summary
Jim Paulsen presents a data-driven, cautious outlook for the U.S. economy and stock market, forecasting a **10% to 20% market correction** in the second half of 2026. While he believes the structural bull market remains intact until 2030, he identifies high short-term risks due to stagnant employment figures (household and payroll jobs average out to **0% growth over 12 months**), a contractionary Quasi Policy Index (rising yields, rising dollar, and falling money supply), and extreme complacency. Defensive sector weights are at their lowest levels since the 2000 dot-com peak (16% of total capitalization), indicating high volatility and zero downside protection. Paulsen notes that a quiet, historical leadership rotation is already underway: the S&P 500 "Old Era" (the 9 sectors excluding tech and communications) has outperformed "New Era" tech over a full 1-year period (as of July 7, 2026), breaking a relative downtrend that began in 2011. Finally, Paulsen outlines his **Demographic Dungeon** thesis: labor force growth has stalled at 0.5%, placing a structural cap on GDP growth (1.5%–2%) and creating long-term downward pressure on inflation and bond yields, which he expects to drop below 3%.

### III. Detailed Key Takeaways

#### 1. Zero Net Job Growth and Stagnant Income
Paulsen debuts several charts illustrating that the economic foundation is weaker than the consensus believes. When combining the Non-Farm Payroll survey (+0.3% year-over-year) and the Household survey (-0.3% year-over-year), the average net job growth over the last 12 months is **zero (0%)**. 
*   **Labor Force Contraction:** The labor force is shrinking. If the labor force had grown at its historical rate of 0.5%–1.0%, the unemployment rate would be near 5.0% rather than mid-3s.
*   **Real Personal Income:** Real disposable personal income (excluding transfer payments and government subsidies) is deeply negative.
*   **Growth Projections:** Atlanta Fed's GDPNow has fallen to **1.25%** for the quarter, and PCENow is below 2%.

> "The payroll numbers are up 3/10ths... The household numbers are down 3/10ths... So they average out at a big goose egg [0% job growth]... labor force growth... is negative over the last year... If [it] had grown its normal half to 1%, my gosh, the unemployment rate would be... close to five."
> — *Jim Paulsen*

#### 2. Quasi Policy Measure and Lagged Oil Spikes
Paulsen introduces his **Quasi Policy Measure**, an index consisting of a 50% weighting on the 10-year Treasury yield, a 30% weighting on the real U.S. Dollar Index, and a 20% weighting on WTI crude oil prices. 
*   **Contractionary Shift:** The recent rise in yields, dollar strength, and energy prices represents a severe tightening of economic conditions.
*   **Surprise Indicator:** Pushing this index forward by three months shows a tight correlation with the US Economic Surprise Index. It indicates a sharp decline in economic momentum in the third quarter of 2026.
*   **Lagged Oil Damage:** Paulsen notes that historically, stock market damage from crude oil spikes occurs with a lag *after* oil prices have peaked and began to fall, as the input costs and behavioral changes filter through the economy.

> "I took that policy variable... pushed it forward by three months and inverted it... and you can see what we're looking for in the coming 3 months... is a pretty good slowdown in economic momentum... the damage for the economy and the stock market generally doesn't show up until oil peaks."
> — *Jim Paulsen*

#### 3. Defensiveness Erased and Record Analyst Complacency
Defensiveness has completely left the stock market. The market capitalization of S&P 500 defensive sectors (utilities, staples, healthcare, REITs) has dropped to **16% of total market capitalization**, matching the lowest level in history recorded at the 2000 dot-com peak. At historical market bottoms, this defensive weighting is typically double (32%). Additionally, the ratio of estimated forward 12-month EPS to the trailing 10-year average EPS is at an all-time high, indicating extreme analyst complacency.

> "We're right back down [in defensive sector weights] to where we were at the dot-com top... 16-17%... At the bottom of the '09 market, you had a defensive waiting that was twice as great... That says... not only is there not much downside protection, but you're also involving yourself in a market... with a lot more volatility."
> — *Jim Paulsen*

#### 4. The "Old Era" vs. "New Era" Rotation
Paulsen notes that S&P 500 tech index has already experienced a 10% pullback, but the broad market has held up due to rotation. S&P 500 earnings are heavily bifurcated: technology sector earnings are surging on AI, while earnings for the other 9 sectors have been flat for three years. Real GDP shows a similar gap: tech-based New Era spending is growing at 8% while the rest of the economy is growing at 1%. 
*   **The Rotation Breakout:** Since October 2025, the nine "Old Era" sectors have outperformed the two "New Era" sectors. As of July 7, 2026, the Old Era has beaten the New Era over a full 1-year period for the first time in this bull market.
*   **Breakout Pattern:** Broad market plays have broken a relative downtrend against tech that was in place since 2011 (a 15-year relative breakout).

> "S&P tech... is already at a 10% decline... As of last night, July 7th... you can go back a full year where the old era parts of the S&P 500 have beat the new era parts... broad market plays... broke a trendline from 2011 down to today... first time in 15 years."
> — *Jim Paulsen*

#### 5. The Demographic Dungeon and Yield Forecasts
Paulsen dismisses concerns that bond yields will soar to 6% due to federal debt. He demonstrates that a simple 10-year weighted moving average of CPI inflation has tracked 10-year yields closely since 1970. In the long term, labor force growth has stalled at 0.5% (the "Demographic Dungeon"). This low growth input caps long-term GDP growth at 1.5%–2% and places structural downward pressure on inflation (0%–1%) and bond yields. He forecasts that 10-year Treasury yields will decline below **3%** over the next few years.

> "Demographics in the United States have worsened... labor force growth... is going to be down here with a half a percent... doing well to get 1.5 to 2% GDP growth... because of the depressing effect... that's suggesting an inflation rate of 0 to 1% [and] a bond yield that's under 3%."
> — *Jim Paulsen*

### IV. Portfolio Positioning & Action Items

1.  **Reduce Exposure to "New Era" Equities:** Shift technology and communication services sectors to a tactical underweight position, anticipating a 20%+ drawdown in these frothy areas.
2.  **Overweight "Old Era" Broad Marketplace Sectors:** Rotate capital into the other nine S&P 500 sectors (industrials, financials, energy, staples, etc.), which have broken a 15-year relative downtrend against tech.
3.  **Prepare for a 10%–20% Correction:** Do not panic or sell entire portfolios. Use the correction (driven by economic momentum deceleration and analyst estimate downward revisions) to add to broad market strategies.
4.  **Extend Duration in Fixed Income:** Accumulate long-term Treasuries, anticipating that the "Demographic Dungeon" (0.5% labor force growth) will pull 10-year yields below 3% over the next few years.
5.  **Lower GDP Growth Expectations in Financial Plans:** Model long-term real GDP growth at a 1.5%–2.0% stall speed rather than historical 3.0%+ averages.
