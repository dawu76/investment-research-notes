5/27/26, 8:25 PM | Euan: I have to log in to read the comments. And I have no clue what my credentials are.

But anyway, let me expand on my answer. And remember that nuance is everything.

Feedback is a real thing. And market makers have been worried about it since there have been market makers. It has always been an issue at expiration when gamma is most concentrated. These guys started front running their own hedges. For example, they knew they would have to sell if the stock dropped so they instead sold at the current level. If they went down to the short gamma they only got flat not long. This gave them a lot more flexibility about how to deal with the now tricky situation. But it also meant their bigger risk was to the upside, which is opposite to what an outside observer would expect. Another defense was that the pit would strongly encourage each other not to panic. They knew feedback from hedging was the issue. So if no one hedged they were all fine. And if you broke this accord you would find yourself not getting any good trades.

But it isn't as bad now as there are many more strikes and expirations to give possibilities to spread off the risk. But more important, is that the ecosystem is now massively different. When market makers were single traders or small groups everything was hedged at the stock level. So if you were fucked in MSFT, you were fucked in MSFT. But now the volume is dominated by 5 or so huge firms. They don't hedge at the stock level. Everything is bucketed and hedged as a portfolio. Options are spread to dispersion books. Stocks are spread to stat arb etc. The effect of feedback is spread out. No-one is more aware of the issue than market makers and they have had quants thinking about ways to mitigate the issue for decades.

But it is still there. So what is the size of the effect? Neil Pearson has done a bunch of studies on this. He used the internal data set from a market making firm. He had 100% accurate position data. And he found that in extreme cases, the daily SPX vol was distorted by a few vols from what it would be otherwise.

So let's say that is big enough for you. How do you take advantage of this? First you need to estimate the market maker positions. This isn't easy. Buys on the bid and sells on the offer don't mean much. Nor does whoever initiated the trade. This is particularly true for the SPX complex where you need to track and aggregate trades in SPX, SP500, XSP and SPY options.
