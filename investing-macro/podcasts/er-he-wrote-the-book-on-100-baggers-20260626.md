# He Wrote the Book on 100-Baggers | Chris Mayer on SpaceX, AI Reckoning, and Why Early Is Overrated

Detailed overview and analysis of the *Excess Returns* podcast featuring **Chris Mayer**, co-founder of Woodlock House Family Capital and author of *100 Baggers*, hosted by Matt Ziegler. The episode was released on June 26, 2026.

---

### I. Executive Summary
Chris Mayer discusses his upcoming book, *The Investor's Odyssey: Resisting the Sirens and Playing the Long Game*, emphasizing the importance of tuning out short-term market noise. He analyzes the recent SpaceX IPO, which reached a valuation of $2.6 trillion, trading at an unprecedented **145 times revenue** with no positive earnings. While comparing it to Google's 2004 IPO (which survived skepticism to become a massive winner), Mayer argues SpaceX's valuation and dual-class governance test limits. He advises that "early is overrated," noting that investors have ample time to buy validated technologies rather than rushing into speculative bubbles. Mayer warns of an impending AI reckoning where low-return features are rationalized, though real long-term AI value will likely accrue to ordinary businesses utilizing AI for margin expansion (e.g., Constellation Software's return-driven culture). Finally, he highlights the Worldly Partners "Generational Investing" study, showing that 82% of 100x winners had drawdowns exceeding 50%, with an average peak drawdown of 65% and a median of 8 years between all-time highs.

### III. Detailed Key Takeaways

#### 1. SpaceX Valuation and Governance Limits
Mayer expresses deep skepticism regarding the valuation of the SpaceX IPO, which hit a market capitalization of $2.6 trillion. At this valuation, the stock traded at roughly **145 times revenue** with no positive earnings. Mayer compares this to Google's 2004 IPO:
*   **Google (2004):** Valued at ~$20 billion, trading at ~80x earnings and between 5x–10x revenue. Value managers (including Mayer) mocked it as overvalued, but it proved to be a multi-bagger.
*   **SpaceX (2026):** Valued at $2.6 trillion, trading at 145x revenue. Even if Ron Baron's target of $10T–$30T is reached, the entry price carries immense risk.
Furthermore, the S1 filings showcase unprecedented corporate governance structures. The dual-class shares grant Elon Musk absolute control, prevent shareholders from suing him, and feature a Mars colony incentive clause that awards Musk a trillion-dollar bonus if a 1-million-person colony is established on Mars. Culturally, however, Musk's policy of granting stock options down to the welder level has created broad employee alignment.

> "Google was trading at... less than 10 [times revenue]... So SpaceX is trading at... 145 times revenue... SpaceX doesn't have any earnings... Knowing the way markets work, you will probably get a chance to own SpaceX at a fraction of this price... Amazon had a 90% drawdown peak to trough."
> — *Chris Mayer*

#### 2. "Early Is Overrated" & The AI Feature Shakeout
Mayer argues that investors frequently fall victim to a false sense of urgency when new technology cycles emerge. Using Alfred Korzybski's general semantics concept, he warns against letting labels like "AI" do the analytical work. Many companies are adding useless AI features (e.g., a golf app round-summarizer that detracts from the user experience) simply because they can. This speculative buildout will lead to a shakeout. Mayer notes that if a technology is truly revolutionary, investors do not need to buy in early; they have years to purchase a validated business with clear financials, even if they miss the first double in stock price.

> "If it is the real deal, you have plenty of time... There's a lot of urge with investors... they want to get in really early... and there's not necessarily any need for that... If you have to wait a few months or quarters to see if it really does have traction, it's not going to kill you."
> — *Chris Mayer*

#### 3. Where Real AI Value Will Accrue
Instead of chasing highly priced hardware and large language models, Mayer suggests looking for ordinary, "boring" companies (e.g., HVAC, storage, or distributors) that use AI internally to drive productivity and margin expansion. He contrasts market-share-chasing management teams with capital-disciplined firms like Constellation Software (`CSU`). Constellation has a deeply ingrained return-on-investment culture and will not fund speculative AI projects unless they show a clear return on capital.

> "You're going to have to expand what you think of as an AI play. Some of these big wins may not come in technology companies... It may be an HVAC company that figures out how to use AI... Constellation Software... has this ingrained culture of return on investment. They're not likely to just spend a lot of money on AI initiatives that don't pay back."
> — *Chris Mayer*

#### 4. The Statistical Reality of 100x Winners
Mayer highlights a recent paper by Worldly Partners titled *"Generational Investing: The Discipline Behind 100x Outcomes"*, which analyzed stocks that returned at least 100x since 1972:
*   **82%** of these ultimate 100x winners experienced a drawdown of **greater than 50%**.
*   The average maximum drawdown was **65%**.
*   The average return was **533x** from the starting point.
*   The median time between consecutive all-time highs was **8 years**.
This study confirms Mayer's *100 Baggers* thesis: achieving massive long-term returns requires tolerating extreme volatility and long periods of flat performance that would cause typical institutional managers to get fired.

> "Worldly Partners... generational investing the discipline behind 100x outcomes... 82% of those stocks lost more than 50% of their market value... the average drawdown was 65%... and it was eight years between highs. So it's a lot of waiting."
> — *Chris Mayer*

#### 5. Passive Flows & "Even God Would Be Fired"
Mayer addresses the lopsided structure of the 2026 market. Torsten Sløk's (Apollo) data shows that if you strip out AI and energy companies, the rest of the S&P 500 is down year-to-date. Passive index inflows and algorithmic rules drive this concentration. Discretionary human-directed trading has shrunk from 80% in 2003 to just 7% in 2026, according to CIBC notes.
Mayer references Wes Gray's classic "Even God Would Be Fired" experiment. Even a portfolio constructed with perfect foresight (buying the absolute best-performing stocks over rolling 5-year periods from 1927 to 2016) suffered drawdowns of over 35% and multi-year underperformance. In the real world, clients would fire this "God" manager before the outperformance could materialize.

> "Wes Gray... even God would be fired... he created the God portfolio... absolute best portfolio you could create... even in that case, these portfolios were suffering 35% drawdowns... so even God would be fired because investors would be like, that sucks."
> — *Chris Mayer*

### IV. Portfolio Positioning & Action Items

1.  **Avoid SpaceX Post-IPO Hype:** Maintain a disciplined stance and do not buy SpaceX at 145x revenue. Wait for a typical post-hype correction (50%+ drawdown) before evaluating entry.
2.  **Focus on Capital Allocators:** Prioritize companies with a strong return-on-capital focus (e.g., Constellation Software) over firms chasing market share or "AI land grabs" without clear ROI metrics.
3.  **Validate before Investing:** Ignore early-stage AI hype. Wait for productivity gains and margin expansion to show up in a company's actual audited financial reports before buying.
4.  **Embrace Multi-Year Volatility:** If holding potential multi-baggers, mentally prepare for 50%–65% drawdowns and up to 8-year periods of flat performance.
5.  **Look for Non-Tech AI Beneficiaries:** Search for industrial, distribution, and service companies using AI to structurally lower operating costs rather than pure-play AI software providers.
