# Japan Travel Insurance Comparison

**Research date:** 2026-05-06  
**Scenario:** 2 adults + 2 children, 2-week trip to Japan

---

## Option A: Japan-Purchased Insurance (After Landing)

The JNTO and Japan Tourism Agency's emergency guide link to one primary provider:

### Tokio Marine & Nichido — OMOTENASHI Policy

- **Purchase site:** https://japantravelinsurance.net/english.html (English)
- **When:** Only after entering Japan — cannot be purchased before departure
- **Max duration:** 31 days

| Duration | Per Person (JPY) | Per Person (~USD at ¥150/$) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 day | ¥800 | ~$5 |
| 7 days | ¥2,960 | ~$20 |
| 14 days | ¥4,870 | ~$32 |
| 21 days | ¥6,470 | ~$43 |
| 31 days | ¥9,420 | ~$63 |

**Family of 4, 14 days: ¥19,480 (~$130 USD total)**

#### Coverage
| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Medical expenses | ¥10,000,000 (~$67K USD) combined cap |
| Medical evacuation / repatriation | Included — within the ¥10M cap |
| Trip cancellation | **No** |
| Baggage / delay | **No** |
| Cashless hospital payment | **Yes** — Tokio Marine pays Japanese hospitals directly |
| English interpreter | Yes (English, Chinese, Korean) |

#### Key Risks and Exclusions
- **Shared cap:** Medical evacuation from Japan to the US costs $80,000–$200,000+. The entire ¥10M (~$67K) cap is shared between medical treatment and evacuation — these two together could easily exceed the limit in a serious emergency.
- **Pre-existing conditions are not covered.** The policy explicitly excludes illnesses, injuries, or accidents not covered under standard overseas insurance terms, which includes pre-existing conditions. Relevant for any family member with a chronic condition, ongoing medication, or recent medical history.
- **Additional covered items (non-medical):** Lost passport and credit card assistance, transportation arrangements, and travel safety information are included — minor but useful ancillary services.

#### The App (Required to Use the Policy)

Tokio Marine operates a dedicated smartphone app (iOS and Android) that is the primary interface for using this policy. Key details:

- **Login required:** The app requires an ID, password, or authentication code issued at contract signing. You must set this up before you need it.
- **App features:**
  - Contact the insurance call center (the required step before going to any hospital)
  - Telephone interpretation in English, Chinese, and Korean during hospital visits
  - Disaster and typhoon alerts for Japan
  - Free Wi-Fi connectivity while traveling
  - Emergency reporting
  - Tourist information
- **Call center hours: weekdays only, closed on weekends, national holidays, and year-end/New Year holidays.** Two contact lines are listed: phone +81 6-6446-2700 at "9:30–17:30 (closed weekends/holidays)" and email web-admin735@tmnf.jp at "9:00–17:00 Mon–Fri (except holidays)." If a child gets sick on a weekend evening, a holiday, or after hours on a weekday, the cashless pathway is unavailable. You would need to pay upfront and claim reimbursement.
- **Calls must be made from your home-country phone line** (not a Japanese SIM), per the policy terms.
- **Interpretation is available by three-way phone call or videophone** — not just audio, so a video call with an interpreter at the hospital bedside is an option.

#### Cashless Process Step by Step

1. **Open the app** and log in with your contract credentials.
2. **Call the Tokio Marine service center** (9:00–17:00 weekdays) via the app.
3. **Tell them which hospital you want to go to.** There is no pre-approved network — any hospital can be nominated.
4. **Tokio Marine calls the hospital** to ask if they will accept direct payment.
5. **If the hospital agrees:** Go to the hospital and receive treatment; Tokio Marine pays the bill directly. You pay nothing.
6. **If the hospital declines or is unreachable:** You pay upfront and submit a reimbursement claim with Japanese-language receipts and itemized statements.

The outcome of step 4 is not guaranteed. Smaller clinics, rural hospitals, and pharmacies frequently decline cashless arrangements.

## Practical Difficulties

### Option A: Tokio Marine OMOTENASHI (Japan-purchased)

#### What works well
- The application, website, and support line are **in English** — no Japanese required to purchase or activate.
- **Cashless hospital payment** is the flagship feature: Tokio Marine contacts the hospital and pays directly; you ideally never touch the bill.
- An **English interpreter is dispatched via the app/call center** while you are at the hospital.

#### What can go wrong
- **Cashless requires calling in advance.** If you go to the hospital first and call after, cashless approval is much harder or unavailable. In a family emergency, calling ahead before seeking care may not be realistic.
- **Cashless is conditional, not guaranteed.** The hospital must agree to participate. Smaller clinics, rural hospitals, and pharmacies may decline. There are no pre-designated in-network hospitals.
- **If cashless fails**, you revert to reimbursement — and must collect Japanese-language documents: 領収証 (receipt), 診療明細書 (itemized statement), prescription records. Hospital staff hand you Japanese paperwork and expect you to manage it.
- The **claim submission fallback** is paperwork-intensive and takes weeks.

---

### Option B: US-Based Insurance (Allianz, Faye, BHTP, etc.)

#### The core problem: pay-first, claim-later

Japanese hospitals **do not bill foreign insurers directly**. With rare exceptions (a handful of international clinics in Tokyo and Osaka), your US insurance card means nothing at the point of care. The standard experience:

1. Show up at the hospital — they ask for payment before discharge.
2. Pay 100% of the bill in cash or by credit card.
3. Collect every receipt and document (all in Japanese).
4. Submit a reimbursement claim to your US insurer after returning home. Expect weeks.

#### The credit card problem

- Many smaller clinics and hospitals still prefer or require **cash**. Fine for a minor illness; not fine for an emergency hospitalization.
- A multi-day hospitalization (e.g., a child with appendicitis) can run **¥500,000–¥2,000,000 ($3,300–$13,000)**. You must pay before discharge.
- For a family facing a $10,000–$30,000 bill, **credit card limits are a real practical blocker**. You can call your issuer to raise the limit temporarily, but this requires planning ahead.
- Some issuers place fraud flags or foreign transaction blocks on large hospital charges.

#### Document burden

- All itemized statements, doctor's notes, and discharge summaries will be in **Japanese**.
- Your US insurer's claim form will require detailed medical records — you must obtain those before leaving Japan.
- Outside Tokyo and Osaka, English-speaking physicians and staff are limited. Asking for the right paperwork in Japanese, without support, is genuinely hard.

#### Language barrier is worse for US insurance holders

Tokio Marine includes an interpreter as part of the service. With a US insurer, you navigate the hospital yourself, ask for Japanese documents yourself, and manage the claim yourself.

---

### The 2026 Unpaid Bills Rule (New)

Starting in FY2026, Japan is flagging unpaid medical bills **as low as ¥10,000 (~$67)** in its immigration database — down from the prior ¥200,000 threshold. An unpaid bill can result in **re-entry denial on future visits**. Do not leave Japan without settling any hospital bill, even small ones.

---

### Practical Friction Comparison

| Friction Point | Tokio Marine (Japan) | US Insurance |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital pays directly? | Yes, if cashless arranged in advance | Almost never |
| Must pay upfront? | Only if cashless fails | Yes, always |
| Large bill on credit card? | No (cashless) / Yes (if cashless fails) | Yes — could be $5K–$30K |
| English support at hospital | Yes — interpreter via app | No — on your own |
| Japanese documents to collect | Only if cashless fails | Always required |
| Reimbursement delay | Only as fallback | Standard — weeks |
| Rural/small clinic cashless | May not be available | Not applicable — always pay upfront |

**Bottom line:** Tokio Marine's cashless system, when it works, is dramatically more convenient — no upfront payment, no Japanese paperwork, no credit card exposure. The risk is that it requires proactive advance contact and is not guaranteed at every hospital. US insurance holders face the full burden of paying large bills on the spot and navigating Japanese medical bureaucracy without any in-hospital support.
