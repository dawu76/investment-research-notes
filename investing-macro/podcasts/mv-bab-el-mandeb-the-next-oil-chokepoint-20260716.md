# MacroVoices #541 Dr. Anas Alhajji: Bab el-Mandeb: The Next Oil Chokepoint Nobody's Watching

Detailed overview and analysis of the *MacroVoices* podcast (Episode 541) featuring **Dr. Anas Alhajji**, energy market expert and founder of *Energy Outlook Advisors*, hosted by Erik Townsend and Patrick Ceresna. The episode was released on July 16, 2026.

---

### I. Executive Summary
Dr. Anas Alhajji provides a comprehensive geopolitical and structural analysis of the escalating Middle East energy crisis. He argues that following the collapse of the U.S.-Iran Memorandum of Understanding (MOU)—driven by rogue Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) factions acting like a drug cartel to protect black-market revenues—the focus must shift to the Bab el-Mandeb strait. Houthi drone strikes on civilian Saudi targets threaten to prompt Western insurers to cancel war-risk marine insurance, locking up 4 million barrels/day of Saudi Red Sea exports and driving WTI far above $100/bbl. Alhajji reinforces his thesis that the U.S. initially closed the Strait of Hormuz using EU insurance solvency laws to signal dominance over China (targeting helium supplies for Asian semiconductor fabricators). He also debunks the bullish SPR refilling thesis, explaining that the physical injection limit (400,000 barrels/day) is too small to move prices, and that the SPR releases were structured as crude loans that oil companies will return only at cheap levels. In the postgame segment, Patrick Ceresna and Massil Beguenane select Cheniere Energy (LNG) with a protective put to express the energy security theme, noting S&P CTA triggers at 7,400.

### III. Detailed Key Takeaways

#### 1. Bab el-Mandeb: The Next oil Chokepoint
Dr. Alhajji warns that the market is overlooking the most critical threat to global supply.
*   **Houthi Escalation:** Rogue Houthi factions, under the influence of IRGC hardliners, violated their agreement with Saudi Arabia by launching drone/missile attacks on the civilian airport in Abha. 
*   **Insurance as a Weapon:** The Houthis do not need to physically block shipping. If they damage a few tankers, Western insurers (primarily UK-based) will immediately cancel maritime war-risk coverage.
*   **Saudi Export Lockup:** Without insurance, Saudi Arabia cannot ship the 4 million barrels/day of crude that it diverted from the Persian Gulf via its East-West pipeline to the Red Sea ports.
*   **Russian Windfall:** Putin is the ultimate winner. Russian crude shipped to India and China runs on a sanctioned shadow fleet insured domestically by non-Western entities, allowing Russian exports to continue uninterrupted while global prices spike.

> "The fear is... the insurance companies... will literally cancel the insurance... if they do that, then Saudi Arabia... cannot send its oil through the Red Sea strait... Putin will be a big winner."
> — *Dr. Anas Alhajji*

#### 2. The Thesis of Intentional U.S. Closure of Hormuz
Alhajji details how the U.S. government leveraged European insurance solvency regulations to restrict Asian manufacturing.
*   **Solvency Law Leverage:** Following a U.S. Navy strike on an Iranian vessel near Sila, the EU required insurers to expand war-risk coverage to the entire Indian Ocean. Under Solvency II regulations (which require massive cash reserves on hand to match coverage), UK insurers could not maintain capital requirements and canceled all policies on a 7-day notice, immediately halting shipping.
*   **Targeting the AI Supply Chain:** Semiconductor manufacturing requires helium. 75% of the helium supplied to Taiwan and South Korea comes from Qatar. The "accidental" destruction of Qatar's helium plant at the start of the conflict cut off Asia's helium supply, while regional energy and electricity costs surged.
*   **Capital Flight to the U.S.:** This bottleneck successfully pressured Asian chipmakers while attracting massive semiconductor investments to the U.S. (e.g., Taiwan's $150 billion investment in Arizona).

> "The first surprise was how quickly it happened... and the second surprise was how smartly they've done it... how the insurance industry was used to close the Hormuz... semiconductor chips cannot be made without helium... coming from Qatar."
> — *Dr. Anas Alhajji*

#### 3. Rogue IRGC Factions and the Open/Close Pickle
*   **Rogue Cartel Dynamics:** IRGC hardliners operate like a drug cartel. Under sanctions, they run highly lucrative black-market oil loops (exports reached their highest level since 2017 in February 2026). They sabotage peace negotiations because a return to normal trade routes transfers oil revenues back to the central government.
*   **Covert U.S.-Iran Cooperation:** The U.S. successfully closed the Strait but cannot reopen it because rogue IRGC factions are demanding permanent tolls. The U.S. is now forced to covertly cooperate with the official Iranian government to locate and militarily eliminate these rogue IRGC factions, as a total regime collapse in Iran would trigger a massive European refugee crisis.

> "The Iranian regime is not unified... hardliners of the IRGC... benefited greatly from the sanctions... they are behaving like a drug cartel... they want this to last longer... they are in for the long term."
> — *Dr. Anas Alhajji*

#### 4. The Fallacy of the SPR Refill Case
Alhajji debunks the popular thesis that the U.S. refilling the SPR will drive crude prices significantly higher.
*   **Flow Asymmetry:** The U.S. has released 99 million barrels (with 73 million remaining), peaking at 1.9 million barrels/day. However, the physical injection capacity of the salt caverns is capped at **400,000 barrels/day**. This slow rate is price-neutral.
*   **Crude Loans vs. Sales:** Most SPR releases were structured as crude loans, not sales. Oil companies (like Exxon) borrowed crude at $120/bbl and must return it with interest "in-kind" (e.g., returning 12M barrels for 10M borrowed). They will buy and return this crude only when prices are low ($60–$80/bbl), meaning refilling acts as a price floor rather than a price driver.

> "We cannot refill at the same rate that we released it... we cannot inject more than 400,000 barrels a day... [Also] those releases are loans, they are not sales... oil companies... will return it when oil is 60 or 70."
> — *Dr. Anas Alhajji*

#### 5. OPEC+ Bylaw Reforms
To survive in a post-Hormuz world where pipelines are highly vulnerable to drone attacks, Alhajji proposes three reforms for OPEC+:
1.  **Quota Exports:** Focus quotas on verifiable exports rather than production.
2.  **Monitor All Liquids:** Expand tracking to all liquids (LNG/NGLs) rather than just crude.
3.  **End-Market Storage:** Partner with major consuming nations (like India) to build massive strategic storage reserves at the end-market destinations (bypassing chokepoints).
