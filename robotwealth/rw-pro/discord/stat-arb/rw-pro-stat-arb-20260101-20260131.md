## 1. strvinmarvin's independent pairs-trading build — a full architecture writeup and overfitting debate

The month's single longest and most substantive thread: strvinmarvin, having built his own version of the course's cointegration-pairs strategy independently (using Claude Code as implementation help, not the RW API), shared his full architecture and backtest results on Jan 10, triggering a detailed design discussion.

- **Core architecture:** S&P 500 + Nasdaq universe run through a lasso regression cointegration filter (from the course's cointegration module), trained on the first year of data and never touched again. This produces ~525 "approved" pairs, traded out-of-sample for the next ~9 months. Each rebalance window ranks pairs by LSR score + Euclidean distance (also from the course methodology); 30-minute spread z-scores drive entries/exits, with a one-bar signal lag (added on `-k`'s recommendation, vs. a default of 0).
- **His departures from the course setup**, each independently motivated:
  - **Triple-lookback confluence filter** — pairs must rank in the top 50 at three different lookbacks (350, 210, 150 bars) simultaneously, filtering out pairs that only look good at one timescale.
  - **Half-life filter** — must mean-revert within ~10 days.
  - **Sector cap** — max 2 pairs per sector, to avoid concentration.
  - **Minimum rebalance threshold** — a position must change by at least 8% of account notional value to trigger a trade at all. He flagged this as helping "in a suspicious way I haven't fully validated," but the logic checked out under scrutiny (see below).
  - A **persistence parameter** (only trade a pair if it stayed in the top 50 across 2+ windows) was tried and abandoned — it cut profits too much.
- **Backtest economics:** profitable at 1bps slippage (the default), still profitable but thinner at 2bps, and roughly break-even around 3bps — "very 'fee' heavy." Profitability holds up to ~15 active pairs but roughly halves in CAGR terms beyond that. Doubling account size improves the stats mechanically, since fixed costs shrink relative to linearly-scaling returns.
- **`-k` (weighted heavily as a core technical voice this month) walked through the overfitting risk directly and endorsed the design**, breaking a somewhat dense back-and-forth into its component points:
  - Confirmed the minimum-rebalance-threshold logic makes sense: *"i would've expected fees to overtake otherwise. that would explain the difference between what you got and what i've usually seen."*
  - Flagged the real risk in strvinmarvin's earlier admission that he'd manually searched ~127,000 pair combinations with "coint" over several months without finding anything better than the base method: *"it's a huge multiple testing problem at that point... 525 pairs is a lot fewer to test than the ~250,000 you would test otherwise!"* — i.e., the lasso pre-filter itself is doing useful work by *reducing* the hypothesis space before any further search happens, which is why the base pair-selection is hard to beat by additional combinatorial search.
  - Followed up with a compact statement of the underlying stats logic: more combinations tested → higher odds of landing on spurious false positives purely by chance, which is exactly why restricting to a pre-filtered or same-industry universe helps.
- **On leverage/margin mechanics (Jan 11–12, side-thread):** strvinmarvin raised — and the group partially validated — a claim that pairs trades might only require 25% margin at some brokers rather than the naive 2x notional, via a short/long sequencing argument (short leg cash proceeds partially fund the long leg). Ben supplied the practical answer: margin requirements vary by broker and by ticker (IBKR: 25–100% depending on the stock, downloadable via `ftp2.interactivebrokers.com`); strvinmarvin later confirmed his own basket came back mostly at 30%. zbanga added a live-trading caveat from his own desk experience: GOOG/GOOGL execution needs to be reasonably fast/skilled, since borrow rates on the legs can spike unpredictably, and professional execution there means quoting both sides and hitting whichever fills first — not simple market orders.

**Sentiment:** Highly constructive, technically deep peer review — a member built something independently, opened it up for scrutiny, and got a rigorous multiple-testing sanity check back from the community's most technical members rather than either praise or dismissal.

## 2. Notebook bug: rank-linear weight mapping was missing, then found to hurt performance anyway

A tight, self-correcting technical exchange between Michael TW 88 and robotkris over Jan 12–13.

- Michael TW 88 spotted that the stat-arb notebook's documentation describes a rank-linear distribution step for converting raw factor scores into weights, but the actual code skips it — instead mapping raw factor values directly to raw weights, preserving their original magnitudes (and risking concentration in the tails). He proposed a `_rank_linear_raw()` function (shared in full in the raw transcript) to fix this, plus flagged that weight-capping needs a redistribution step to stay fully invested once weights are clipped.
- **robotkris shared his own internal R implementation** for the capping/redistribution half of this (`apply_max_weight_ls()`, iterative cap-and-regross by date/side) and confirmed the notebook needed updating, initially believing the rank-linear step was already present and just missing from the docs.
- **The twist:** a day later, Michael TW 88 retested and reported the opposite of his original assumption — mapping factor scores to rank-linear raw weights actually **decreases performance**, because it effectively delta-neutrals the portfolio. robotkris's explanation, worth surfacing in full since it's a genuinely useful piece of factor-design intuition: *"the effect is concentrated in the tails and is roughly proportional to the magnitude of the signal, so weighting in proportion to the rank discards that information to an extent."* In other words, the "bug" (using raw magnitudes instead of rank) may have been accidentally correct — extreme signals really do carry more information here, so rank-flattening them destroys signal.

**Sentiment:** A good example of the group's habit of testing its own assumptions rather than accepting a plausible-sounding fix at face value — the initially "obvious" bug fix turned out to hurt performance, and robotkris's tails-concentration explanation gives a reusable heuristic for other factor work.

**Pull quotes:**
- robotkris: *"Yeah that's right... maybe that's why I didn't include it. We saw in the research that the effect is concentrated in the tails and is roughly proportional to the magnitude of the signal, so weighting in proportion to the rank discards that information to an extent."*
- Michael TW 88: *"actually, i take that back, mapping factor scores to rank-linear raw weights effectively delta neutrals the portfolio and decreases performance."*

## 3. Lookahead bias caught in Michael TW 88's IC/tearsheet analysis

Michael TW 88 ran an alphalens tearsheet on the January-updated pairs list (spearman-rank IC analysis on the top 500 pairs → 334 unique tickers, and separately on a filtered top-100-by-ticker-score subset), and results looked strong enough that he asked the channel directly: *"does this look too good? is anyone else doing this analysis?"*

- His own working theory, offered before robotkris weighed in: the pairs-selection process by construction datamines pairs that already do well on the two selection criteria, so the strategy is "betting on it autocorrelating" — plausible, but not the actual issue.
- **robotkris identified the real problem — genuine lookahead bias**, not just favorable selection: *"You're testing the pairs over the same historical period that selected them. You would need to test it in a rolling walk-forward fashion (select pairs --> test on the next period, repeat)."* Michael TW 88 confirmed and acknowledged he'd missed it on first read. robotkris separately noted the production spreads data in the RW lab is already walk-forward adjusted, with a new universe selected monthly — i.e., this specific failure mode doesn't affect the API-backed system members are actually trading, only ad-hoc offline analysis like this one.

**Sentiment:** A useful public correction — a member's polished-looking analysis had a textbook lookahead flaw, caught quickly and explained clearly, with a reassurance that the live system architecture is not exposed to the same issue.

**Pull quotes:**
- robotkris: *"This is interesting, but be aware that there's lookahead built in here... You would need to test it in a rolling walk-forward fashion (select pairs --> test on the next period, repeat)."*
- Michael TW 88: *"well at least this confirms the datamine is doing it's job... it confirms you're not gonna pull one over us haha"*

## 4. robotkris's volume-based feature research — informed vs. uninformed flow, and mixed replication

The month's other major technical thread, running Jan 22–23, driven almost entirely by robotkris sharing new research and code, with Michael TW 88, Per, and `-k` testing and pushing back on generalizability.

- **The core idea, in robotkris's own framing:** two new features layered onto the existing spread z-score — (1) a time-series z-score of log volume, cross-sectionally re-standardized each day ("is this stock's unusual volume unusual given the volume in the market?"), and (2) an *asymmetry* feature comparing cross-sectional volume z-scores between the two legs of each pair, flattened from pair-level to ticker-level.
- **His findings, which he called "really interesting"** and shared in detail:
  - Low cross-sectional volume predicts reversion — when both stocks are quiet, divergence is more likely noise that reverts.
  - The asymmetry feature is directional in an asymmetric way: for short signals (positive z-score), high volume asymmetry predicts *continuation* — his read is this is "probably the informed trading we want to avoid." For long signals (negative z-score), high asymmetry instead predicts *reversion*, which he speculated but did not confirm might reflect forced selling or bounded selling pressure.
  - The effect survived demeaning returns, so it's not simply "stocks trend up" bleeding into the result.
  - Gently tilting portfolio weights using these signals (reducing weight ~60% on anything that looked like informed trading, nudging other positions up 10–20%) produced "quite a bump in performance," in portfolios that stayed reasonably balanced (max 6% position size, roughly market-neutral tilt).
  - Caveat volunteered upfront, not extracted under questioning: it doesn't help decay or turnover, which he flagged as needing further work, and the transformation used was intentionally simple ("getting late and I just wanted to see if it would work").
- robotkris shared the full R implementation on request (rolling 20-day log-volume mean/SD, time-series z-score, then cross-sectional re-standardization, then the pairwise asymmetry join) and walked Michael TW 88 through exactly how it plugs into the existing `pair_factor` construction, correcting one misreading of the join logic along the way.
- **Independent replication was mixed.** Per connected this to a related, previously-circulated idea — dusting off *volume autocorrelation* as a separate reversion/confirmation factor, on the theory that uninformed trading is itself a reflexive, autocorrelated mechanism that temporarily pushes price beyond where informed traders have set it — and shared a relevant external article (sysls on X). Both Per and `-k` then tried to replicate the volume-autocorrelation angle independently and **could not generalize it** beyond the specific case in the article: `-k` got results only on overall S&P 500 returns, inconsistent by industry; Per got a near-identical result for the small-bank basket used in the source article but nothing comparable for other sectors, and pushed on the mechanism itself: *"It's not intuitively clear to me how dollarvolume autocorrelation predicts directional dislocation of price. It would make more sense to use price/return space to assess whether an asset is mispriced... and the dollar volume space to qualify the displacement as a result of informed trading (stay out) or uninformed (more likely to revert, get in)."*
- andyw and Per went back and forth on interpretation — andyw's summary of the original poster's thesis (dollar volume tracks uninformed flow that overreacts and pushes prices out of whack) drew a sharp follow-up from Per (*"how do you determine direction from total volume auto corr alone?"*) and from ppaolo (*"autocorelation gives you the duration of overreaction not direction correct?"*). `-k` closed the month's discussion with a concrete next step rather than further theorizing: treat the mispricing statistic as the primary alpha signal, and use dollar volume as a conditioning variable — regress forward returns on mispricing, dollar volume, autocorr, and their interactions (plus other characteristics like industry) to see what the coefficients actually look like.

**Sentiment:** robotkris's core volume-asymmetry result (informed vs. uninformed continuation/reversion) landed well and was adopted with working code shared same-night; the adjacent, previously-circulated volume-autocorrelation idea got a much cooler reception on independent replication — genuinely useful signal on one narrow basket, but the mechanism doesn't obviously generalize, and the month closed with a concrete regression-based next step from `-k` rather than resolution.

This is probably the informed trading we want to avoid. For long signals (negative zscore), high asymmetry predicts reversion (is this forced selling?? maybe it's a function of selling pressure being bounded?? Don't know yet)."*
