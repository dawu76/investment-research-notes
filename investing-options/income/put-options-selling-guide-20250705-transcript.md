## What Are Options? [02:30 - 04:46]

### Basics of Put Options

There are two main types of options: puts and calls. We're focusing on puts today.

For every buyer of an option, there is a seller. In this case, the seller of that option will be us.

**When someone buys a put:**
- They have the right but not the obligation to sell a stock at a given stock price on or before an expiration date

**As the seller of that put option:**
- We have the obligation to buy those shares at the same strike price on or before the same expiration date
- The buyer gives us some amount of money to guarantee that we'll keep our promise to buy those stocks

### Example Trade: TSM $180 Put [04:46 - 06:05]

Here's a TSM $180 strike price put option that we sold for an expiration of 35 days from today. The seller of this put option receives **$512** from the buyer.

**Scenario 1 - Option Expires Worthless (Win):**
If TSMC ends up above $180 (for example, stays at $185), then the put option we sold expires out of the money and becomes worthless. We have no obligations to buy 100 shares of TSMC. The buyer won't want to sell it at $180 because the shares are worth more money. We pocket the $512.

The amount of credit we receive is the maximum profit we can collect on this trade.

**Scenario 2 - Assignment (Worst Case):**
If TSMC drops below $180 (let's say to $175), then we as the seller must buy 100 shares of TSMC at $180 per share. This requires approximately **$18,000** to buy 100 shares of TSM.

The seller exercises their right to sell at $180 because the stock is worth $175, making a $5 profit on the difference.

### ROI Analysis [06:05 - 07:00]

- Maximum profit: $512
- Required capital (worst case): $18,000
- **Return on invested capital: 2.8%**

This changes based on the stock price. For example, if you sold a put option at $9, the maximum collateral would only be $900. The amount of money is tied directly to the stock and the strike price you choose.

## Three Main Factors Impacting Option Price [12:45 - 21:50]

### Factor 1: Days to Expiration [13:10 - 16:20]

Here's a chart of the TSM $185 put mapped against its date to expiration (same strike price, different dates).

**Obvious observation:** The further out we sell our options, the more credit we receive.
- 91 days to expiration: $945
- 21 days to expiration: $232

**Focus on the curve:** The curve starts accelerating around the **45-day mark**, meaning option prices start losing value more quickly. This is called **theta decay**. Your option loses value each day if everything else remains the same, and that decay speeds up in the 45-50 day zone.

**Comparison Example:**
- 91 DTE: $945
- 28 DTE: $420

During a 91-day span, you could sell three 28 DTE options back-to-back-to-back:
- Total collected: **$1,260** (3 × $420)
- vs. one 91 DTE: $945

Selling further out isn't necessarily best. You're leaving money on the table.

**Recommendation:** Sell anything around **45 days and below**. If you want to make it simple, sell every 30 days (monthlies).

### Factor 2: Strike Price [16:20 - 20:30]

Here's an option chain (same format across all brokerages - Robinhood, Webull, IBKR). It maps out the option price for every strike price available to sell or buy on a specific stock.

**TSM Option Chain Example (TSM at $197.82):**
- $195 strike: $764
- $190 strike: $593
- $185 strike: $425

**Key principle:** The further away from the stock price, the less money you collect. The closer you are, the more money you collect.

For beginners, taking on more risk than necessary when selling options can make it very dangerous. We want to balance between being conservative and aggressive.

#### Probability of Profit (POP)

POP is a beginner-friendly metric. It's your brokerage telling you what they think the chances are of you making a profit on this trade.

**TSM Examples (28 days out):**
- $195 strike: 69% POP
- $190 strike: 73% POP
- $185 strike: 78% POP

Obviously, the further out of the money, the higher your chances of winning.

**Recommended POP:** **70-80%** for beginners

This recommendation is based on statistics. One standard deviation of a bell curve covers approximately 68%. Slightly more than one standard deviation (around 75%) mitigates a lot of risk.

**Example on TSM:**
- Target: 75% POP
- Strike: $185 put option
- Credit: $410
- Return: **2.2% in 28 days**
- Annualized: If you do this 12 times per year, that's approximately **24%**, much better than the 10% average from the S&P 500

### Factor 3: Which Stock to Sell On [20:30 - 23:30]

Option pricing is highly dependent on the underlying equity or stock.

**TSM Example:**
- Current price: $197.82
- $185 strike (10 points away): $410 credit
- Required decline: 12-13 points (6.6%)

**Microsoft Comparison (same 28 days):**
- Same 6.6% decline would bring it to $410
- $410 strike option: only **$240** credit (vs. TSM's $410)

To collect $410 on Microsoft, you'd need to sell the $425 strike (only a $13 decrease from current price).

**Why is Microsoft worth less than TSM?**

The main reason is **Implied Volatility (IV)**. IV measures how much the market expects the stock to move. The more volatile a stock, the more expensive the options.

**Comparing IVs:**
- TSM $185 put: 28% IV
- Microsoft $410 put: 19% IV

TSM has higher IV because it's expected to move more. Higher IV = more expensive options = more credit collected.

#### How to Choose Stocks

You want to find stocks with high IV that you're comfortable owning. Use an IV screener or check the IV percentile:
- **IV Percentile:** Compares current IV to the past year's range
- 50% IV percentile = current IV is at the median of the past year
- Higher IV percentile = better for option sellers

**My approach:** Look for IV percentile above 50%, preferably in the 60-80% range. This means the stock is more volatile than usual, which is advantageous for sellers.

## Advanced Techniques [23:30 - 35:40]

### The Wheel Strategy [23:30 - 27:15]

The wheel strategy is one of the most popular strategies for generating monthly income. It has three main steps:

**Step 1: Sell Cash-Secured Puts**
- Collect premium while waiting to potentially buy stock at a discount
- If assigned, you own 100 shares

**Step 2: If Assigned, Sell Covered Calls**
- You own the shares, now sell calls against them
- Collect premium while potentially selling shares at a profit
- If assigned, shares are sold (usually at a profit)

**Step 3: Repeat**
- Go back to Step 1 and sell puts again

**Example on PLTR (Palantir):**
- Stock at $29.50
- Sell $28 put (30 DTE, 75% POP): $150 credit

If PLTR stays above $28, keep the $150 and repeat. If it drops below $28, you buy 100 shares at $28 (effectively $26.50 after the $150 credit).

Now you own shares at an average cost of $26.50. Sell a $30 covered call for another $150. If PLTR goes above $30, your shares are sold at $30, giving you a $3.50 profit per share ($350 total) plus the $300 in premiums ($150 + $150).

**Total profit:** $650 on a $2,800 investment = **23% return**

### Managing Losing Trades [27:15 - 32:40]

#### Technique 1: Roll Down and Out

When a trade goes against you, you can "roll" to manage the position.

**Example: TSM $160 Put Going Bad**
- Opened when TSM was at $175
- TSM drops to $150 with 4 days left
- Option worth $1,018 (would lose $683 if closed)

**Rolling Strategy:**
- Close the $160 put (buy it back for $1,018)
- Simultaneously sell a new put at a lower strike and/or further date
- Example: Sell $145 put 30 days out for $800

**Result:**
- Net debit: $218 ($1,018 - $800)
- New break-even: $145
- More time for stock to recover
- Lower assignment risk

**Key insight:** You're taking a small loss now to avoid a potentially bigger loss and give yourself more time and room for recovery.

#### Technique 2: Take the Assignment

Sometimes the best move is to accept assignment and own the stock.

**When to do this:**
- You believe in the company long-term
- The stock is at a price you're comfortable owning
- You have the capital available

**After assignment:**
- You own 100 shares
- Start selling covered calls to generate income
- Potentially profit when the stock recovers

**Example continued from TSM $160:**
- Accept assignment at $160 ($16,000)
- Already collected $335 premium
- Effective cost basis: $156.65
- Start selling covered calls at $165 or $170

#### Technique 3: Close Early at 50% Profit

One of my favorite techniques is closing winning trades early.

**Why close at 50%?**
- You've captured most of the profit
- Reduce risk of the trade turning against you
- Free up capital for new trades

**Statistics show:** Most of the theta decay happens in the last 2 weeks. By closing at 50% profit (usually around day 10-14 for a 30 DTE), you:
- Capture the easy money
- Avoid the stress of holding to expiration
- Can redeploy capital into new trades

**Example:**
- Sold TSM $185 put for $410 (30 DTE)
- After 10 days, option worth $205
- Close for 50% profit ($205 profit)
- Sell a new put with fresh 30 DTE

## Common Mistakes to Avoid [40:30 - 43:50]

### Mistake 1: Selling Puts on Stocks You Don't Want to Own

Remember, selling a put means you might have to buy the stock. Only sell puts on companies you'd be happy to own at that price.

**Bad example:** Selling puts on a meme stock just for high premiums, but you don't believe in the company.

**Good example:** Selling puts on Apple because you want to own it anyway and you're getting paid to wait.

### Mistake 2: Going Too Far In the Money

Chasing high premiums by selling at-the-money or in-the-money puts is dangerous.

**Example:**
- TSM at $185
- Selling $190 put for $850 (looks attractive!)
- But 55% POP means 45% chance of assignment
- High risk for a small amount of extra premium

**Better approach:** Stick to 70-80% POP range

### Mistake 3: Not Having Enough Capital

Selling puts requires significant capital or margin. If you don't have enough:
- You might face margin calls
- Forced to close positions at the worst time
- Can't take advantage of opportunities

**Solution:** Always maintain cash buffer (at least 10-20% of portfolio)

### Mistake 4: Over-Trading

Selling too many puts at once:
- Increases risk dramatically
- Ties up all your capital
- Leaves no room for adjustments

**Better approach:**
- Start small (1-2 positions)
- Build up gradually as you gain experience
- Leave room for new opportunities

### Mistake 5: Ignoring Earnings Dates

Stocks can make huge moves around earnings announcements. Avoid having options expiring right around earnings unless you specifically want that risk.

**Check earnings calendars** before selling puts. Give yourself cushion (at least 1 week before/after earnings).

### Mistake 6: Panic Closing During Volatility

Market drops can be scary, but panic closing can lock in losses unnecessarily.

**Better approach:**
- Have a plan before entering the trade
- Set criteria for when you'll close (e.g., -50% of credit received)
- Remember that volatility creates opportunities

#### Credit Spreads Explained [57:00 - 59:00]

**Regular put selling:**
- Sell one option at $200
- Max collateral: $20,000

**Credit spread:**
- Sell one option at $200
- Buy another option below it at $195
- If it drops below $195, you sell your 100 shares at $195
- **Max collateral needed: only $500** (instead of $20,000!)

- 35 days to expiration
- Collect: $165
- Max loss: $500 - $165 = $335
- If it expires above $200: keep the $165

## Why Delta Isn't Always Reliable [59:00 - 63:30]

### Understanding Delta

**Delta** is an indicator of probability of profit:
- 15 delta = 85% chance of winning
- 30 delta = 70% chance of winning
- Similar to the POP strategy discussed earlier

### The Tasty Trade Study

Using SPY data from 2005 to present (article from 2019-2021), trading 45 DTE at different deltas:

**Predicted vs. Actual Results (Put Side):**
- 15 delta: Should be 15% ITM, actually only **8%** ITM
- 30 delta: Should be 30% ITM, actually only **20%** ITM
- 40 delta: Should be 40% ITM, actually only **26%** ITM
- 50 delta: Should be 50% ITM, actually only **37%** ITM

### Why This Happens

The stock market has grown significantly over the last 20 years. The market is more often in an uptrend than a downtrend.

**What this means:**
- Trading with delta is actually LESS risky than predicted
- If selling 15 delta (85% POP), chances of winning are actually much higher
- We're leaving money on the table by being too conservative

**This is why I tend to be aggressive when selling puts** - the market is generally less risky than what delta or the Greeks predict.

**Caveat:** If using delta works for you and gives you confidence, continue doing so. Tinker with deltas until you find a strategy that works for you.

## Rolling Options [70:00 - 75:00]

### What is Rolling?

**Rolling** means extending the duration of your option. If you have 7 days left on your original 30 DTE option and want to extend it, you roll to a later date (e.g., another 45 days).

### How to Roll on Fidelity [70:30 - 72:30]

**Example: Nvidia $130 put with 22 days left**

**Step 1:** Click "Roll"
- Buy back current option (spend money to close)
- Example: spend $108 to close July 3rd $130 put

**Step 2:** Sell new option at later date
- Example: July 11th (one week later)
- Choose strike price

**Three Rolling Options:**

1. **Rolling Out (same strike):**
   - Keep $130 strike
   - Collect $155 on new option
   - Net credit: $47 ($155 - $108)

2. **Rolling Down (lower strike):**
   - Change to $125 strike (further OTM)
   - Collect $94
   - Net debit: -$14 (you PAY to roll down)

3. **Rolling Up (higher strike):**
   - Change to $135 strike (closer to money)
   - Collect $258
   - Net credit: $150

**Rolling further out in time:**
- Roll $130 to $135 two weeks out
- Collect $212 net credit

### The Number One Rule: Never Roll for a Debit [73:00 - 73:30]

**NEVER spend money to roll.** Always collect credit when rolling.

In the example where we rolled from $130 to $125 (rolling down), we'd spend $14. That's not okay in our system. We always want to be collecting money.

**Better rolls:**
- Roll at same strike ($130) and collect $98
- Roll up to $135 and collect $212

### When to Roll [73:30 - 75:00]

**Purpose of rolling:** Maximize the amount of credit collected.

**Easy method:** Look at time remaining vs. value remaining.

**Example with 30 DTE option (collected $200):**

**At 15 days remaining:**
- If $150+ of value remains: DON'T roll (collect the remaining $150 in 15 days)
- If only $50 remains: DO roll (you've already profited 75%, time for new trade)

**At 7 days remaining:**
- Typically 10% of value remains (90% decayed)
- Time to roll and collect more money

**Real example from Fidelity:**
- Rolling July 3rd put to July 18th at $135 strike (rolling up)
- Collect $212 = **1.5% for one week**
- Rolling to same strike ($130) for one week = $100 (75% return for one week)

These are excellent returns. If making 1.5% per week, that's already better than the 1.5% monthly target.

## Personal Learnings and Advice [82:30 - 82:00 (end)]

### Discipline and Consistency is Key [82:30 - 86:00]

Following the rules we laid out is critical:
- 30-45 DTE
- Sell on high-quality stocks
- Don't sell puts on stocks you can't afford

**These rules came from prior experiences, mistakes, and large losses.**

Following these rules can be very difficult. The mental and psychological part of trading is the most important part.

**You WILL face this situation:**
- Your first option goes in the money
- You don't want to get assigned (don't want to spend thousands buying 100 shares)
- You'll feel the urge to close the option

**You must fight that urge.** Build the emotional and psychological tolerance to follow these rules.

**For beginners:** Consider starting with paper trading to understand the mechanics and emotional aspects without real money risk.

#### Finding Your Comfort Level

**Too conservative?**
- You may think my 2% monthly returns are too risky
- You want to sell further OTM
- Getting 1%, 1.25%, even 0.75% is FINE
- This allows you to follow the rules consistently
- In the long run, you're better off

**Too aggressive?**
- You want money fast
- Selling on risky stocks for 5-6% per week
- Being extremely greedy
- Taking on risk you're not aware of
- When things go bad, you could ruin your account

**Control these emotional impulses.** If you need to tinker with the rules and strategies to follow them consistently, do it.

**Stick to the system.** The rules work because they account for different market conditions.
