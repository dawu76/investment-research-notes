### I. Executive Summary
Rory Johnston provides a data-driven look at the physical oil market in the wake of the Strait of Hormuz conflict. He explains that WTI's collapse to $67.26/bbl and the emergence of prompt contango in Brent are caused by a temporary "jailbreak" surge in Middle East outflows. This surge is driven by drawing down stranded floating storage (drawn at a rate of 4 million barrels/day) rather than fresh upstream production, which is lagging exit flows. Geopolitically, the truce remains highly unstable as the U.S. and Iran pursue mutually exclusive shipping control terms. Johnston highlights that the feared $200/bbl oil spike was averted solely because Beijing made a discretionary policy decision to cut China's imports by 5 million barrels/day. In the postgame segment, Patrick Ceresna and Massil Beguenane introduce the "MacroVoices Trading Desk" using data from `cotsignal.com`, selecting a bull call spread on Valero Energy (VLO) to play elevated crack spreads.

### III. Detailed Key Takeaways

#### 1. The Mechanics of the Hormuz Shipping 'Jailbreak'
Rory Johnston dissects the physical tanker data to explain why crude flat prices have collapsed.
*   **Surging Exit Flows:** On multiple days in late June, exit flows through the Strait of Hormuz alone exceeded 20 million barrels/day (pre-war baseline). Combined with maxed-out Red Sea reroutes (Yanbu pipeline, Fujairah bypass), Middle East exports temporarily hit 130% of pre-war volumes.
*   **The Exits-to-Loadings Mismatch:** This surge is not supported by new crude production. Exits are running at ~12 million barrels/day, but fresh loadings are only 5-6 million barrels/day. The remaining 6 million barrels/day is being drawn from stranded floating storage in the Gulf.
*   **The Upstream Bottleneck:** Floating storage drawdowns (running at over 4 million barrels/day) are only sustainable for another week or two. Once exhausted, exit flows must decline to match fresh loadings. Inbound empty "ballast" tankers (VLCCs) have returned rapidly at ~10 million barrels/day, meaning the bottleneck is not shipping capacity, but the slow recovery of upstream production.

> "We have a jailbreak going on... flows out of Hormuz alone exceed 20 million barrels [some days]... but fresh loadings have materially lagged the pace of exits... we've only seen loadings of around 5 to 6 million barrels a day."
> — *Rory Johnston*

#### 2. Mutually Exclusive Claims of Shipping Control
The U.S. and Iran have signed a ceasefire MOU, but they hold incompatible interpretations of shipping corridor management.
*   **The Iran Toll Booth:** Iran believes the MOU grants them permanent regulatory control to charge tolls ($1/barrel). The U.S. operates under the assumption that Iran has zero control.
*   **Flashes of Kinetic Heat:** When international shipping challenged Iran's authority by navigating the Omani southern channel, Iran targeted a freighter with a drone. The U.S. retaliated by bombing Iranian coastal missile and radar sites. Following another round of drone strikes and U.S. airstrikes, an Axios-brokered Sunday night ceasefire was announced to calm markets.
*   **IMO Suspension:** The International Maritime Organization (IMO) attempted to coordinate an evacuation plan through the southern channel, but suspended the operation after tankers were targeted.

> "The situation... in Hormuz is unstable because both sides are pursuing something that is mutually exclusive... both United States and Iran can't get their way with the normalization of flows."
> — *Rory Johnston*

#### 3. Why the $200 Oil Shock Was Averted
At the start of the conflict, consensus held that a Strait closure lasting until June would spark a massive global crude deficit and $200/bbl oil. Johnston explains how this was prevented:
*   **IEA SPR Releases:** Western strategic reserves released 3.5 to 4 million barrels/day.
*   **China's Strategic Abstention:** Beijing made a discretionary policy decision to cut China's crude imports by **5 million barrels/day** compared to the pre-war (Dec-Feb) average. Beijing drew down domestic strategic stockpiles and intermediate inventories, experiencing only a 30% domestic fuel price rise while global prices doubled. 
*   **Relieving Competition:** By stepping away from the market, China allowed other hard-hit Asian importers (South Korea, India, Australia) to import normal volumes without bidding up prices.

> "The delta in crude imports we saw between the December to February average... to what they imported through June was a 5 million barrel a day delta... discretionary choice by Beijing... China has not stepped in as a competitor."
> — *Rory Johnston*

#### 4. The Split Petroleum Market and the Refining Bottleneck
Johnston highlights a stark divergence between crude oil and refined products:
*   **Prompt Contango in Crude:** WTI fell to $67.26/bbl and prompt spreads for Brent/Dubai entered contango, indicating a temporary spot surplus of crude as tankers reach refineries simultaneously.
*   **Seasonal High Crack spreads:** Conversely, refined product markets are extremely tight. Diesel crack spreads are trading near seasonal all-time highs of $60/bbl, and gasoline is at $50/bbl (diesel crack spreads are nearly equal to WTI's flat price).
*   **Ukrainian drone Strikes:** Ukrainian attacks on Russian refining infrastructure have reduced Russian product exports to post-COVID lows, forcing Russia to export raw crude at record highs. Since refineries are the sole consumers of crude, damaged refining capacity globally (Middle East and Russia) has reduced crude demand, depressing raw crude prices while driving product prices higher.

> "Crude oil is currently the weakest part of the petroleum market... Diesel crack spreads... are almost as large as the flat price of WTI... we keep losing [refineries] like flies in Russia."
> — *Rory Johnston*

#### 5. Speculative Positioning and Squeeze Risk
Speculators have built near-record short positions in crude, matching the levels seen in December 2025. WTI is resting on its 200-day moving average support of $70/bbl. Speculator length is completely washed out, providing a strong floor. Normalizing spec positions alone could drive crude $6 to $10/bbl higher, while a surprise geopolitical catalyst could trigger a short squeeze of $15 to $20/bbl.
